## 7. Digital, Satellites & Data

Your new handheld can do something beginners a generation ago only read about: it can reach a repeater in the sky. The same radio that brings up the local machine can catch a satellite crossing overhead, beacon your position onto a public map, and — with a computer or a phone standing in for the radio — put you on a repeater on another continent. This is the tour of that corner of the hobby, and it carries the second half of pool subelement T8: 35 questions across groups T8B, T8C, and T8D, 3 of them on your 35-question exam. In this chapter you'll learn what counts as a digital mode; how FT8 decodes signals you cannot hear; how APRS puts stations on a map; how DMR and mesh networks move data; how internet linking welds repeaters together; how to book a ten-minute appointment with a satellite or the space station; and how contests, fox hunts, and grid locators turn operating into a game.

### The digital zoo: what counts as digital

Chapter 2 taught the mode families — AM, FM, SSB, and CW — and how wide each one's footprint on the dial is. This chapter is about what hams actually do with those modes once a computer joins the station. "Digital" is the umbrella for every mode where information travels as data that a computer generates or decodes, instead of as analog voice straight from a microphone. The keyboard does the talking; the radio is the pipe.

The exam's inventory question deserves a slow read: digital modes include packet radio, IEEE 802.11, and FT8 — and the keyed answer is all of them (T8D01). Packet radio is data in addressed bursts, which you will meet properly in a moment. FT8 is the weak-signal sensation you will meet next. And IEEE 802.11 is the technical name for ordinary Wi-Fi — yes, Wi-Fi counts as an amateur digital mode when it runs under amateur rules on amateur frequencies, as the mesh section below shows. The exam loves the "Wi-Fi isn't radio" trap; it is radio, and hams bend it onto their own bands.

Three more vocabulary entries round out the zoo, and each is a straight definition the pool wants verbatim. **CW** is simply another name for a Morse code transmission (T8D09) — a carrier keyed on and off in dits and dahs, the oldest digital mode of all, living mostly on HF. **PSK** stands for Phase Shift Keying (T8D06), a family of keyboard-to-keyboard chat modes that encode text in tiny shifts of the wave's phase; memorize "Phase," because every distractor is a plausible P-word like "Pulse Shift Keying." **NTSC** is the analog fast-scan color television signal standard (T8D04) — the old broadcast TV system and the ancestor of amateur fast-scan TV, whose enormous footprint from chapter 2's bandwidth ladder is why that activity lives on the UHF bands and up.

One exam-strategy note belongs here, because this subelement leans on the pattern hard. "All these choices are correct" is the keyed answer on six of your 35 questions (T8B03, T8B04, T8D01, T8D03, T8D08, T8D10) — but it is also a deliberate trap on three others (T8B01, T8B06, T8C02). Never pick it on habit. Read each option on its own merits; only when every option is genuinely true does "all these choices are correct" become the answer.

### FT8 and WSJT-X: weak signals on a clock

The mode that redrew the hobby's map is **FT8**, a digital mode designed for very low signal-to-noise operation — contacts below audibility, exchanged in timed 15-second sequences (T8D02). Read that definition twice, because it is the whole exam answer. "Below audibility" is not a metaphor: FT8's decoder pulls complete exchanges out of noise in which your ear hears nothing at all. Stations that could never hear each other's voices trade reports through FT8 every single day.

The trick is a strict shared clock. Every FT8 station transmits and receives in alternating 15-second slots — you transmit for 15 seconds while the other station listens, then you swap — and all the world's computers stay in lock-step because each one is synced to internet time. The practical consequence lands on you: your PC clock must be accurate to about a second, or your software decodes nothing. Set your computer to sync its clock automatically and forget it. The on-screen view is a waterfall, a scrolling frequency-versus-time display where dozens of conversations appear as parallel traces, and an FT8 contact itself is minimal and scripted: call signs, signal reports, and 73 (best regards), over in about a minute. The software can even sequence the standard replies for you, so a quiet evening of FT8 can run with one hand on the mouse.

**FACT:** FT8 is a digital mode designed for very low signal-to-noise operation — contacts below audibility, exchanged in timed 15-second sequences.

FT8's home is **WSJT-X**, a free software suite, and the exam asks what else it supports: Earth-Moon-Earth, weak-signal propagation beacons, and meteor scatter — all of them (T8D10). Earth-Moon-Earth, always abbreviated EME, is exactly what it sounds like: bouncing your signal off the Moon and hearing it come back. Meteor scatter rides the brief ionized trails of burning meteors. Weak-signal propagation beacons let you watch a band open and close in real time. All of it is one idea repeated — weak-signal modes leaning on precise timing and patient decoding. As a Technician you can try FT8 where your data privileges live; chapter 8 pins down those band privileges, while this chapter's job is knowing what the mode is.

### APRS and packet radio

**APRS** is the mode you can demo to a friend in thirty seconds: open a map website, and there are ham stations moving around on it in real time. The exam pins both what it carries and what it is for. APRS carries GPS position, short text messages, and weather data — all three (T8D03). Its signature use is providing real-time tactical digital communications with stations plotted on a map (T8D05): picture a public-service bike ride where the event coordinator sees every rest stop, every roving support vehicle, and every first-aid team as a labeled dot on one map. In North America the whole system shares a single channel, 144.390 MHz — a frequency worth memorizing, and not only for the exam.

The mechanics fit in one breath. Your tracker — a radio with a GPS attached, or a handheld with both built in — beacons a tiny packet on the national channel. Hilltop **digipeaters** (digital repeaters) hear it and relay it onward. An **iGate**, a receiver bridged to the internet, picks the packet up and pushes it online, which is how your dot appears on map sites like aprs.fi for anyone to watch. Because every station in the region shares that one channel, the community custom is to beacon sensibly — a car parked for the weekend does not need to announce itself constantly. Beyond position dots, club weather stations beacon temperature, rain, and wind, and balloon-chase crews track high-altitude balloons by APRS; both make easy "look at the map right now" demos at a club meeting.

{{fig:ch07-aprs-path}}

**FACT:** APRS carries GPS position, short text messages, and weather data, and its signature use is real-time tactical digital communications with stations plotted on a map; in North America it lives on 144.390 MHz.

Underneath APRS sits plain **packet radio**, and the exam wants its anatomy. Every packet radio transmission includes a header that contains the call sign of the station the information is being sent to, a checksum for error detection, and automatic repeat request in case of error — all three (T8D08). The header is the address label, the To line on the envelope. The checksum is arithmetic the receiver re-runs on the arriving data: if the numbers do not match, the packet arrived damaged. And **ARQ** is what happens next — the receiving station detects the errors and sends a request for retransmission (T8D11). Errors get fixed by asking for another copy, not by encryption or compression; those are the distractors. The hardware box that builds and decodes the packets is called a **TNC** (terminal node controller), a modem for radio, and chapter 2 already told you the carrier it feeds: VHF packet radio rides on FM (T8A02), which is why an ordinary 2-meter rig can play.

### DMR and mesh

**DMR** — Digital Mobile Radio — came to ham radio from the commercial world, and the exam wants its defining trick: DMR time-multiplexes two digital voice signals on a single 12.5 kHz repeater channel (T8D07). Time-multiplex means the channel deals the two conversations alternating slices of time — two **time slots** — interleaved so quickly that each listener hears one continuous conversation. One repeater channel, two simultaneous talks, double the capacity of the analog machine it replaces. The rest of DMR's vocabulary was chapter 6's territory, so here is the one-line refresher: a color code is the access code programmed into your radio to reach a specific DMR repeater, and a talkgroup is the identifier that organizes traffic so you hear only your group. DMR's exam home in this chapter is the two-slots fact; the access vocabulary stays with subelement T2.

**FACT:** DMR (Digital Mobile Radio) time-multiplexes two digital voice signals on a single 12.5 kHz repeater channel (two "time slots").

A ham **mesh network** answers a different question: what if the data network itself is the goal? An amateur mesh network is built from commercial Wi-Fi equipment with modified firmware, operating on amateur frequencies (T8D12) — off-the-shelf routers, re-flashed to run inside amateur allocations under Part 97 and linked rooftop to rooftop into a web where every node relays traffic for the others. Emergency-communications groups deploy mesh networks (the AREDN project is the name you will hear) to move images, forms, and messages across a town when the commercial internet is down. It is T8D01's Wi-Fi answer made real: same 802.11 technology, amateur rules, amateur frequencies.

### Internet-linked radio

Take a repeater and give it an internet connection, and you get one of the friendliest tools in the hobby. The underlying technology is **VoIP** — Voice over Internet Protocol — voice delivered over the internet using digital techniques (T8C07). Your voice becomes data packets for the internet leg of the trip and turns back into radio at the far end. Two systems dominate, and the exam tests the difference between them.

**IRLP** — the Internet Radio Linking Project — is a technique to connect amateur radio systems, such as repeaters, via the internet (T8C08). You drive it from your radio: punch the node's access code on your keypad and the local repeater links to a distant one, so your five-watt handheld comes out of a mountaintop in another state or another country. The keypad language is DTMF — over-the-air access to a node is by DTMF codes from your radio (T8C06), the same two-tones-per-keypress signaling chapter 6 introduced for repeater features.

**EchoLink** answers a different need: it lets you operate through a repeater without a radio — from a computer or phone app (T8C09). Stuck in an apartment with no antenna, or traveling without your handheld? The app on your phone or laptop connects through the internet to a **gateway** — an amateur station that connects other amateur stations to the internet (T8C11) — and that station puts your voice on the air with you as the control operator. Which is exactly why one gate stands at the door, and it is an exam question: before using EchoLink you must register your call sign and provide proof of license (T8C10). The validation step is what keeps the internet end of an on-air system limited to licensed operators. Keep the exam's contrast straight: IRLP is radio-to-radio through the internet, commanded over the air by DTMF codes; EchoLink also welcomes the no-radio app user. The distractors on T8C09 — IRLP, D-STAR, DMR — are all radio-based systems.

{{fig:ch07-echolink-topology}}

Linked operation has its own manners, learned the first time you try it. Announce that you are on the link, so both ends know the conversation spans systems. Leave extra pause time between transmissions, because the internet hop plus two repeater squelches adds delay, and the pause lets anyone break in. And send the disconnect code when you are done, so the link frees both repeaters for the next user.

### Satellites: a repeater in the sky

Here is the chapter's worst-kept secret: your Technician license is already a satellite license. An amateur FM satellite is a cross-band repeater in the sky — it hears you on one band and repeats you on another, exactly like the machines of chapter 6, except this machine passes overhead a few times a day. Working one is a ten-minute appointment with a moving target, and it is some of the most fun a handheld can deliver.

{{fig:ch07-satellite-pass}}

Start with listening, because listening needs no appointment and no license at all. A satellite **beacon** is a transmission from the satellite carrying status information — **telemetry** about the health and status of the satellite, its temperatures, and its systems (T8B01, T8B05). Anyone, licensed or not, may receive satellite telemetry (T8B11), which makes decoding a beacon the perfect zero-pressure first satellite project: aim an antenna at the pass, listen, decode.

When you want to know when to listen, a tracking program does the astronomy for you. Its input is the **Keplerian elements** (T8B06) — the "keps," a small set of orbit-description numbers refreshed regularly over the internet. Its outputs are everything an operator needs: maps showing the satellite's track over the Earth, the time and azimuth and elevation of each pass's start, peak, and end, and the apparent frequency of the satellite including Doppler shift — the keyed answer is all of these (T8B03). A **pass** runs from **AOS** (acquisition of signal, the satellite rising over your horizon) through its highest point to **LOS** (loss of signal, the satellite setting again) — a window of roughly ten minutes. That window repeats so often because **LEO** means Low Earth Orbit, which has a period of around 100 minutes (T8B10): a full lap in about an hour and forty minutes, so the same bird returns several times a day.

**FACT:** LEO means Low Earth Orbit, which has a period of around 100 minutes.

Now the part that surprises every beginner: the satellite's frequency will not sit still. **Doppler shift** is the observed change in signal frequency caused by relative motion between the satellite and the Earth station (T8B07) — the same physics as a passing siren dropping in pitch, except here the pitch is radio frequency. As the satellite rises and approaches, its downlink arrives high; it slides through the published frequency at closest approach; it ends low as the satellite recedes toward the far horizon. Doppler scales with frequency, so on the 70-centimeter downlink the slide is roughly 10 kHz over a pass, while on the 2-meter uplink it is small enough to ignore — which is why operators chase the downlink with their tuning knob and leave the transmitter fixed.

{{fig:ch07-doppler-curve}}

> **Worked example:** SO-50's downlink is 436.795 MHz, and Doppler shifts it about 10 kHz over a pass. Program three memory channels and step through them as the bird flies. At AOS, with the satellite rising and coming toward you, listen 10 kHz high: 436.795 + 0.010 = **436.805 MHz**. At mid-pass, listen right on **436.795 MHz**. At LOS, with the satellite moving away, listen 10 kHz low: 436.795 − 0.010 = **436.785 MHz**. Your uplink stays fixed at 145.850 MHz for the whole pass, because Doppler at 2 meters is only a small fraction of the 70-centimeter slide. Three memories, one up-arrow button, ten minutes — that is the entire Doppler trick.

Mode designators tell you which bands a satellite uses, and the letters read in a fixed order: uplink first, downlink second. A satellite operating in **U/V mode** has its uplink in the 70-centimeter band and its downlink in the 2-meter band (T8B08) — U for the UHF uplink, V for the VHF downlink. The exam's distractors invent "ultraviolet" frequencies and a 15-meter/10-meter pair; the letters are band codes, and the first letter is always the one you transmit up. Satellites commonly use FM, SSB, and CW/data modes — all of them (T8B04). FM birds like SO-50 relay one channel at a time, while **linear transponders** relay a whole slice of spectrum, carrying several SSB or CW conversations side by side.

**FACT:** A satellite operating in U/V mode has its uplink in the 70-centimeter band and its downlink in the 2-meter band.

Two behaviors of the bird itself round out the group. **Spin fading** is the rhythmic rise and fall of a satellite's signal strength caused by the rotation of the satellite and its antennas (T8B09): the antennas sweep past you as the satellite turns gently, and the signal swells and dips with the rotation. It is normal, and the discipline is not to "fix" it with more power. Which brings up the power rules the exam tests twice. Excessive uplink power can block access by other users of a satellite (T8B02) — on a single-channel FM bird, the loudest uplink captures the repeater, chapter 2's capture effect all over again, this time in orbit. And on a linear transponder, the right level is when your downlink signal is about as strong as the satellite's beacon (T8B12). Louder than the beacon means you are hogging the transponder's shared power budget; match the beacon and everyone gets a turn.

Two beginner-sized targets deserve their own boxed numbers, both pinned as of this writing. **SO-50** is the classic first FM satellite: uplink 145.850 MHz FM, downlink 436.795 MHz. It sleeps between uses, so you wake it by arming a ten-minute timer with a 2-second carrier using a 74.4 Hz tone, then operate with a 67.0 Hz tone — program both tones as separate memories before your first pass. The **ISS** is the other target, and your Technician license is enough: any US amateur with a Technician class or higher license may contact the ISS (the rules live in §97.207(a) and §97.209(a), and chapter 8 unpacks them with pool question T1B02). Its voice downlink is 145.800 MHz worldwide, and its VHF packet frequency is 145.825 MHz, uplink and downlink. When the crew runs slow-scan TV events, the current SSTV downlink is 437.550 MHz in Robot36 mode — verified against ariss.org in July 2026 — not the 145.800 that older guides print; ISS frequencies and modes change over the years, so check ariss.org close to any event. Successful contacts have been made with simple vertical and ground-plane antennas; the skill is timing the pass, not the size of the station.

Operators who work the FM birds compress success into three habits: open your squelch so weak signals get through, use the best antenna you can manage, and tune for the Doppler effect. On top of those sits one iron rule: if you cannot hear the satellite, do not transmit — a station that cannot hear the bird only jams the uplink for everyone else. Exchanges on a busy pass are fast and minimal, contest-style: your call sign and your grid locator in phonetics, and on a quiet pass you can add your name and state. A radio that can listen on the downlink while you transmit (full duplex) lets you hear your own signal coming back from the bird — the surest confirmation you are getting in.

### Operating activities: contests, fox hunts, grid locators

The rest of T8C is radio as a sport, starting with **contesting**: contacting as many stations as possible in a specified period (T8C03). A contest runs on a clock — usually a weekend — and every contact scores for working someone new. The good practice the exam expects follows straight from the clock: send only the minimum information needed for identification and the contest exchange (T8C04). The exchange is the contest's prescribed payload, a signal report plus whatever the rules ask for, and brevity is politeness: hundreds of stations are waiting behind you.

The piece of geography that contests trade is the **grid locator**: a letter-number designator assigned to a geographic location (T8C05), from the Maidenhead system that carves the world into coded rectangles — "FN31" is one four-character example. Grid locators are the currency of VHF contests and of satellite passes, where a call sign plus a grid square can be the whole conversation. The exam's distractor wants you to think a grid locator is an azimuth-and-elevation pair; it is a location code, not an antenna heading.

**Radio direction finding (RDF)** is the sport of finding transmitters, and it has a serious side: RDF techniques are used to locate sources of noise, interference, or jamming (T8C01). The key tool is a directional antenna (T8C02) — an antenna that hears best in one direction, so sweeping it around tells you where the signal lives. Clubs turn that skill into a Saturday game: a hidden-transmitter hunt, universally called a **fox hunt**, where someone hides a small beacon and everyone else unrolls handhelds and tape-measure Yagis to find it. The same sweep-and-triangle method tracks down a noisy power line or a jammer on the repeater — which is exactly why the trap answer on T8C02 is "all these choices are correct": the question asks which item is useful, and only the directional antenna is.

Three on-ramps are worth knowing as activities, not exam material. The Rookie Roundup is a contest built for you — "Rookies" licensed within the last three years are the stations everyone else may work. ARRL Kids Day, each January and June, puts kids on the air alongside a licensed adult for low-stakes first contacts. And Field Day, the fourth full weekend of June, is the hobby's national campout: clubs operate off-grid all weekend on every mode — satellite contacts included — and visitors are welcome. All three are places a brand-new Technician can log real contacts on day one.

### Exam Focus

Subelement T8's second half — groups T8B, T8C, and T8D — holds 35 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 3 of them, one per group. Here are seven, quoted exactly as the pool prints them, spanning all three groups; T8B07 pairs with the Doppler-curve figure, T8B08 and T8B10 with the satellite-pass figure, T8C08 with the EchoLink figure, T8D02 with the FT8 timeline, and T8D05 with the APRS path above.

> **T8B07** What is Doppler shift in reference to satellite communications?
> A. A change in the satellite orbit
> B. A mode where the satellite receives signals on one band and transmits on another
> C. An observed change in signal frequency caused by relative motion between the satellite and Earth station
> D. A special digital communications mode for some satellites
> **Answer: C** — Doppler shift is the frequency change caused by relative motion — the passing-siren effect, and the reason you retune during a pass.

> **T8B08** What does it mean if a satellite is operating in U/V mode?
> A. The satellite uplink is in the 15-meter band and the downlink is in the 10-meter band
> B. The satellite uplink is in the 70-centimeter band and the downlink is in the 2-meter band
> C. The satellite operates using ultraviolet frequencies
> D. The satellite frequencies are usually variable
> **Answer: B** — the letters read uplink first, downlink second: up on 70 centimeters, down on 2 meters, and "ultraviolet" is pure invention.

> **T8B10** What does the term LEO mean in reference to communication satellites?
> A. Low Energy Orbit, which conserves battery power
> B. Low Elevation Orbit, which appears close to the horizon from the earth station
> C. Low Equilibrium Orbit, which has a slightly unstable period
> D. Low Earth Orbit, which has a period of around 100 minutes
> **Answer: D** — LEO is Low Earth Orbit, and "around 100 minutes" is the exam-facing period to memorize.

> **T8C08** What is the Internet Radio Linking Project (IRLP)?
> A. A technique to connect amateur radio systems, such as repeaters, via the internet
> B. A system for providing access to websites via amateur radio
> C. A system for informing amateurs in real time of the frequency of active DX stations
> D. A technique for measuring signal strength of an amateur transmitter via the internet
> **Answer: A** — IRLP links amateur radio systems like repeaters through the internet, and you reach a node over the air with DTMF codes.

> **T8D02** What is FT8?
> A. A wideband FM voice mode
> B. A digital mode capable of low signal-to-noise operation
> C. An eight-channel multiplex mode for FM repeaters
> D. A digital slow-scan TV mode with forward error correction and automatic color compensation
> **Answer: B** — FT8 is the weak-signal digital mode that completes contacts below audibility in timed 15-second sequences.

> **T8D05** Which of the following is an application of APRS?
> A. Providing real-time tactical digital communications in conjunction with a map showing the locations of stations
> B. Automatically showing the number of packets transmitted via PACTOR during a specific time interval
> C. Providing voice over internet connection between repeaters
> D. Providing information on the number of stations signed into a repeater
> **Answer: A** — APRS's signature use is real-time tactical digital communications with stations plotted on a map, on 144.390 MHz in North America.

> **T8D07** Which of the following describes DMR?
> A. A technique for time-multiplexing two digital voice signals on a single 12.5 kHz repeater channel
> B. An automatic position tracking mode for FM mobiles communicating through repeaters
> C. An automatic computer logging technique for hands-off logging when communicating while operating a vehicle
> D. A digital technique for transmitting on two repeater inputs simultaneously for automatic error correction
> **Answer: A** — DMR time-multiplexes two digital voice signals on one 12.5 kHz repeater channel — the two time slots.

### Key Takeaways

- Digital modes include packet radio, IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi), and FT8 — all of them; CW is simply another name for a Morse code transmission, PSK stands for Phase Shift Keying, and NTSC is the analog fast-scan color TV standard.

- FT8 is designed for very low signal-to-noise operation — contacts below audibility in timed 15-second sequences, so your PC clock must be accurate to about a second; its WSJT-X suite also supports Earth-Moon-Earth, weak-signal propagation beacons, and meteor scatter.

- APRS carries GPS position, texts, and weather data on 144.390 MHz in North America, flowing tracker to digipeater to iGate to map; every packet radio transmission carries a destination-call-sign header, a checksum, and ARQ, which requests retransmission when the receiver detects errors.

- DMR time-multiplexes two digital voice signals on a single 12.5 kHz repeater channel — two time slots — and an amateur mesh network is commercial Wi-Fi equipment with modified firmware operating on amateur frequencies.

- IRLP connects repeaters via the internet, commanded over the air by DTMF codes; EchoLink lets you operate through a repeater from a computer or phone app with no radio, but you must register your call sign and provide proof of license first; a gateway is the station that connects amateur stations to the internet.

- Satellites: anyone may receive telemetry; Keplerian elements drive tracking programs; Doppler shift slides the downlink from about 10 kHz high at AOS to low at LOS; U/V mode is uplink on 70 centimeters and downlink on 2 meters; LEO means Low Earth Orbit with a period of around 100 minutes; spin fading is normal — and the right uplink power makes your downlink about as strong as the beacon.

- Contesting means contacting as many stations as possible in a specified period, sending only the minimum information needed for identification and the exchange; a grid locator is a letter-number designator for a geographic location; and RDF with a directional antenna runs fox hunts and tracks down noise, interference, and jamming.
