## 2. How Signals Travel: Modes & Modulation

Picture your first week with a license and a handheld radio. You press the push-to-talk button, say your call sign, and a repeater twenty miles away hears you clearly. Somewhere between your mouth and that repeater, your voice became a radio wave — and the way it became one has a name: the mode. Flip through a ham magazine or a radio catalog and the modes are everywhere: FM for the local repeater net, SSB for the operator chasing Australia on a summer evening, CW for the faint beeping at the bottom of the band. In this chapter you'll learn what a carrier is and what modulation does to it, the three mode families every ham recognizes (AM, FM, and CW, plus AM's clever offspring SSB), which modes hams actually use for which jobs, the four bandwidth numbers the exam expects you to know, and the one weakness of FM that the exam also expects you to know. None of it requires math beyond a single division problem, and all of it will make the mode button on your first radio finally make sense.

### The radio spectrum: where hams live

Everything radio happens somewhere on the electromagnetic spectrum — the same vast range of energy that includes visible light, the warmth you feel from a campfire, and the X-rays at the dentist's office. What makes a radio wave a radio wave is simply its frequency: how many times per second the wave oscillates, measured in hertz (Hz). One kilohertz (kHz) is a thousand hertz, one megahertz (MHz) is a million, and one gigahertz (GHz) is a billion. Radio waves live at the low-frequency end of that range, and the *radio spectrum* is the part of it we use to communicate.

Nobody owns the whole dial. Governments divide the spectrum into *allocations* — frequency ranges set aside for particular services. FM broadcast stations get one allocation (that's why they all live between 88 and 108 MHz), television stations get others, and air traffic control, cell phones, weather satellites, and garage-door openers each get their own. The amateur radio service gets its allocations too, sprinkled generously from the shortwave region all the way up through microwave frequencies. A frequency range allocated to hams is called a *band*, and ham bands carry friendly names taken from their approximate wavelength: the "2-meter band" is the band whose waves are about two meters long, and the "70-centimeter band" works the same way. (Chapter 1 showed the shortcut behind the names — wavelength in meters is about 300 divided by the frequency in megahertz — and chapter 3 will drill it.) The names are approximations, not measurements to build antennas by, but they're how hams talk: "I was on two meters last night" tells another ham exactly where you were.

For this chapter you only need three region names, each defined by its frequency range:

- **HF** (high frequency): 3 to 30 MHz. These waves can bounce off the ionosphere — an electrically charged region of the upper atmosphere — and come back down hundreds or thousands of miles away. HF is the long-distance region, home to shortwave broadcasting and to hams talking across oceans; chapter 3 explains how the bouncing works.

- **VHF** (very high frequency): 30 to 300 MHz. Mostly local and regional communication, roughly line-of-sight — the repeater region. FM broadcast stations and your 2-meter band live here.

- **UHF** (ultra high frequency): 300 to 3,000 MHz. Even shorter-range in open country, but handy in and around buildings and cities. The 70-centimeter band lives here, along with familiar neighbors like cell phones and Wi-Fi routers. Beyond UHF lies the microwave region, where hams have allocations too — though those are adventures for later in your ham career.

Why all this dividing up? Because spectrum is a shared public resource, and two transmissions on the same frequency at the same time interfere with each other. Allocations keep a television station from landing on top of an air-traffic frequency — and inside the amateur allocations, they keep hams from landing on top of the neighbors. You'll see the interference idea again when we talk about bandwidth later in this chapter.

As a Technician you have privileges on a generous set of bands, and the figure below marks the ones that matter most to you: a slice of 10 meters down in HF, then 6 meters, 2 meters, and 1.25 meters in VHF, and 70 centimeters and 23 centimeters in UHF. Read it as a map, not as homework — the horizontal axis climbs in frequency from left to right, the three regions are shaded, and your bands are flagged so you can see at a glance how much territory a Technician license actually opens. Don't memorize the boundaries yet — chapter 3 and chapter 8 come back to them. For now, just keep the rough map in your head: HF for far away, VHF and UHF for nearby. And notice one more thing about how hams use a band: unlike broadcast stations, which each sit on one assigned channel, hams may tune anywhere inside their band allocations. That freedom is exactly why the width of a signal — the bandwidth you'll meet later in this chapter — matters so much in the amateur service.

### Carriers and the three mode families

A radio transmitter left completely alone produces a *carrier*: a steady, unvarying radio wave parked exactly on one frequency. A carrier by itself communicates nothing. It's the radio equivalent of a blank sheet of paper — perfectly good paper, but with nothing written on it. To make the carrier carry your voice, the transmitter changes some property of the wave in step with the sound: it writes on the paper. That process — combining speech or data with the carrier — is called *modulation*, and at the other end the receiver reverses it, peeling the voice back off the wave. Different ways of writing on the carrier give different modes, and the choice of mode shapes everything downstream: how the signal sounds, how far it reaches, how much spectrum it occupies, and what kind of radio you need to send and receive it.

There are three big families of modulation, and the exam pool cares about all three.

**AM — amplitude modulation.** The oldest family: the transmitter makes the carrier's *amplitude* — its strength — rise and fall in step with the voice waveform. Louder sound, stronger wave; softer sound, weaker wave. Picture a flashlight beam growing brighter and dimmer as you speak into it, and you have the idea. AM is what AM broadcast stations and shortwave broadcasters use, and it's what airliners use to talk with control towers, because even a weak, noisy AM signal stays understandable. Hams rarely use full AM for voice anymore — but they use its refined offspring constantly, as you'll see in a moment.

**FM — frequency modulation.** Here the carrier's strength stays constant, and instead its *frequency* wobbles slightly — a hair above the channel center for one part of the sound wave, a hair below for the next. The information rides on the wobble, not the strength. A close cousin is **PM — phase modulation**, which shifts the wave's timing (its phase) rather than its frequency. The two are so closely related in practice that the exam pool writes "FM or PM" as a single answer choice, and this book does the same. FM is the mode of FM broadcast radio and — far more important for you — of nearly all local ham voice communication on VHF and UHF.

**CW — continuous wave.** The simplest family of all, with no voice at all: the carrier is simply switched on and off in the short-and-long patterns of Morse code — dits and dahs. CW stands for "continuous wave," an old engineering name for a plain unmodulated carrier, and in everyday ham speech "CW" simply means Morse code sent by keying a carrier. The sending can be as humble as a hand key on a wooden base or as polished as electronic paddles that form the dits and dahs for you. It sounds primitive, and it is. That's its superpower, as the bandwidth section will show.

The figure below puts all three side by side: the same voice, carried three ways. In AM the outline of the wave's strength traces the shape of the sound. In FM the wave's strength never changes but its spacing squeezes and stretches with the sound. In CW there is no sound at all — just the carrier appearing and disappearing in the dit-dah pattern. If you ever feel lost in the acronyms, come back to this picture: one voice, three ways of writing it on a radio wave.

**SSB — single sideband.** Now for AM's clever offspring, because the very first question in this exam group asks about it. When a carrier is amplitude-modulated with speech, the resulting signal actually contains three pieces: the original carrier, still sitting on its own frequency, plus two mirror-image copies of the voice — one just above the carrier's frequency and one just below it. Those copies are called *sidebands*: an upper sideband and a lower sideband. And here is the key insight — each sideband, all by itself, contains the complete voice. The carrier adds nothing new, and the second sideband is a duplicate of the first. Single sideband (SSB) transmission simply throws away the carrier and one sideband and sends only the sideband that remains. Same voice, roughly half the spectrum space, and no transmitter power wasted on a carrier that carries no information. SSB is a form of amplitude modulation — that is exactly what exam question T8A01 asks, and it's why you'll sometimes hear SSB described as "AM with the wasted parts removed."

There's a small price for that efficiency, and you'll hear it the first time you tune across an SSB signal with the wrong receiver setting: because there's no carrier for an ordinary AM or FM receiver to lock onto, SSB comes out as unintelligible quacking until the radio is set to SSB and tuned carefully. An SSB-capable receiver rebuilds the missing carrier internally — that's the whole trick, and it's why your radio has a mode switch in the first place.

One piece of vocabulary before we move on: a transmitted signal of a given type is called an *emission*. When the pool asks which signal type has the narrowest bandwidth, it's comparing emissions — CW, SSB voice, FM voice, and the rest. And if all of this feels abstract, take heart: your radio handles the engineering. Choosing a mode on a real transceiver is literally a button press — the transmitter and receiver reconfigure themselves around your choice. What the exam (and this chapter) asks of you is to know what each choice means and when to reach for it.

### Who uses what (and why)

Modes are tools, and hams pick the tool that fits the job. Two jobs cover most of what a new Technician will do: talking locally with people in your own town or region, and reaching for distant stations on the edge of what's possible. Each job has a mode that suits it, and the reasons are practical, not traditional — audio quality, spectrum economy, and how the mode behaves when signals get weak.

**FM (or PM) is the everyday local mode.** Practically every VHF and UHF voice *repeater* runs on FM. A repeater is an unattended relay station, usually on a hilltop or a tall building, that listens on one frequency and retransmits whatever it hears on another, so that small handheld and mobile radios can talk across a whole region instead of just a few blocks. (Chapter 6 is all about repeater life; here you only need the mode.) FM is also the standard modulation for VHF *packet radio*, the long-running ham technique of sending data between computers in short, addressed bursts — in its heyday it carried keyboard-to-keyboard chats and mailbox networks, and it still moves messages and data links today. Why FM for these jobs? On the strong, steady signals of local communication it delivers clean, easy-to-understand audio; the radios are simple and inexpensive; and because an FM carrier keeps constant strength whether you're talking or silent, transmitters can run efficiently. For talking across town, FM is hard to beat.

**FACT:** FM (or PM) is the mode used for VHF/UHF voice repeaters and for VHF packet radio.

**SSB is the long-distance voice mode.** When hams stretch for weak, faraway stations — chasing *DX*, the ham shorthand for long-distance contacts — they reach for SSB. That holds on the HF bands, where SSB is the standard ham voice mode worldwide, and it holds on VHF and UHF too, where weak-signal enthusiasts point beam antennas at the horizon and use SSB to work stations hundreds of miles beyond normal repeater range. The reason is efficiency. SSB spends no power on a carrier and duplicates nothing, so every watt of transmitter power goes into the one sideband that carries the voice — exactly what you want when the signal arriving at the other end is barely above the noise. Listen to a weak-signal frequency for a few minutes and you'll hear the payoff: voices rising out of the static, fading back down, and getting copied anyway. (As a Technician, your HF voice privileges live on 10 meters; chapter 8 maps out privileges in detail.)

When two SSB operators meet on the air, their radios have to agree on which sideband to use — otherwise each station hears the other as unintelligible, Donald-Duck gibberish. An old ham convention settles the question in advance: on the 10-meter HF band and on the VHF and UHF bands, everyone uses the *upper sideband (USB)*. For orientation once you get on the air, it also helps to know that below 10 MHz the convention is the *lower sideband (LSB)* — but the exam only asks about the USB rule. A convention like this is community practice, not an FCC regulation; it exists because it makes everyone's radios compatible with everyone else's. Once you know the convention, using it is effortless — you set the mode switch to USB and never think about sidebands again.

**FACT:** The convention for single sideband on 10-meter HF and on the VHF/UHF bands is upper sideband (USB).

And CW? It's still everywhere — weak-signal work, contests, simple low-power radios built from kits — precisely because it's so narrow. A mode that needs almost no spectrum and almost no power never really goes out of style. Which brings us to the chapter's most exam-critical idea. (And if you're wondering about digital modes like FT8, satellites, or internet-linked radio — those all get their own treatment in chapter 7.)

### Bandwidth: the ladder to memorize

*Bandwidth* is how much spectrum a signal occupies — how wide a slice of the dial it takes up, measured in hertz, kilohertz, or megahertz. Picture the spectrum as a highway and each signal as a vehicle: a bicycle takes part of one lane, a car takes a full lane, and a wide-load truck takes several lanes and a pair of escort vehicles. Bandwidth matters because the highway is shared. The wider each signal, the fewer simultaneous conversations fit in a band — and since hams can tune anywhere in their allocations rather than sitting on assigned channels, signals that are wider than they need to be crowd out neighbors who could be using that space. Every signal also needs a little elbow room on each side so it doesn't overlap the next conversation over, so the effective cost of a wide mode is even higher than its raw number suggests.

The exam asks you to recall four bandwidth values. There's no formula and nothing to compute — these are simply measured properties of each mode, the way a car's length is a measured property of the car. The trick is not to memorize four loose numbers but one ordered ladder, from narrowest to widest:

1. **CW ≈ 150 Hz.** A keyed carrier needs almost no room at all — about one hundred fifty hertz, roughly a twentieth of an SSB signal. That makes CW the narrowest common emission, and it's why dozens of Morse conversations can fit comfortably in the space a handful of phone signals would fill — and why a CW signal can still be copied when conditions are miserable.

2. **SSB voice ≈ 3 kHz.** One sideband carrying speech takes about three kilohertz — half of what a full AM voice signal needs, as the figure below shows.

3. **FM voice on VHF repeaters ≈ 10 to 15 kHz.** FM's clean local audio costs spectrum, because the carrier's frequency has to swing back and forth with the sound and that swing needs room: one FM voice signal is as wide as several SSB signals.

4. **AM fast-scan TV ≈ 6 MHz.** *Fast-scan TV* is ordinary full-motion television — hams really do transmit live moving pictures — and video devours bandwidth because a moving picture carries so much more information per second than a voice. Six megahertz is wider than the entire 2-meter band. (Television's still-image cousin, slow-scan TV, sends one frame at a time and is correspondingly narrower.)

Say the ladder out loud a few times — *one-fifty hertz, three kilohertz, ten-to-fifteen kilohertz, six megahertz* — and notice how each step is a big jump, not a subtle one. That spacing is your friend on the exam: the four values are so far apart that if you can just keep their order straight, the right answer usually stands out immediately.

**FACT:** The bandwidth ladder to memorize is: CW ≈ 150 Hz (the narrowest common emission), SSB voice ≈ 3 kHz, FM voice on VHF repeaters ≈ 10–15 kHz, and AM fast-scan TV ≈ 6 MHz.

The figure below makes the second rung of the ladder intuitive. A full AM voice signal — carrier plus both sidebands — occupies about 6 kHz. Remove the carrier and one sideband to make SSB, and the very same voice fits in about 3 kHz: half the space, with no information lost. You can see it directly in the picture: the AM signal's wide footprint with its tall carrier spike in the middle, and next to it the SSB signal, a single compact hump. That economy is SSB's great advantage, and the exam states it as a direct comparison with FM: SSB signals have the narrower bandwidth.

**FACT:** SSB's advantage over FM is its narrower bandwidth.

> **Worked example:** How many SSB voice signals fit in the spectrum that one FM repeater signal occupies? Take the ladder's own numbers: FM voice ≈ 15 kHz wide, SSB voice ≈ 3 kHz wide. Divide: 15 ÷ 3 = **5**. Five SSB conversations fit where one FM conversation sits. That's why SSB is the weak-signal, long-distance mode — it sips spectrum, so more stations fit in the band, and less of your transmitter's energy goes to waste on anything but your voice.

That five-to-one ratio is worth pausing on. On a crowded band — a contest weekend, a big emergency drill, a sudden opening to faraway stations — the mode everyone chooses decides how many conversations the band can host at once. CW and SSB keep the doors open for more operators; FM and fast-scan TV trade that capacity away in exchange for easy local audio and live pictures. Neither choice is wrong; they just serve different jobs.

One caution about the exam itself: the pool loves to scramble these four numbers across questions — 15 kHz shows up as a wrong answer in the SSB question, and 150 kHz as a wrong answer in the FM question. Anchor each number to its rung on the ladder — CW, SSB, FM, TV, always in that order — and the scrambles stop being scary.

### FM's catch: the capture effect

FM has one tested weakness, and it has a name worth remembering: the *capture effect*. When two FM signals reach a receiver on the same channel, the receiver's circuitry locks onto the stronger one and suppresses the weaker one almost completely. You don't hear the weak signal faintly underneath the strong one — you effectively don't hear it at all. Only one signal can be received at a time, and it's always the strongest.

It's like listening to two people talk at once from across a noisy room: your ears track the louder voice, and the quieter person might as well be mouthing the words.

You'll meet the capture effect in real life the first time two stations key up on a repeater at once — hams call that *doubling*. Instead of a tangled mix of both voices, the repeater usually passes only the stronger signal; the weaker station may never know it wasn't heard. For local repeater life that's mostly a feature — it keeps a busy channel sounding clean instead of garbled, and it's one more reason FM owns the repeater world. But for weak-signal DX work it's a deal-breaker. On SSB or CW, a faint distant station can still be heard and copied while stronger signals share the speaker — the human ear is remarkably good at picking one voice out of a jumble, which is why contest operators can pull a single call sign out of a pileup of overlapping SSB voices. On FM, a weak signal sitting under a stronger one simply vanishes. That completes the "who uses what" puzzle: FM owns the strong-signal local world, and SSB and CW own the weak-signal long-distance world.

**FACT:** FM's disadvantage is the capture effect — only the strongest signal is received at a time.

Don't let the capture effect scare you off FM, though — for the kind of operating most new Technicians actually do, it works in your favor. Your repeater contacts will be strong-signal affairs, and the capture effect is part of what makes them sound crisp. Just file away the trade-off: when the day comes that you want to dig a whisper of a station out of the noise on 6 meters or work a distant grid on sideband, you'll reach for SSB or CW — and you'll know exactly why.

### Exam Focus

This chapter covers pool group **T8A** — 12 questions, from which your 35-question exam draws exactly 1. Every question in the group is answerable from what you just read: the mode families and who uses them, the USB convention, the bandwidth ladder, and the capture effect. Here are eight of the twelve, quoted exactly as they appear in the pool, each with the correct answer and a one-line reason. Read the question, cover the answer line, and see whether you can pick it before peeking — that's the same mental motion you'll use on exam day.

> **T8A01** Which of the following is a form of amplitude modulation?
> A. Spread spectrum
> B. Packet radio
> C. Single sideband
> D. Phase shift keying (PSK)
> **Answer: C** — SSB is AM with the carrier and one sideband removed, so it belongs to the AM family.

> **T8A03** Which type of voice mode is often used for long-distance (weak signal) contacts on the VHF and UHF bands?
> A. FM
> B. DRM
> C. SSB
> D. PM
> **Answer: C** — SSB is the weak-signal, long-distance voice mode, on VHF/UHF just as on HF.

> **T8A04** Which type of modulation is commonly used for VHF and UHF voice repeaters?
> A. AM
> B. SSB
> C. PSK
> D. FM or PM
> **Answer: D** — repeaters run FM (or its cousin PM), the everyday local mode.

> **T8A06** Which sideband is normally used for 10-meter HF, VHF, and UHF single-sideband communications?
> A. Upper sideband
> B. Lower sideband
> C. Suppressed sideband
> D. Inverted sideband
> **Answer: A** — the convention is upper sideband (USB) on 10 meters and on VHF/UHF.

> **T8A07** What is one characteristic of single sideband (SSB) compared to FM?
> A. SSB signals are easier to tune in correctly
> B. SSB signals are less susceptible to interference
> C. SSB signals have narrower bandwidth
> D. SSB signals are less susceptible to high SWR
> **Answer: C** — SSB's tested advantage is its narrower bandwidth, about 3 kHz against FM's 10 to 15 kHz.

> **T8A08** What is the approximate bandwidth of a typical single sideband (SSB) voice signal?
> A. 1 kHz
> B. 3 kHz
> C. 6 kHz
> D. 15 kHz
> **Answer: B** — a ladder value: SSB voice ≈ 3 kHz.

> **T8A11** What is the approximate bandwidth required to transmit a CW signal?
> A. 2.4 kHz
> B. 150 Hz
> C. 1000 Hz
> D. 15 kHz
> **Answer: B** — CW is the narrowest common emission, at about 150 Hz.

> **T8A12** Which of the following is a disadvantage of FM compared with single sideband?
> A. Voice quality is poorer
> B. Only one signal can be received at a time
> C. FM signals are harder to tune
> D. FM signals are more susceptible to high SWR
> **Answer: B** — the capture effect: an FM receiver passes only the strongest signal on the channel.

The four questions not quoted here — T8A02, T8A05, T8A09, and T8A10 — come straight from the same material: VHF packet radio uses FM or PM, CW is the narrowest common emission, FM voice takes 10 to 15 kHz, and AM fast-scan TV takes about 6 MHz. If you can recite the bandwidth ladder and the three who-uses-what pairings, you own this whole group. Two acronyms that appear only as distractors above are just names to recognize, not modes to learn here: PSK (phase shift keying) and DRM (a digital broadcast mode) both belong to the digital world covered in chapter 7.

### Key Takeaways

- Modulation combines speech or data with a steady carrier; the three mode families are AM (varies the carrier's amplitude), FM/PM (varies its frequency or phase), and CW (keys the carrier on and off in Morse code).

- SSB is a form of AM — the carrier and one sideband are removed, sending the same voice in about half the spectrum.

- FM (or PM) is the mode of VHF/UHF voice repeaters and VHF packet radio; SSB is the long-distance, weak-signal voice mode on VHF/UHF and on HF.

- The sideband convention on 10-meter HF and on the VHF/UHF bands is upper sideband (USB).

- The bandwidth ladder, narrowest to widest: CW ≈ 150 Hz (the narrowest common emission), SSB voice ≈ 3 kHz, FM voice ≈ 10–15 kHz, and AM fast-scan TV ≈ 6 MHz.

- SSB's advantage over FM is its narrower bandwidth; FM's disadvantage is the capture effect — only the strongest signal is received at a time.
