## 3. Propagation: Where Your Signal Goes

You press the push-to-talk button on your new handheld and your friend across town hears you on 2 meters, loud and clear. But the repeater on the ridge twenty miles away — a machine built to hear weak signals — never answers. That same week, at a club night, an HF radio pulls in a voice from a thousand miles away like it's coming from the next room. Same hobby, same speed of light, completely different results. The difference is propagation: everything that happens to your signal between your antenna and the other station's. In this chapter you'll learn what a radio wave actually is, the one formula that ties frequency to wavelength, why VHF and UHF usually stop at the horizon, why signals fade, how antenna polarization can cost you a contact or save it, what the ionosphere does for HF, and the handful of exotic modes that sometimes fling a VHF signal hundreds of miles past the horizon. All of it maps to pool subelement T3 — 35 questions, 3 of them on your exam.

### What a radio wave is

A radio wave is two invisible fields traveling through space together, locked at right angles. The first is an **electric field** — the same kind of field that surrounds a charged battery terminal. The second is a **magnetic field** — the same kind that wraps around a magnet or a current-carrying wire. As the wave moves, the electric field points one way, the magnetic field points at a right angle to it, and the whole package travels straight ahead in a third direction. Physicists call the package an **electromagnetic wave**. Light is exactly the same kind of wave, just at a far higher frequency than anything your radio uses.

This geometry answers two exam questions at once: a radio wave consists of an electric field and a magnetic field at right angles to each other (T3B01 and T3B03). And when hams talk about a wave's **polarization**, they mean the orientation of the electric field — never the magnetic one (T3B02). If the electric field stands up and down, the wave is vertically polarized. If it lies flat, parallel to the ground, the wave is horizontally polarized. The magnetic field is always there, riding at its right angle, but by universal agreement it doesn't get to name the wave.

Now the speed. **Free space** is the physicist's name for perfectly empty space — no air, no walls, nothing in the way. In free space, every radio wave travels at the speed of light: approximately 300,000,000 meters per second, three hundred million meters every second, written $3 \times 10^8$ m/s for short. And here is the part the exam keeps testing: the speed does not depend on frequency. A 3.5 MHz HF signal and a 440 MHz UHF signal cross the same mile of empty space in exactly the same time. You will see answer choices claiming that higher frequencies, or microwaves, travel faster. That is a myth, and it is always the wrong answer. (In air, waves move a tiny bit slower than in free space — so little that no Technician question cares.)

**FACT:** In free space every radio wave travels at the speed of light regardless of frequency — approximately 300,000,000 meters per second (3×10⁸ m/s).

### Frequency and wavelength: the one formula

Chapter 1 introduced **frequency**: the number of complete cycles a wave finishes in one second, measured in hertz (Hz). A 146 MHz signal completes 146 million cycles per second. **Wavelength** is the other half of the description: the physical distance the wave covers during one cycle — one full wave, nose to tail, measured in meters.

Because every wave moves at the same speed, frequency and wavelength are chained together. Picture a freight train passing a crossing at a fixed speed: if more cars pass each second, each car must be shorter. Same here. The higher the frequency, the shorter the wavelength — the two are **inversely related**, which is exactly the phrase the pool wants (T3B05), and the only direction that makes physical sense. An answer claiming that wavelength gets longer as frequency rises has the relationship backwards.

The full physics relationship is c = f·λ: speed equals frequency times wavelength, with the speed of light c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s. Rearrange it to solve for wavelength and you get the one formula this entire subelement asks you to remember — the pool prints it as an answer in words: wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz (T3B06). Written compactly, $\lambda = 300 / f$ when f is in MHz.

Treat the 300 as a deliberate approximation, not magic. The true speed of light is 299,792,458 meters per second; rounding to 300,000,000 turns the arithmetic into something you can do in your head, and it is plenty close for band names and exam answers. This book always presents the 300-formula that way — a handy approximation of the real relationship, never an exact identity.

> **Worked example:** Your handheld is set to 146 MHz. What is the approximate wavelength? Wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz, so 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.05 meters. That is exactly why the band from 144–148 MHz is called the 2-meter band. Run the same drill on 50 MHz: 300 ÷ 50 = 6 meters — the 6-meter band, which spans 50–54 MHz. If you can divide 300 by a number, you already own this exam question.

> **The math, if you want it:** Where does the 300 come from? Speed equals frequency times wavelength, so wavelength equals speed divided by frequency. The speed is 300,000,000 meters per second, and "megahertz" means millions of cycles per second — divide 300,000,000 by 1,000,000 to fold the "mega" into the constant, and you are left with 300 divided by the frequency in MHz. Nothing more exotic than that.

Band names come straight from this formula (T3B07): hams identify a band by its approximate wavelength in meters in addition to its frequency. A signal at 146 MHz gives you 2 meters; 50 MHz gives you 6 meters; the UHF band around 440 MHz goes by "70 centimeters" for the same reason — 300 ÷ 440 ≈ 0.68 meters, or 68 centimeters, rounded to 70. Notice the rounding: band names are approximations, friendly labels rather than exact measurements. The 2-meter band does not sit at exactly 2.000 meters, and nobody cares. What the exam expects you to know precisely are the band edges you met in the rules chapters; the wavelength name just has to be close. When another ham says "I was on 2 meters last night," they are telling you the wavelength and letting you infer the frequency.

Three spectrum boundaries round out this group, and the exam expects them memorized exactly: HF is 3 to 30 MHz, VHF is 30 to 300 MHz, and UHF is 300 to 3000 MHz (T3B08, T3B09, T3B10). The names expand to high frequency, very high frequency, and ultra high frequency, and the boundaries nest: each range starts where the previous one ends — 3, 30, 300, 3000. Say the chain out loud a few times and it is yours. Watch for the unit trap hiding in the answer choices: "300 to 3000 kHz" looks like the UHF answer, but 3000 kHz is only 3 MHz — down in HF territory, a thousandfold away from UHF.

**FACT:** The formula for converting frequency to approximate wavelength is: wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz.

**FACT:** HF is 3 to 30 MHz, VHF is 30 to 300 MHz, and UHF is 300 to 3000 MHz.

### Line of sight, the radio horizon, and fading

For daily VHF and UHF operating, think flashlight. A VHF or UHF signal normally travels in a straight line from your antenna, like the beam from a flashlight: if the other antenna is in the beam, you have a contact; if a hill or a city block stands between, you have a shadow. Hams call this **line of sight**, and it is the normal propagation mode from 6 meters on up. The ionosphere — the sky-bounce machinery you will meet shortly — usually leaves UHF signals alone, so a simplex UHF contact (both radios on the same frequency, taking turns) rarely reaches past the horizon (T3C01). Your 70-centimeter band is a local band by physics, not by rule.

The horizon that matters sits slightly farther away than the one your eyes see. The **visual horizon** is where the Earth's curve cuts off light; the **radio horizon** is where it cuts off radio. Because the atmosphere bends — refracts — radio waves slightly, they follow the Earth's curve a little way past the visual horizon (T3C11). It is a small bonus of extra distance, but the exam draws the distinction precisely: radio horizon, farther; visual horizon, nearer. One of the wrong answers claims radio signals move somewhat faster than the speed of light — you already know, from the speed-of-light section above, why that cannot be right.

**FACT:** The radio horizon lies beyond the visual horizon because the atmosphere refracts radio waves slightly.

Real line-of-sight paths are rarely clean single beams, which leads to the most tested idea in this whole chapter: **multipath**. Your signal can reach the receiving antenna over more than one path at once — one copy arriving direct, another bouncing off a warehouse wall, a water tank, a hillside. Each path is a slightly different length, so the copies arrive at slightly different moments in the wave's cycle. Where two copies arrive in step — in phase — they add, and the signal gets stronger. Where they arrive exactly out of step — out of phase — they cancel, and the signal nearly vanishes.

{{fig:ch03-multipath-fading}}

One mechanism, four exam questions:

- Move a handheld's antenna only a few feet and the VHF signal strength can jump or collapse — you have moved from a canceling spot to a reinforcing spot, or back (T3A01). A few feet is a full wavelength or more at VHF, which is all the distance multipath needs. The distractor blaming the Doppler effect is bait; Doppler shift is real, but it belongs to the satellite chapter, not to stepping sideways in a parking lot.

- Drive while listening and the flutter comes rapid-fire: the signal swells and dies, swells and dies, as the car crosses reinforcement and cancellation zones every few feet. Operators call that rapid flutter **picket fencing**, because it can sound like a stick dragged along a fence (T3A06).

- Multipath also happens on skywave paths, where the ionosphere provides the extra paths; there it produces irregular **fading** — the slow, unpredictable rise and fall you hear on HF signals (T3A08).

- For data modes, copies arriving at different moments smear into one another, and the result is increased error rates in the transmission (T3A10).

Fading is not the only way the world eats your signal; the world can also absorb it, and absorption climbs with frequency. Leaves and branches soak up UHF and microwave energy, so heavy vegetation hurts reception of weak signals on those bands (T3A02) — a forested path is a lossy path at 70 centimeters. Rain and snow decrease range at microwave frequencies (T3A07). But fog and rain have little effect on 10-meter and 6-meter signals (T3A12): at those wavelengths the weather is simply too small an obstacle to matter. The pattern to remember is that weather matters more the higher the frequency — at HF you can essentially ignore it, at microwave you cannot.

So a signal leaving your antenna can take up to three kinds of path: the **direct wave** straight through the air, line-of-sight; the **ground wave** hugging the surface, which dies out quickly at the frequencies Technicians use most; and the **sky wave**, launched upward to be bent back down by the ionosphere. Figure 3.2 shows all three leaving one transmitter. Your daily VHF life happens on the direct wave. The sky wave is HF's superpower, and it is where we go next — right after a stop to settle polarization, which behaves differently on those two kinds of path.

### Polarization in practice

Polarization stopped being abstract the first time a ham rotated an antenna and heard a signal change. Because polarization is the orientation of the electric field, it tracks the orientation of the antenna that launched the wave: a vertical whip radiates a vertically polarized wave, and a beam whose elements lie flat, parallel to the ground, radiates a horizontally polarized one. The FM and repeater world standardized on vertical, because vertical whips are what cars and handhelds wear naturally. The long-distance weak-signal world — operators chasing CW and SSB contacts at the edge of range on VHF and UHF — standardized on **horizontal** polarization (T3A03). It is the convention, everyone matches it, and matching is what matters, as the next two questions show.

Here is the pair the exam writers built on purpose, and it trips up everyone who memorizes one half without the other. Read the two as a team.

**On a line-of-sight path, polarization survives the trip.** The wave leaves your antenna vertical and arrives at the other antenna still vertical. If the receiving antenna is oriented the same way, it collects the wave fully. If it is cross-polarized — rotated 90 degrees, say a horizontal beam pointed at a vertical whip's signal — the antenna sits wrong for the field passing through it, and received signal strength drops sharply (T3A04). Picture sliding a letter through a mail slot: aligned, it slides in; rotated 90 degrees, almost nothing gets through. Cross-polarized antennas over a line-of-sight VHF or UHF path reduce received signal strength. Full stop.

**On a skywave path, the ionosphere scrambles polarization on the way.** As the charged layers bend the wave, they also twist its orientation, so the signal that comes back to Earth is **elliptically polarized** — a tumbling mixture of vertical and horizontal that keeps changing. There is no single right orientation left to match, which flips the conclusion: on an ionospheric path, either vertically or horizontally polarized antennas may be used for transmission or reception (T3A09).

Same physical wave, opposite practical advice — and no contradiction, because the paths are different. Line of sight: polarization is preserved, so match orientations or lose signal. Skywave: polarization is scrambled, so any orientation works. If you remember the pair as "match on the ground, anything goes via the sky," both questions answer themselves.

{{fig:ch03-polarization}}

One more orientation trick belongs here because it is about geometry, not hardware. Suppose buildings block your line of sight to a repeater you want to use. The direct path is in shadow — but the signal can also ricochet, and a **directional antenna** (one that concentrates in a chosen direction, like the beams you will meet in chapter 4) lets you aim at a reflected path: bounce your signal off a water tower, a big building, a hillside that both you and the repeater can see (T3A05). You are deliberately choosing the multipath route instead of suffering it. It does not always work, but it works often enough that it is the pool's own answer.

### The ionosphere and HF skip

High above the weather, starting dozens of miles up, sunlight carries enough energy to knock electrons loose from air molecules. The result is a vast, shifting region of electrically charged gas: the **ionosphere**, the region of the atmosphere that reflects HF radio waves (T3A11). "Reflects" is the pool's word and the one to pick on the exam; physicists prefer "refracts," because the bending is gradual, the way light bends entering water, rather than a hard mirror bounce. Either way, the exam's distractors are the stratosphere and the troposphere — real layers, wrong jobs.

This bending is HF's defining trait. Long-distance ionospheric propagation — **skywave**, or just "skip" — is far more common on HF than on VHF and above (T3C02). At VHF and higher, the wave mostly punches through the ionosphere and keeps going into space, which is why those UHF signals stay local. At HF the charged layers are dense enough, relative to the wavelength, to bend the wave all the way back to Earth. The classic picture is skipping stones across a pond — low, flat throws and long hops. It is a good picture as long as you stay honest about the mechanics: nothing ricochets off a hard ceiling; the wave enters the charged layer, curves through it, and comes out heading down. But the shape of the result — a hop of hundreds or thousands of miles, with silence in between — is exactly right.

The silence in between has a name. Close to the transmitter, the ground wave dies out within miles. The sky wave comes back down far away — often hundreds of miles out, sometimes much farther. The ring in between, too far for the ground wave and too near for the returning sky wave, hears nothing: the **skip zone**. It is why an HF net can be inaudible two counties away and perfectly readable two states away.

{{fig:ch03-ionosphere-skip}}

How well the ionosphere works depends on the sun, and the sun runs on a cycle of roughly eleven years from quiet to active and back. Sunspots are the visible scoreboard: more sunspots, stronger charging of the upper atmosphere, better HF skip — especially on the higher HF bands and the low-VHF bands just above them.

You will meet the ionosphere's layers by letter in the exam's answer choices, so keep a rough map. The **D region**, lowest down, mostly absorbs signals rather than returning them — when it appears in an answer choice about reaching beyond the horizon, it is bait. The **E region**, higher up, is the home of the sporadic-E openings you will meet in the next section. The **F region**, highest of the three, does the serious long-haul bending — the layer behind the daylight DX in the two questions below. (You may also see it split into F1 and F2; "F2 region refraction" shows up as a distractor on VHF questions, where it does not belong.) Hams call long-distance work "DX," and two exam questions pin down the details. First, the best long-distance propagation on 10 meters via the F region runs from dawn to shortly after sunset during periods of high sunspot activity (T3C09): daylight hours, active sun. Second, at the peak of the cycle both 6 meters and 10 meters can deliver F-region DX (T3C10). That is when the 6-meter band earns its old nickname, "the magic band": a band that behaves like ordinary VHF for years suddenly opens up to a continent.

### VHF beyond the horizon: the exotic modes

VHF's line-of-sight rule has exceptions — real, workable, and on the exam. Each exception has a name, a signature, and usually a favorite band, and the pool's job for you is matching them up.

**Sporadic E** is the exception you are most likely to notice first. Now and then, patches of the ionosphere's E region become intensely charged — charged enough to bend even VHF signals back to Earth. The result is occasional strong signals from beyond the radio horizon on the 10-, 6-, and 2-meter bands (T3C04): a quiet 2-meter band suddenly full of loud stations a thousand miles out, for minutes or hours, then gone. "Sporadic" means exactly that — it comes and goes without keeping a schedule.

**Tropospheric ducting** works lower down, in the weather layer itself. Normally air gets cooler as you climb; a **temperature inversion** flips that, trapping a layer of warm air over cooler air (T3C08). The boundary between the layers bends VHF and UHF signals and can hold them, ducting them along past the horizon the way a pipe guides water. On a regular basis — not once a decade, but often enough to plan around — tropo ducting carries VHF and UHF to ranges of approximately 300 miles (T3C06). The wrong answers name D region refraction, F2 region refraction, and Faraday rotation; the D region mostly absorbs signals rather than returning them, F2 belongs to the HF story above, and Faraday rotation is a real effect that has nothing to do with over-the-horizon VHF range.

**FACT:** Tropospheric ducting, caused by temperature inversions, regularly allows over-the-horizon VHF and UHF communications to ranges of approximately 300 miles.

**Knife-edge diffraction** is the mountain trick. Waves bend slightly as they graze a sharp edge — a ridge line, a peaked roof — and the diffracted energy spills into the shadow behind the obstruction (T3C05). Signals really do get over or around obstructions this way, weaker than line of sight but often workable.

**Auroral backscatter** is the strangest one you will ever hear. During an aurora, the charged curtains over the far north reflect VHF signals; operators point their beams at the aurora and work each other off the scatter. The returned signals arrive distorted, with a characteristic raspy sound (T3C03) — once heard, never mistaken for anything else.

**Meteor scatter** turns dust into a path. A meteor burning up leaves a short-lived ionized trail, and for a second or two that trail can reflect a VHF signal. The bursts work on several bands, but the band best suited for communicating via meteor scatter is 6 meters (T3C07).

Keep the matching list in your head: sporadic E — strong beyond-horizon openings on 10, 6, and 2 meters; tropo ducting — about 300 miles, thanks to temperature inversions; knife-edge — bending over and around ridges; aurora — raspy distortion; meteors — 6 meters. Five modes, five fingerprints.

None of this means your handheld will reach three hundred miles tomorrow. These modes are exceptions that visit, not rules you can schedule a net around — and that is exactly how the pool frames them: occasional strong signals, regular-enough ducting, brief meteor bursts. When one of them does arrive, ordinary VHF and UHF operators drop everything and call it a band opening, because for a little while the local bands stop being local. Knowing why it happens is worth three exam questions; being on the air when it happens is worth a logbook full of stories.

### Exam Focus

Subelement T3 (groups T3A–T3C) holds 35 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 3 of them — one per group. Here are eight, quoted exactly as the pool prints them, spanning all three groups; T3A04 and T3A09 are the deliberately paired polarization questions you met above.

> **T3A01** Why do VHF signal strengths sometimes vary greatly when the antenna is moved only a few feet?
> A. The signal path encounters different concentrations of water vapor
> B. VHF ionospheric propagation is very sensitive to path length
> C. Multipath propagation cancels or reinforces signals
> D. The Doppler effect causes slight frequency shifts which result in changes in signal strength
> **Answer: C** — a few feet is a wavelength at VHF, and moving that far shifts you between spots where the arriving path copies cancel and spots where they reinforce.

> **T3A04** What is the effect of antenna cross-polarization over a line-of-sight VHF or UHF path?
> A. Modulation sidebands might become inverted
> B. Received signal strength is reduced
> C. Signals have an echo effect
> D. Nothing significant will happen
> **Answer: B** — on line of sight the polarization arrives intact, so a receiving antenna rotated 90 degrees from it collects far less signal.

> **T3A09** Which of the following results from the fact that signals propagated by the ionosphere are elliptically polarized?
> A. Digital modes are unusable
> B. Either vertically or horizontally polarized antennas may be used for transmission or reception
> C. FM voice is unusable
> D. Both the transmitting and receiving antennas must be of the same polarization
> **Answer: B** — the ionosphere scrambled the polarization, so no single orientation is left to match; the deliberate contrast with T3A04.

> **T3B06** What is the formula for converting frequency to approximate wavelength in meters?
> A. Wavelength in meters equals frequency in hertz multiplied by 300
> B. Wavelength in meters equals frequency in hertz divided by 300
> C. Wavelength in meters equals frequency in megahertz divided by 300
> D. Wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz
> **Answer: D** — the pool's own drill: 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.05 meters for the 2-meter band.

> **T3B10** What frequency range is referred to as HF?
> A. 300 to 3000 MHz
> B. 30 to 300 MHz
> C. 3 to 30 MHz
> D. 300 to 3000 kHz
> **Answer: C** — HF is the bottom slice, 3 to 30 MHz; choice D is the unit trap, since 3000 kHz is only 3 MHz.

> **T3C04** Which of the following types of propagation is most commonly associated with occasional strong signals on the 10-, 6-, and 2-meter bands from beyond the radio horizon?
> A. Backscatter
> B. Sporadic E
> C. D region absorption
> D. Gray-line propagation
> **Answer: B** — sporadic E is the mode with exactly this fingerprint: occasional, strong, beyond the horizon, on 10, 6, and 2 meters.

> **T3C06** What type of propagation is responsible for allowing over-the-horizon VHF and UHF communications to ranges of approximately 300 miles on a regular basis?
> A. Tropospheric ducting
> B. D region refraction
> C. F2 region refraction
> D. Faraday rotation
> **Answer: A** — temperature inversions in the troposphere duct VHF and UHF over the horizon, regularly about 300 miles.

> **T3C11** Why is the radio horizon for VHF and UHF signals more distant than the visual horizon?
> A. Radio signals move somewhat faster than the speed of light
> B. Radio waves are not blocked by dust particles
> C. The atmosphere refracts radio waves slightly
> D. Radio waves are blocked by dust particles
> **Answer: C** — slight atmospheric refraction lets radio waves follow the Earth's curve a little past what your eyes can see.

### Key Takeaways

- A radio wave is an electric field and a magnetic field at right angles, traveling together; its polarization is defined by the orientation of the electric field.

- In free space every radio wave moves at the speed of light — approximately 300,000,000 meters per second — regardless of frequency. Nothing travels faster at higher frequencies.

- Wavelength and frequency are inversely related, and the pool's formula is wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz: 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.05 meters (the 2-meter band), 300 ÷ 50 = 6 meters (the 6-meter band).

- Memorize the boundaries exactly: HF is 3 to 30 MHz, VHF is 30 to 300 MHz, UHF is 300 to 3000 MHz.

- VHF and UHF normally travel line of sight, and the radio horizon lies slightly beyond the visual horizon because the atmosphere refracts radio waves slightly.

- Multipath — one signal arriving over several paths and combining in or out of phase — explains strength changes over a few feet, the mobile flutter called picket fencing, irregular ionospheric fading, and increased error rates on data.

- Match antenna polarizations on line-of-sight paths, because cross-polarization costs received signal; on skywave paths the ionosphere has scrambled polarization, so either orientation works.

- Skywave is HF's superpower: the ionosphere bends HF back to Earth, best from dawn to shortly after sunset during high sunspot activity, with both 6 and 10 meters delivering F-region DX at the sunspot peak — while VHF's exotic reach comes from sporadic E, tropospheric ducting (about 300 miles), knife-edge diffraction, auroral backscatter, and meteor scatter (best on 6 meters).
