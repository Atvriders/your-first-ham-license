## 1. Electricity & Radio from Zero

Imagine you have just unboxed your first handheld radio. The manual says the battery is 7.4 volts, the charger draws 1.5 amperes, and the radio transmits 5 watts — and every one of those words feels like a foreign language. This chapter teaches you that language from the very beginning; no electronics background is needed, and the only math is arithmetic you can do on a phone calculator. In this chapter you'll learn what current, voltage, resistance, and power actually are; how to convert between units like kilohertz and megahertz without breaking a sweat; the two simple formulas — Ohm's law and the power law — that the exam loves; what every basic electronic component does; and how to read the three schematic diagrams printed right in the question pool. This is the biggest teaching chapter in the book for a reason: it prepares you for 96 pool questions, 8 of the 35 on your exam.

### Electricity from zero: current, voltage, and power

Everything electrical in this book comes down to one event: electrons moving through a material. An electron is a tiny charged particle, and metals like copper are full of electrons that are free to wander. **Current** is the flow of electrons in a circuit — the exact term the pool asks for (T5A03) — and it is measured in **amperes**, usually shortened to "amps" and written A (T5A01). A **circuit**, by the way, is just a complete loop: a path out of the power source, through the components doing the work, and back again.

If current is the flow, **voltage** is the push. The friendliest analogy is water in a pipe: voltage is like water pressure, and current is like the flow rate past a point. A difference in pressure is what makes water move, and the electrical version is just as direct — a difference in voltage is what causes electron flow (T5A05). That question was revised in February 2026, so older study guides may phrase it differently; the current exam wording asks what causes electron flow, and the answer is voltage. Voltage is measured in **volts**, written V. A battery is a voltage source: it maintains a steady push between its two terminals, ready to move electrons around any loop you connect.

**Power** is the rate at which electrical energy is used (T5A10), measured in **watts**, written W (T5A02). Your radio's "5 W" transmit rating tells you how fast it turns battery energy into radio waves and heat. Power is the quantity you care about whenever you ask "how much work is this electricity doing?"

**Resistance** is opposition to current flow, measured in **ohms**, written with the Greek letter omega, Ω. Everything opposes current at least a little, and the pool wants you to know that resistance opposes every kind of current flow — DC, AC, and RF alike (T5A11). You will meet all three flavors properly in the next section; for now, read RF as "radio-frequency current, the kind that becomes radio waves."

Materials sort themselves by how freely their electrons move. A **conductor** lets current flow easily: metals conduct because they have many free electrons (T5A07), which is why wires are made of copper. An **insulator** barely conducts at all; glass is a good electrical insulator (T5A08), and so are most plastics, which is why wires wear plastic jackets.

New words: current, voltage, power, resistance, conductor, insulator, ampere, volt, watt, ohm. Master these and you hold most of pool group T5A.

### AC, DC, frequency, and wavelength

Current comes in two basic flavors. **Direct current (DC)** flows steadily in one direction — what you get from a battery. **Alternating current (AC)** reverses itself rhythmically: the pool's definition is current that alternates between positive and negative directions (T5A09). Watch for the classic wrong answer, current that alternates between "positive and zero" — that is pulsating DC, a current that surges and relaxes but never actually reverses. Wall power in the United States is AC, reversing 60 times every second.

That reversing introduces **frequency**: the number of complete cycles per second (T5A04). One cycle is one full round trip — positive, back through zero, negative, and home again. The unit of frequency is the **hertz**, written Hz (T5A06); 1 Hz is one complete cycle per second, so US wall power is 60 Hz.

{{fig:ch01-ac-dc-waveforms}}

Here is the bridge to radio. A radio wave is an alternating wave too, just millions of times faster, and every wave carries two linked properties: its frequency and its **wavelength** — the distance the wave travels during one complete cycle. The two are joined by the wave's speed: frequency times wavelength equals the speed of light, $f \times \lambda = c$, where c is about 300,000,000 meters per second. Higher frequency means shorter wavelength; they trade off.

You do not have to juggle 300,000,000. The pool itself teaches a shortcut: wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz, $\lambda \approx 300 / f$. The 300 is the speed of light rounded and re-expressed in megahertz-friendly units; chapter 3 drills this properly, so today you only need to know the relationship exists.

{{fig:ch01-wavelength-freq}}

> **Worked example:** The most popular Technician band sits around 146 MHz. What is its wavelength? λ = 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2 meters — which is exactly why hams call it "the 2-meter band." The band's nickname is simply its wavelength.

New words: alternating current (AC), direct current (DC), frequency, hertz, wavelength. That finishes pool group T5A — 11 questions you can now answer.

### Units, prefixes, and decibels

Hams deal in tiny currents and enormous frequencies, so every unit comes with a family of **metric prefixes** — multipliers that snap onto the front of a unit. The ladder steps by 1000 (three orders of magnitude) per rung:

- pico (p) = one trillionth, 10⁻¹²

- nano (n) = one billionth, 10⁻⁹

- micro (µ) = one millionth, 10⁻⁶

- milli (m) = one thousandth, 10⁻³

- (the base unit itself: volts, amperes, hertz, watts, farads)

- kilo (k) = one thousand, 10³

- mega (M) = one million, 10⁶

- giga (G) = one billion, 10⁹

One rule converts everything: **toward a smaller unit, multiply; toward a larger unit, divide** — by 1000 for each rung you cross. Milli- is smaller than the base unit, so amperes to milliamperes multiplies: 1.5 A = 1500 mA (T5B01). Kilo- is larger, so kilohertz to megahertz divides: 28400 kHz = 28.400 MHz (T5B12). Crossing two rungs at once is a factor of 1000 × 1000 — pico to micro, for instance: 1,000,000 pF = 1 µF (T5B08). The same rule covers 1,500,000 Hz = 1500 kHz (T5B02), one kilovolt = 1000 volts (T5B03), one microvolt = one millionth of a volt (T5B04), 500 milliwatts = 0.5 W (T5B05), 3000 mA = 3 A (T5B06), and 2425 MHz = 2.425 GHz (T5B13).

{{fig:ch01-prefix-ladder}}

> **Worked example:** Write 3.525 MHz in kHz — this is pool question T5B07 almost word for word. The kHz is the smaller unit, one rung down the ladder, so multiply by 1000: 3.525 × 1000 = 3525 kHz. Sliding the decimal point three places is the whole trick.

One more measuring word: the **decibel (dB)**, a way of comparing two powers as a ratio. Forget logarithms — the exam only needs three anchors. A power increase to double is about 3 dB (5 W to 10 W, T5B09). An increase to four times is about 6 dB — and ratios work in reverse, so shrinking to one quarter is about −6 dB (12 W to 3 W, T5B10). An increase to ten times is exactly 10 dB (20 W to 200 W, T5B11). Positive dB means an increase; negative dB means a decrease.

**FACT:** A power increase to double is approximately 3 dB, to four times is approximately 6 dB (so a decrease to one quarter is approximately −6 dB), and to ten times is exactly 10 dB.

> **The math, if you want it:** Decibels are defined by $\mathrm{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(P_2 / P_1)$ — ten times the base-10 logarithm of the power ratio. The anchors fall straight out of it: log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.3 gives roughly 3 dB, and log₁₀(10) = 1 gives exactly 10 dB. Nothing beyond the anchors is ever on the exam.

New words: milli-, micro-, nano-, pico-, kilo-, mega-, giga-, decibel. That is all of pool group T5B.

### Capacitance, inductance, and impedance

Two more "abilities to store energy" complete the basic vocabulary, and the exam's favorite trick is swapping them, so learn them as a pair:

- **Capacitance** is the ability to store energy in an **electric field**, and its unit is the **farad**, written F (T5C01, T5C02).

- **Inductance** is the ability to store energy in a **magnetic field**, and its unit is the **henry**, written H (T5C03, T5C04).

Memory hook: a **c**apacitor holds an **e**lectric field — C and E sit side by side. The leftover partner, the inductor, takes the magnetic field. You will meet the physical components a few sections from now; here it is the quantities that matter.

**Impedance** is the total opposition a circuit offers to alternating current — ordinary resistance plus **reactance**, the extra opposition that capacitors and inductors present to AC (T5C12). It is measured in ohms, just like resistance (T5C05): resistance for the DC world, impedance for the AC world.

One warning that earns exam points: unit abbreviations are case-sensitive, and the pool tests the cases. Kilohertz is written **kHz** — lowercase k (T5C06 asks exactly this) — while megahertz is written **MHz** with a capital M (T5C07). In both, the H is capital, honoring Heinrich Hertz. The same pattern runs through mA, µV, and pF: prefix case is load-bearing, so "KHZ" and "Mhz" are always wrong answers.

**FACT:** Unit abbreviations are case-sensitive and the pool tests them: kilohertz is written kHz (lowercase k) and megahertz is written MHz (capital M) — in both, the H is capital.

New words: capacitance, farad, inductance, henry, impedance, reactance, kHz, MHz — the first half of pool group T5C.

### The power law: P = E × I

Now the two formulas that carry the exam's math questions. First, a notation alert: **the exam writes voltage as E** — from EMF, electromotive force, an old name for voltage — while most modern books, including this one, write V. E and V both mean volts, and you may treat them as the same letter. The pool also prints multiplication as a plain "x" where this book uses ×. So the pool's "P = I x E" and this book's $P = V \times I$ are the same sentence.

Here is the **power law**: power equals voltage multiplied by current, $P = V \times I$ (T5C08, whose printed answer is "P = I x E" — same thing, in the pool's notation). The intuition: push (volts) times flow (amperes) equals rate of work (watts). Rearrange the formula two ways and you can find any one quantity from the other two: I = P ÷ V and V = P ÷ I.

> **Worked example:** Your mobile radio draws 10 A from the car's 13.8 V supply. How much power is that? P = V × I = 13.8 V × 10 A = 138 W (T5C09). Two siblings use the same pattern: a 12 V supply pushing 2.5 A delivers 12 × 2.5 = 30 W (T5C10), and delivering 120 W at 12 V takes 120 ÷ 12 = 10 A (T5C11). Every wrong answer is the right two numbers combined the wrong way.

**FACT:** The formula for electrical power in a DC circuit is P = E × I — power equals voltage multiplied by current (the pool's own answer prints "P = I x E").

That completes pool group T5C.

### Ohm's law and series/parallel circuits

**Ohm's law** ties voltage, current, and resistance into one relationship: $V = I \times R$ — or as the pool prints it, E = I × R (T5D01–T5D03). The easiest way to hold all three forms is the cover-the-unknown triangle: cover the letter you want to find, and what remains is the formula. Cover I and get I = V ÷ R; cover R and get R = V ÷ I; cover V and get V = I × R. If you remember one picture for exam day, make it this one.

{{fig:ch01-ohms-law-triangle}}

Drill the triangle in all three orientations with the pool's own numbers:

> **Worked example:** A resistor has 12 V across it and 1.5 A flowing through it. What is its resistance? You want R, so cover R: R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 1.5 = 8 Ω (T5D05). Its siblings: 90 V with 3 A flowing gives 90 ÷ 3 = 30 Ω (T5D04); a 24 Ω resistor across 240 V carries 240 ÷ 24 = 10 A (T5D09); and 2 A through 10 Ω needs 2 × 10 = 20 V (T5D12). Notice that each distractor is the same two numbers with the wrong operation — if you know which letter you are solving for, you cannot lose.

Two last circuit patterns. In a **series circuit**, the components are strung one after another on a single path, like beads on one string — so the same current flows through every component (T5D13). In a **parallel circuit**, each component gets its own branch across the same two points — so the same voltage appears across every component (T5D14). Hook: "**s**eries, **s**ame current." Your house is wired in parallel, which is why every outlet sees the same 120 V.

{{fig:ch01-series-parallel}}

**FACT:** Ohm's law is E = I × R, rearranged as I = E / R and R = E / I.

**FACT:** In a series circuit the current is the same through all components, and in a parallel circuit the voltage is the same across all components.

New words: Ohm's law, series circuit, parallel circuit. Pool group T5D — all 14 questions — is now yours.

### Passive components, switches, and batteries

Time to meet the parts themselves. A **resistor** is a component whose whole job is to oppose — that is, limit — current flow (T6A01). Resistors set currents to safe values, divide voltages, and appear in nearly every circuit ever drawn. A **potentiometer** is an adjustable resistor — think of a volume knob — and the parameter it controls is resistance (T6A02, T6A03).

The two energy-storers from earlier, now as hardware. A **capacitor** is two conductive plates separated by an insulating layer called the **dielectric**, and it stores energy in an electric field between the plates (T6A04, T6A05). An **inductor** is a coil of wire, and it stores energy in the magnetic field that builds around the coil (T6A06, T6A07). Same electric/magnetic pairing as before — the components match the quantities.

**Switches** have a small vocabulary of their own. The **pole** count is the number of separate circuits the switch handles; the **throw** count is the number of contact positions each pole can connect to. An SPST switch — single-pole single-throw — is a plain on-off: one circuit, one contact position. An **SPDT** switch — single-pole double-throw — connects one circuit to either of two others (T6A08), like a railroad turnout sending one train down track A or track B. Keep SPST in mind: component 3 in pool figure T-2 is one (T6A09), and you will confirm that yourself when we read the figure shortly.

Finally, batteries. **Rechargeable** chemistries include nickel-metal hydride (NiMH), lithium-ion, lead-acid, and nickel-cadmium (T6A10) — the stuff of handheld-radio battery packs and car batteries. The odd one out, and the exam's target, is **carbon-zinc**: the classic disposable flashlight battery, the one listed chemistry that is not rechargeable (T6A11).

New words: resistor, potentiometer, capacitor, dielectric, inductor, pole, throw, SPST, SPDT, rechargeable, carbon-zinc, NiMH, lithium-ion, lead-acid — all of pool group T6A.

### Semiconductors: diodes, LEDs, and transistors

A **semiconductor** is a material between conductor and insulator — usually silicon — whose conduction engineers can control with great precision. That controllability is what makes modern electronics possible, and three exam components are built from it.

The **diode** is the one-way valve of electronics: it lets current flow in one direction only (T6B02). Its two electrodes are the **anode**, the entry side when the diode conducts, and the **cathode**, the exit (T6B09) — and on the physical part the cathode end is often marked with a stripe (T6B06), matching the bar in its schematic symbol. Pushing current through in the conducting direction costs a small **forward voltage drop**, which differs among diode types (T6B01). An **LED** — light-emitting diode — is simply a diode that emits light when forward current flows through it (T6B07).

The **transistor** is the component that built the modern world: three regions of semiconductor material (T6B04) that can work as an **electronic switch**, with a small current turning a larger one on and off (T6B03), or as an **amplifier**, a circuit that provides power gain (T6B10). **Gain** just means the output compared to the input — of voltage, current, or power (T6B11).

Transistors come in two families with two different sets of electrode names, and the pool drills both, plus the diode's pair:

| Component | Electrodes |
|---|---|
| Diode | anode, cathode |
| Bipolar junction transistor (BJT) | emitter, base, collector (T6B12) |
| Field effect transistor (FET) | gate, drain, source (T6B05) |

And the acronym: **FET stands for Field Effect Transistor** (T6B08). Say the three rows out loud once — anode/cathode, emitter/base/collector, gate/drain/source — and the electrode questions cannot surprise you.

New words: semiconductor, diode, anode, cathode, forward voltage drop, LED, forward current, transistor, BJT, FET, emitter/base/collector, gate/drain/source, gain — all of pool group T6B.

### Reading a schematic: the three pool figures

A **schematic** is an electrical diagram drawn with standard component **symbols** (T6C01), and here is the insight that matters: what a schematic shows accurately is how components are connected — not wire lengths, not physical appearance (T6C12). A schematic is a subway map, not an aerial photo. Where wires join electrically, the drawing places a filled dot (a **node**, or junction), and a **ground symbol** — drawn the pool's way, as three slanted strokes of decreasing length, longest on top — marks the circuit's common return point. Twelve of the pool's questions ask you to identify numbered parts on three schematics — and because they sit in three different exam groups, up to three of the 35 questions on your exam can come straight from this section.

{{fig:ch01-component-symbols}}

Walk the symbol panel once: a zigzag is a resistor (add a diagonal arrow for the variable kind); two facing plates are a capacitor (a curved lower plate for the polarized kind, an arrow through the plates for variable); humps are an inductor (an arrow tap for variable); two coils facing each other across core lines are a transformer; a triangle pointing into a bar is a diode (add two radiating arrows for an LED, or a bent Z-shaped bar for a Zener); a circle with an arrowed leg is an NPN transistor; alternating long and short plates are a battery; the three slanted strokes are ground; a blade between two circles is a switch; a small open rectangle is a fuse; a dome is a lamp; a curly brace is a connector; an outline triangle is an antenna; and a circle with two bars inside is the AC voltage source.

**Figure T-1 — a transistor lamp switch.**

{{fig:ch01-pool-fig-t1}}

At the far left sits an unnumbered two-position switch. Its upper contact runs through **component 1, a resistor**, into the base of **component 2, an NPN transistor** — the tell is the arrow on its lower (emitter) leg, pointing outward. **Component 3 is a lamp**, the dome on the top wire, and **component 4 is a battery** at the right. **Component 5 is a ground symbol** beneath the switch's lower contact; identical unnumbered grounds sit under the transistor and the battery. The circuit story: close the switch and a small base current flows through resistor 1; the transistor then conducts, switching battery current through the lamp. The exam asks for each part by number: component 1 is the resistor (T6C02), 2 the transistor (T6C03), 3 the lamp (T6C04), 4 the battery (T6C05) — and the transistor's function is to **control the flow of current** (T6D10).

**Figure T-2 — an AC power supply.**

{{fig:ch01-pool-fig-t2}}

Read it left to right, the way the power flows. **Component 1 is the AC voltage source** (wall power), **2 a fuse** in the top wire, and **3 a switch** — one blade controlling one circuit with one contact position: single-pole single-throw, which is exactly what T6A09 asks. **4 is the transformer**, stepping the AC voltage down; **5 a rectifier diode**; **6 a capacitor** — the polarized plates — smoothing the pulsing DC; **7 a resistor and 8 an LED** (the two radiating arrows give the LED away) forming the "power on" indicator branch; **9 a variable resistor**, the zigzag with a diagonal arrow; and **10 a Zener diode** holding the output steady, with the output terminal at the far right and ground symbols along the bottom. The exam asks about component 4 (the transformer, T6C09), 6 (the capacitor, T6C06), 8 (the LED, T6C07), and 9 (the variable resistor, T6C08).

**Figure T-3 — an antenna tuner, also called a transmatch.**

{{fig:ch01-pool-fig-t3}}

**Component 1 is a connector** — this is where the transmitter plugs in. The signal runs through **component 2, a variable capacitor**, past a junction dot, through a second (unnumbered) variable capacitor, and up to **component 4, the antenna**. Hanging from the junction down to ground is **component 3, a variable inductor** — a coil with an adjustable tap shorting part of it. Two adjustable capacitors plus an adjustable inductor to ground make a "T"-shaped matching network between transmitter and antenna. Now the trap the exam sets: T6C10 asks what component 3 is, and "variable capacitor" waits right there as a choice because component 2, sitting next to it, is one. The asked component is the coil — the variable **inductor**. Learn the position numbers, not just the symbol shapes, and T6C10 and T6C11 (component 4: the antenna) are free points.

New words: schematic, component symbol, ground symbol, node/junction. That covers pool group T6C — and every figure question in the entire pool lives in this one section.

### What circuits do

The last group asks what jobs components do inside real equipment. Follow the DC power-supply chain you just walked in figure T-2: **transformer → rectifier → filter → regulator**. The **transformer** changes AC voltage up or down — never to DC (T6D06). The **rectifier** (the diode) changes AC into a varying, pulsating DC (T6D01). The filter capacitor smooths those pulses, and the **voltage regulator** holds the output voltage steady (T6D05) — in figure T-2, that is the Zener diode's job.

Four more one-liners. A **relay** is an electrically controlled switch: a small current through its coil switches a larger current (T6D02). **Shielded wire** — a conductor wrapped in a conductive sleeve — keeps unwanted signals from coupling in or out (T6D03). A **meter** displays an electrical quantity such as voltage or current (T6D04). And the standard visual indicator component is the LED (T6D07) — cheap, bright, and nearly immortal.

Finally, two radio-flavored stars. Put an inductor and a capacitor together — in series or in parallel — and you get a **resonant (tuned) circuit**: the circuit that selects one frequency out of the crowd (T6D08, T6D11). Every radio uses tuned circuits to pick out the station you want. And an **integrated circuit (IC)** — a "chip" — packs many semiconductors and other components into one package (T6D09).

New words: rectifier, regulator, transformer, relay, shielded wire, meter, indicator, resonant/tuned circuit, integrated circuit (IC), Zener diode — and with that, pool group T6D and all 96 of this chapter's questions are covered.

### Exam Focus

This chapter covers pool subelements T5 (Electrical Principles) and T6 (Electronic and Electrical Components) — groups T5A through T5D and T6A through T6D, 96 of the pool's 409 questions, worth 8 of the 35 questions on your exam: one from each group. Here are ten representative questions, quoted exactly as the pool prints them — including the revised T5A05 and all three pool figures.

> **T5A05** A difference in which of the following causes electron flow?
> A. Voltage
> B. Ampere-hours
> C. Capacitance
> D. Inductance
> **Answer: A** — a difference in voltage is the push that makes electrons flow; this is the question's revised 2026 wording.

> **T5B09** Which decibel value most closely represents a power increase from 5 watts to 10 watts?
> A. 2 dB
> B. 3 dB
> C. 5 dB
> D. 10 dB
> **Answer: B** — doubling power is the ≈3 dB anchor.

> **T5C08** What formula is used to calculate electrical power (P) in a DC circuit?
> A. P = I x E
> B. P = E / I
> C. P = I2 x E
> D. P = I / E
> **Answer: A** — power equals current times voltage; the pool prints "P = I x E", this book writes P = V × I — same formula.

> **T5D01** What formula is used to calculate current in a circuit?
> A. I = E x R
> B. I = E / R
> C. I = E2 x R
> D. I = E2 / R
> **Answer: B** — cover I in the Ohm's-law triangle and E over R remains: I = E / R.

> **T6A09** What type of switch is represented by component 3 in figure T-2?
> A. Single-pole single-throw
> B. Single-pole double-throw
> C. Double-pole single-throw
> D. Double-pole double-throw
> **Answer: A** — one blade controlling one circuit with one contact position: single-pole single-throw.

> **T6B12** What are the names of the electrodes of a bipolar junction transistor?
> A. Signal, bias, power
> B. Emitter, base, collector
> C. Input, output, supply
> D. Pole one, pole two, output
> **Answer: B** — emitter, base, collector; gate, drain, and source belong to the FET.

> **T6C02** What is component 1 in figure T-1?
> A. Resistor
> B. Transistor
> C. Battery
> D. Connector
> **Answer: A** — the zigzag feeding the transistor's base.

> **T6C06** What is component 6 in figure T-2?
> A. Resistor
> B. Capacitor
> C. Regulator IC
> D. Transistor
> **Answer: B** — the polarized-plate filter capacitor smoothing the DC rail.

> **T6C10** What is component 3 in figure T-3?
> A. Connector
> B. Meter
> C. Variable capacitor
> D. Variable inductor
> **Answer: D** — the tapped coil to ground; component 2 next to it is the variable capacitor set as the trap.

> A. Give off light when current flows through it

### Key Takeaways

- Current is the flow of electrons, measured in amperes; voltage is the push that causes the flow, measured in volts; power is the rate at which electrical energy is used, measured in watts; resistance opposes every kind of current — DC, AC, and RF — and is measured in ohms.

- Frequency counts complete AC cycles per second, in hertz; wavelength is the distance a wave travels in one cycle, and the pool's shortcut is λ in meters ≈ 300 ÷ frequency in MHz — which is why 146 MHz is the 2-meter band.

- The prefix ladder steps by 1000: pico, nano, micro, milli, base, kilo, mega, giga — toward the smaller unit, multiply; toward the larger, divide. Unit case matters: kHz takes a lowercase k, MHz a capital M.

- Two triangles cover the exam's math: Ohm's law V = I × R and the power law P = V × I. Cover the unknown letter to get the formula — and remember that the pool's E and this book's V both mean volts.

- Decibels by anchors: double ≈ 3 dB, four times ≈ 6 dB (so one quarter ≈ −6 dB), ten times = 10 dB; positive is an increase, negative a decrease.

- Components in one line each: a resistor limits current, a capacitor stores an electric field, an inductor stores a magnetic field, a diode passes current one way, an LED lights up, a transistor switches or amplifies, and an inductor plus a capacitor selects a frequency.

- A schematic shows how components connect, drawn with standard symbols rather than pictures — learn the numbered positions in pool figures T-1, T-2, and T-3, including the trap that figure T-3's component 3 is the variable inductor.
