## 0. Welcome: What Ham Radio Is & How Licensing Works

Maybe you picked up a little radio at a flea market and wondered who you could talk to with it. Maybe you watched a volunteer at a bike race relay messages with a handheld, or you just like the idea of talking across the world without a phone company in the middle. However you got here, you are welcome — this book assumes no electronics background, no radio experience, and no Morse code. In this chapter you'll learn what ham radio actually is, how the three US license classes fit together, what the Technician exam looks like, and exactly what happens between "I think I'll take the test" and your first legal transmission.

### What Ham Radio Is (and Isn't)

Amateur radio — "ham radio" — is a licensed radio service for people who communicate and experiment for the love of it. Its official name is the Amateur Radio Service, and in the United States it is regulated by the FCC, the Federal Communications Commission, the government agency that writes and enforces the rules for the service.

So what do hams actually do? A lot, it turns out. Hams talk locally on VHF and UHF — the very high frequency and ultra high frequency bands that carry everyday chats across town, often through relay stations called repeaters. Hams talk around the world on HF, the high frequency "shortwave" bands whose signals can bounce off the upper atmosphere and land on another continent. Hams experiment: they build antennas, write software, bounce signals off the Moon, and push data through the air in new ways. And hams serve: when a hurricane or wildfire knocks out phones and internet, trained amateur operators are often the ones carrying messages for their communities.

This is not a side effect. It is the service's reason for existing. FCC rule §97.1 states the basis and purpose of the Amateur Radio Service in five prongs, and they are worth knowing from day one. First, emergency communications: ham radio is a voluntary, noncommercial communication service, valued especially for what it provides in an emergency. Second, advancing the radio art: hams have a proven record of pushing radio technology forward, and the service exists to continue that. Third, advancing skills in both the communication and technical phases of the art — meaning how to operate well and how the equipment works. Fourth, the service expands the existing reservoir of trained operators, technicians, and electronics experts. Fifth, it continues the amateur's unique ability to enhance international goodwill, one person-to-person contact at a time. Chapter 8 gives these five prongs their full treatment; for now, just notice how much of the hobby's culture they explain.

Two things ham radio is not. It is not commercial: by rule, the service is noncommercial, so you cannot use it to conduct business or get paid for operating. And it is not exclusive. There is no age limit for a US amateur license, and no Morse code exam for any license class — the FCC dropped that requirement back in 2007. Kids pass this exam. Retirees pass this exam. People who have never soldered a wire pass this exam, because the entire question pool is public and this book teaches all of it.

One honest note before we go further: this book is about the United States. The exam you will take is a US exam, run under FCC rules, and other countries run their own licensing systems. If you are in the US, you are in exactly the right place.

### The Three License Classes

Your **license class** is the level of license you hold, and each class grants its own privileges — the frequencies and ways of operating you may use. Think of the three US classes as steps on a ladder, each one granting more than the last.

The first step is **Technician**, the license this book prepares you for. A Technician can use all amateur frequencies above 30 MHz — that covers the VHF and UHF bands where everyday local and regional radio lives — plus limited privileges on the HF bands. For most new hams, that means handhelds, mobile radios, local repeaters, satellites, and a taste of worldwide HF. It is a genuinely big playground.

The second step is **General**, which adds privileges on all bands and modes — most of HF, in plain terms, which is where long-distance contacts become routine. The third step is **Amateur Extra**, which conveys all available US amateur privileges. Most new hams start at Technician and upgrade later, and passed exams never have to be repeated.

You may also hear older names: Novice, Technician Plus, Advanced. Here is the precise situation. Five classes of amateur operator license exist in the FCC's rules — Novice, Technician, General, Advanced, and Amateur Extra — but the FCC currently issues new licenses only for Technician, General, and Amateur Extra. The others are legacy classes, sometimes called grandfathered classes: a grandfathered class is one the FCC no longer issues to anyone new, while people who already hold it keep their licenses and privileges. If someone at a radio club mentions an Advanced class ticket, that is history talking, not a path available to you.

Each class has its own written exam, and the exams are called elements. The Technician exam is Element 2. General adds Element 3, and Amateur Extra adds Element 4.

**FACT:** The Technician class license requires passing examination Element 2 only; General requires Elements 2 and 3; Amateur Extra requires Elements 2, 3, and 4.

One element, one exam, one afternoon. That is the whole barrier between you and the airwaves.

### The Exam Itself

The Technician exam is 35 multiple-choice questions, and you need 26 correct answers to pass. That is the passing score — about three quarters, which means you can miss nine questions and still walk out licensed.

**FACT:** Element 2 is "35 questions concerning the privileges of a Technician Class operator license. The minimum passing score is 26 questions answered correctly."

Here is the part that should calm any exam nerves. Every question on every Technician exam comes out of one public document: the **question pool**, the complete published list of questions from which exams are drawn. The 2026–2030 Technician pool contains exactly 409 questions. It is organized into ten topic areas called subelements, named T1 through T9 plus T0 — rules, operating procedures, propagation, station practices, electrical principles, components, circuits, signals, antennas, and safety. Each subelement is divided into **groups**, 35 groups in all, and your exam draws exactly one question from each group. So the exam is automatically balanced across every topic.

The pool is written and published by the NCVEC, the National Conference of Volunteer Examiner Coordinators, and it is fixed for exams given from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2030. Fixed means fixed: there are no surprise questions, no trick material from outside the pool, and no last-minute swaps. Every question on your exam is in Appendix A of this book, word for word, with the correct answer and a plain-language explanation of why it is correct. By exam day you will have seen every question you could possibly be asked.

### Before Exam Day

Do this first, days before your exam: get your FRN. The FRN is your identity in every piece of FCC business you will ever do.

**FACT:** An FRN (FCC Registration Number) is a 10-digit number assigned to a business or individual registering with the FCC, used to identify all of the registrant's business dealings with the FCC.

You get your FRN by registering in CORES, the FCC's COmmission REgistration System, using an FCC username account you create on the FCC's website. Registering in CORES to get your FRN carries no fee and no exam requirement. An FRN registered online is available immediately — you will walk away from the website with your number in hand. Write it down and bring it to your exam session, because exam candidates are required to have their FRN before exam day. One privacy point worth knowing: your Social Security number is given to the FCC inside CORES registration. It does not go on the exam form.

Next, find an exam session — a scheduled event where the exam is actually given. Exams are administered by VEs, Volunteer Examiners: accredited amateur operators who give exams as a team of at least three. Every VE team works under a VEC, a Volunteer Examiner Coordinator, which is an FCC-recognized organization that coordinates exam sessions and forwards the results to the FCC. Two good places to find a session are the ARRL's online exam-session finder, which searches by ZIP code, is updated daily, and includes sessions sponsored by non-ARRL organizations, and hamstudy.org/sessions, which lists sessions run by many different VE teams.

Some teams also run remote exams — online sessions supervised over video — and the finders list them alongside in-person ones. Whether remote testing is available depends entirely on the individual VE team, and some organizations test in person only, so treat remote exams as a possibility to ask about, never a certainty to plan around.

What do you bring? One legal photo ID — a state driver's license, government passport, military ID, or student school photo ID all work — or, if you have no photo ID, two forms of non-photo ID such as a birth certificate with a seal or a Social Security card. Bring your FRN. And bring the session fee, which pays for one attempt at each of the three exam elements if you want to try for more than Technician on the same day. The ARRL VEC exam session fee is $15.00 for calendar 2026.

If you are younger than 18, the ARRL VEC session fee is a reduced $5.00, and ARRL's Youth Licensing Grant Program reimburses the FCC's one-time application fee for new-license candidates under 18 who test under the ARRL VEC program.

At the session you will fill in the application form, the NCVEC Quick-Form 605 — Form 605 for short — the standard exam-session application. Your email address and FRN are mandatory on it. The email matters because the FCC sends all of its correspondence, including your license itself, by email.

### After You Pass: From CSCE to Call Sign

The moment you hand in your answer sheet, the VEs grade it — immediately, right there at the session. When you pass, three VEs certify that you are qualified for the license, and they issue you a Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination, universally called a CSCE: the VEs' signed proof that you passed. Keep it safe; it conveys exam credit for 365 days.

Then the paperwork leaves your hands. The VE team sends your application to the coordinating VEC — never directly to the FCC — and the VEC screens the information, resolves any discrepancies, and forwards everything to the FCC electronically.

Now the one fee in the whole process.

**FACT:** The FCC charges a $35 application fee, effective April 19, 2022, on applications for a new license, a renewal, a rule waiver, or a modification requesting a new vanity call sign — per application.

In the FCC process as described by ARRL, when the FCC receives your application from the VEC it emails you a payment-instructions link, and you have 10 calendar days from the issuance of your application file number to pay. Nobody collects this fee at the exam session — VECs and VE teams must not collect it — because you pay online, directly to the FCC, through the CORES payment system. If the fee is not paid within the 10-day window the FCC dismisses the application, but you would not have to retest: the VEC can refile the application at any time before your CSCE expires.

Now the hard rule of this whole chapter, stated before anything else: do not transmit yet. Passing the exam feels like finishing, but legally it is not.

**FACT:** Your operating authority begins when your license grant appears in the FCC's ULS consolidated license database — passing the exam alone does not authorize you to transmit.

The ULS is the FCC's Universal Licensing System, the public database whose entry for your grant is your operating authority. A **license grant** is simply the FCC's official entry in that database saying your license has been issued. The wait is short: your grant typically appears in the ULS the next business day after you pay the application fee. Typically is the honest word — it is not a promise, so allow a few days to about two weeks, and look your name up in the ULS before you press the transmit button. The moment your grant appears there with your new call sign, you are a licensed radio amateur, and the airwaves are yours.

Two more pieces of mail arrive in that same stretch, both by email. After the fee is paid and the application processed, the FCC emails a link to your official electronic license — no paper license arrives in the mail — and the download link in that email is valid for 30 days, so save the file somewhere safe. And your **call sign**, the letters and numbers that will identify your station on the air for years to come, arrives with the grant itself. You do not pick it: it is assigned by the sequential call sign system, the FCC's default method, which draws the next available call sign from the regional-group list for your license class and mailing address. If you later want something shorter or more personal, a vanity call sign is a possibility down the road — but your sequential call sign works perfectly, and plenty of hams keep theirs for life.

### Your First Checklist

Everything in this chapter, compressed into what you can actually do, starting today:

1. Register in CORES with an FCC username account and write down your FRN.

2. Find an exam session near you with the ARRL session finder or hamstudy.org/sessions — or ask a VE team whether it offers a remote session.

3. Read chapters 1 through 9 of this book.

4. Drill with Appendix A and the practice-exam generator until you are passing practice exams comfortably.

5. The night before, gather your photo ID, your FRN, and the session fee.

6. Pass the 35-question exam and walk out with your CSCE.

7. Pay the $35 FCC application fee online within 10 days of the payment email.

8. Wait for your license grant to appear in the ULS — no transmitting before that moment.

9. Get on the air and make your first contact.

### Key Takeaways

- Ham radio is a noncommercial radio service for communication, experimentation, and emergency service — and anyone can get in, with no age limit and no Morse code exam.

- Three license classes are issued today: Technician, General, and Amateur Extra; this book takes you through Technician.

- The Technician exam is 35 questions drawn one per group from the public 409-question pool, and 26 correct answers pass.

- The pool is fixed for exams from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2030, and every question is printed in Appendix A — there are no surprise questions.

- Get your FRN in CORES before exam day; bring one legal photo ID (or two non-photo IDs), your FRN, and the session fee.

- After you pass, the VEC forwards your application to the FCC, and the FCC emails you a link to pay the application fee online.

- Passing the exam alone does not authorize you to transmit — your operating authority begins when your license grant appears in the FCC's ULS database.
