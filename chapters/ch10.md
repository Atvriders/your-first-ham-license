## 10. Exam Day & Your First Radio

Tomorrow is the exam. Your ID is on the kitchen table, your FRN is written on an index card, and you have taken so many practice exams that the questions are starting to feel like old friends. That is exactly where you should be. In this chapter you'll learn how to spend the final week, what actually happens at an exam session from sign-in to grading, what to do if the first attempt does not go your way, how a passed exam becomes a real call sign, what kind of radio to buy first, and how to turn your first thirty days on the air into a habit that sticks.

### The Week Before: Study Strategy

You have read the book. Now the job shifts from learning to drilling, and the pool makes that easy: every question on your exam is one you have already seen. Reread the Exam Focus section at the end of each chapter — those are the questions each chapter exists to answer, with the plain-language reasons the answers are correct. Then work Appendix A end to end. It is the entire 409-question pool, verbatim, with the correct answer marked and a one-line why for every question. If any group of questions still feels shaky, go back to the chapter it points to rather than memorizing around the gap.

Finish with timed practice exams. This book's companion practice test draws a valid 35-question exam from the pool — one question per group, exactly like the real thing — and grades it for you with the explanations, so you can test yourself honestly. Take a few of these under exam conditions: no notes, no phone, one sitting. When you are passing comfortably at home, you are ready, because the real exam asks nothing you have not already answered.

A few test-taking habits pay for themselves on a fixed public pool. Read all four choices before you pick one — some questions offer "all these choices are correct," and the right move is to verify each item on its own rather than grabbing it automatically or skipping it on principle. On the math questions, watch for the wrong-operation distractors: the choices typically include the answer you get if you multiply when you should have divided, so do the arithmetic before you look, then find your number among the options. And keep the scoring in your head like a warm coat: 26 of 35 passes. You can miss nine questions and still walk out with a pass. Calm beats perfect.

### Exam Day: What Happens at the Session

Pack three things the night before. First, identification: one legal photo ID, or two forms of non-photo ID if you have none.

**FACT:** Exam candidates must present one legal photo ID (such as a state driver's license, government passport, military ID, or student school photo ID) or, if none, two forms of non-photo ID (such as a birth certificate with seal or a Social Security card).

Second, your FRN — the 10-digit FCC Registration Number you got in CORES before exam day, which identifies you in every piece of FCC business. Third, the session fee. The ARRL VEC exam session fee is $15.00 for calendar 2026, and one fee pays for one attempt at each of the three exam elements if you want to try for more than Technician on the same day.

If you are younger than 18, the ARRL VEC session fee for calendar 2026 is a reduced $5.00, and ARRL's Youth Licensing Grant Program reimburses the FCC's one-time application fee for new-license candidates under 18 who test under the ARRL VEC program.

Other VECs set their own fees, and some teams charge nothing at all, so check with the team running your session rather than assuming a number.

At sign-in you will complete the application form: the NCVEC Quick-Form 605, usually just called Form 605, the standard exam-session application (the current edition is the 2022 form). You fill in Section 1, where your email address and FRN are mandatory — the email matters because the FCC sends all of its correspondence, including your license, by email. Every applicant also answers the Basic Qualification Question, which asks about felony conviction status. Answer it honestly. A YES triggers additional FCC procedures, but it is not an automatic bar to a license.

Then the exam itself. You will be tested by a team of at least three VEs — Volunteer Examiners, accredited amateur operators working under a VEC, a Volunteer Examiner Coordinator, the FCC-recognized organization that coordinates the session. A typical ARRL VEC session tests about ten people and lasts about three and a half hours, most of which is paperwork and waiting while others finish. Your exam is 35 multiple-choice questions drawn one per group from the pool. Picture an ordinary room — a library meeting room, a church hall — a dozen people bent over answer sheets, and a small team of friendly volunteers who genuinely want you to pass. That is the whole scene.

One variation worth knowing about: some VE teams run remote exams — online sessions supervised over video — alongside in-person ones. Availability depends entirely on the individual team, and some organizations test in person only, so treat remote testing as a possibility to ask about, never a certainty to plan around. The ARRL session finder and hamstudy.org/sessions both list which sessions are which.

When you hand in your answer sheet, the VEs grade it immediately, right there at the session, and the VEs alone determine whether your answers are correct. If you pass, three VEs certify that you are qualified for the license grant and issue you a Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination — the CSCE, their signed proof that you passed. If you do not pass, the VEs return your application and tell you the grade. Either way, you leave the room knowing exactly where you stand.

### If You Don't Pass (and It's Fine)

Plenty of fine operators failed an element on the way in, and the system has a door for exactly that situation — though it is the team's door to open, not yours.

**FACT:** Under ARRL VEC retest policy, a failed element may be retaken at the same session only if the team has a different version of that element the applicant has not taken, the team has the time, resources, and willingness, and the applicant pays an additional test fee.

**FACT:** Nothing in FCC rules entitles a failed candidate to an immediate retest — offering a second (different-version) exam at the same session is entirely the VE team's decision.

Notice why the retake is always a different version: by rule, no compromised examination may be administered, and the same question set may never be re-administered to the same examinee. So if the team offers you a second try, thank them, take a breath, and go again — it is a fresh draw from the same pool you have been drilling. If they cannot offer one, there is no shame in coming back to another session. The pool is fixed until June 30, 2030. It is not going anywhere, and neither are you.

### After You Pass: From CSCE to Call Sign

Here is the hard rule of this entire chapter, stated before everything else: do not transmit yet. Passing the exam feels like the finish line, but legally it is not. Your CSCE is proof you passed — it is not operating authority. Your operating authority begins only when your license grant appears in the FCC's ULS, the Universal Licensing System database.

Now the sequence, which moves fast. The VEs submit your application to the coordinating VEC — never directly to the FCC — and the VEC screens the information, resolves any discrepancies, and forwards everything to the FCC electronically.

Then comes the one fee left in the process, and nobody collects it at the session: VECs and VE teams must not collect the FCC application fee, because you pay it online directly to the FCC through the CORES payment system. The FCC charges a $35 application fee on applications for a new license.

In the FCC process as described by ARRL, when the FCC receives your application from the VEC it emails you a payment-instructions link, and you have 10 calendar days from the issuance of your application file number to pay — or you can pay even sooner by looking up your pending application by FRN in the FCC application search. Watch your inbox and your spam folder for that email. If the fee is not paid inside the 10-day window, the FCC dismisses the application — but you do not have to retest. The VEC can refile the application at any time before your CSCE expires.

After you pay, the wait is short.

**FACT:** Your license grant typically appears in the FCC's ULS database the next business day after you pay the application fee — and you may not transmit until it appears there.

Typically is the honest word — it is a typical timing, never a promise — so allow a few days to about two weeks, and look your name up in the ULS before you press the transmit button. The moment your grant appears there, with your brand-new call sign attached, you are a licensed radio amateur.

Two more things arrive in that same stretch. The FCC emails a link to your official electronic license — no paper license comes in the mail — and per the FCC process as described by ARRL, the download link in that email is valid for 30 days, so download the file and save it somewhere safe. And your CSCE is not disposable either.

**FACT:** A CSCE is valid for 365 days from its issue date for the element credit it conveys, and no subsequently issued CSCE renews another CSCE's validity period.

Keep yours safe. It is your record that you passed Element 2, and element credit matters when you upgrade. One teaser for later: when you eventually pass a higher-class exam, a different rule lets you use your new privileges immediately, right at the session — but that is a story for the General course.

### Your First Radio

Now the fun purchase. The advice here stays at the category level — no brands, no prices, no "best radio" — because the right choice depends on your local radio life, and any judgment beyond that would be a guess dressed up as a recommendation.

The classic first radio is a VHF/UHF FM handheld, universally called an HT. It is cheap, simple, battery-powered, and it works the local repeaters you learned about in chapter 6 — which is where most everyday conversation in most places actually happens. Before you buy, find out which bands your local repeaters use: 2 meters and 70 centimeters cover most repeaters in most areas, and a dual-band HT covers both. Your club or the local repeater listing will tell you in one email.

One honest warning from chapter 4: the stock "rubber duck" antenna that comes with an HT is convenient but inefficient — a flexible compromise antenna, not a good one. The highest-value upgrade you can buy is not more power; it is a better antenna. A decent mobile-mount antenna on a car roof, or a small outdoor antenna at home, will transform what an inexpensive HT can do. Antenna before power, every time.

Later you might add a mobile rig — a higher-powered radio designed for the car — for commuting and stronger repeater work, and eventually a base station at home. But start small, learn what you actually use, and let your operating habits guide the second purchase. As for where to buy: new from any amateur radio dealer, used from hamfests and club members (used HTs are a time-honored first radio), or — best of all — a club loaner, because many clubs lend starter radios to brand-new members precisely so you can get on the air before spending anything.

### Your First Contacts

Your grant is in the ULS, the HT is charged, and now comes the part nobody warns you about: the first time you press the transmit button, your thumb will hesitate. Everyone's does. The cure is preparation.

Start by programming your local repeaters into the radio. From chapter 6 you know the recipe: the repeater's output frequency (the one you listen on), the offset direction and amount, and the CTCSS access tone, programmed as a frequency such as 100.0 Hz. Most radios also store these as named memory channels, so "club repeater" becomes one button. Test with a listen first — if you can hear the repeater's activity, your receive side is right.

For the first transmission, keep it small. A repeater contact is one short sentence: the other station's call sign first, then your own. Or announce yourself by saying your call sign followed by "listening" — that is the repeater way, and someone may well answer. If you would rather start even quieter, 146.520 MHz is the national calling frequency for 2-meter FM simplex, radio-to-radio with no repeater in the middle, and it exists for exactly this kind of first contact.

Then do the single best thing a new ham can do: check into a local net. Nets — the scheduled on-air meetings from chapter 6 — are structured, welcoming, and run by a net control station whose whole job is to take check-ins in order. You say your call sign when invited, net control acknowledges you, and just like that you have operated on the air correctly in front of an audience that was happy to have you. Most areas have several nets a week: club nets, emergency-preparedness nets, swap nets. Ask at the club meeting which ones welcome new check-ins — the honest answer is almost always all of them. First-contact nerves do not survive a good net.

### Your 30-Day Plan

Here is a realistic first month on the air, one gentle week at a time.

**Week 1 — Get on the air.** Program your local repeaters into the radio — offset and tone, per chapter 6. Listen to a full net without transmitting, just to hear the rhythm. Make your first simplex contact on 146.520, even if it is with a club friend standing across the room.

**Week 2 — Join the community.** Check into a local net using the manners from chapter 6 — say your call sign when invited, follow net control's lead. Introduce yourself at a club meeting, in person or online; tell them you are brand new, because clubs love brand new.

**Week 3 — Try a digital mode.** Decode some FT8 signals or watch APRS stations move across a map, per chapter 7 — receiving first, transmitting later. Notice how much of amateur radio is computers and radios shaking hands.

**Week 4 — Build the station properly.** Set up your station the chapter 5 way, check your SWR, and review the RF exposure picture for your actual setup per chapter 9. Then open the General book and start reading — because week 5 is where the world gets bigger.

### What's Next: General and Beyond

Technician is a genuinely big playground — all amateur frequencies above 30 MHz, plus a taste of HF. General opens the rest of HF: most of the shortwave bands where contacts across continents become ordinary, and it is the next book in this series. The General exam is Element 3, another 35-question test drawn from a public pool you can study exactly the way you studied this one — and you already have Element 2 credit, so you never repeat what you have passed. Beyond that sits Amateur Extra, Elements 2, 3, and 4, conveying all available US amateur privileges. There is no rush and no expiration on your ambition: your Technician license is normally granted for a 10-year term, and the hobby will wait for you. But if the HF bug bites — and it usually bites — you know exactly where to go.

### Key Takeaways

- Bring one legal photo ID (or two forms of non-photo ID), your FRN, and the session fee — and check with the VE team for the exact amount.

- Every exam question is one you have seen: drill Appendix A and timed practice exams until you pass comfortably at home.

- 26 of 35 passes — you can miss nine questions and still walk out with your CSCE.

- A same-day retake after a failed element is entirely the VE team's call, and it is always a different version of the exam.

- No transmitting until your license grant appears in the FCC's ULS database — the CSCE proves you passed, but it is not operating authority.

- Pay the FCC application fee within the 10-day window from your application file number; if the window lapses, the VEC can refile and you do not retest.

- Buy for your local repeaters — an HT on the bands your area actually uses — and upgrade the antenna before the power.
