### Beat 1 — Repeater anatomy: input, output, offset
- A repeater simultaneously retransmits your signal on a different channel to extend range. Jargon: the repeater is "the machine"; it listens on its **input** (where you transmit) and transmits on its **output** (where you listen).
- Offset = the difference between the repeater's transmit and receive frequencies (T2A07). **A common** offset is ±600 kHz on 2 m (T2A01), ±5 MHz on 70 cm (T2A03) — see the wording law in §8: never "the offset." Modern radios auto-shift; odd splits exist near band edges.
- Where beginners find machines: club websites and online repeater directories (RepeaterBook is the de facto one) list output frequency, offset direction, and tone per repeater (practice color, r5 §1).
- Figure: `{{fig:ch06-repeater-offset}}`.
- Vocabulary: repeater, input, output, offset, odd split.

### Beat 2 — Simplex and the calling frequency
- Simplex = transmitting and receiving on the same frequency (T2A11); contrast with duplex repeater operation. Figure: `{{fig:ch06-simplex-duplex}}`.
- 146.520 MHz is the national calling frequency for FM simplex on 2 m (T2A02).
- Band plans designate simplex channels so nearby stations can talk without tying up a repeater (T2B09); a band plan is **a voluntary guideline** for modes and activities within a band — expected community behavior, never an FCC mandate (T2A10; wording law §8).
- Vocabulary: simplex, duplex, band plan, national calling frequency.

### Beat 3 — Tones, codes, and access
- CTCSS = a sub-audible tone sent with your voice audio that opens the receiver's squelch (T2B02); DCS is the digital-bitstream equivalent. If you hear a repeater's output but can't bring it up: wrong offset, wrong CTCSS tone, or wrong DCS code — all three (T2B04).
- **CTCSS/DCS is access control and selective calling, NOT privacy** (wording law §8) — any receiver in carrier squelch hears the whole conversation; "PL" is just Motorola's 1951 trade name. Program tones by frequency (e.g., 100.0 Hz), never by radio "tone number."
- Squelch mutes receiver audio when no signal is present (T2B13). The reverse function listens on the repeater's input so you can hear the other station directly (T2B01).
- DTMF = two simultaneous audio tones per keypress — how you drive repeater features such as autopatch and IRLP (T2B06).
- Overdeviation: talking too loudly into an FM mic makes audio drop out on voice peaks — back off the mic (T2B05).
- Linked repeater networks: a signal received by one repeater is retransmitted by all of them (T2B03).
- DMR: a color code is an access code programmed into the radio to reach a specific repeater (T2B12) — **state this pool definition first**; the "DMR's analog of a CTCSS tone" analogy may follow only as orientation (§7.6). A talkgroup is an identifier that organizes traffic so listeners hear only their group, joined by programming the group's ID (T2B14, T2B07).
- Courtesy color (r5 §1): repeaters ID themselves roughly every 10 minutes; the "courtesy tone" beep means leave a beat so anyone can break in; **kerchunking** — keying without identifying — is rude and an unidentified transmission.
- Figure: `{{fig:ch06-ctcss-gate}}`.
- Vocabulary: CTCSS, DCS, DTMF, squelch, deviation/overdeviation, reverse function, linked repeater network, color code, talkgroup, kerchunking.

### Beat 4 — Calling and conversation manners
- Call a specific station (on a repeater or answering a CQ): the other station's call sign first, then yours (T2A04, T2A05) — called station first, always.
- CQ on simplex/HF phone: repeat "CQ" a few times, "this is" + your call sign, pause to listen, repeat (T2A06); CQ means "calling any station" (T2A08).
- On a repeater, indicate you're monitoring with your call sign + "listening" (T2A09). **CQ is for simplex and HF, not repeaters** — say it plainly (§7.6).
- Nobody owns a frequency: two stations that keep interfering should negotiate continued shared use (T2B08) — "we were here first" is not a rule.
- Q-signals as vocabulary to recognize: QRM = interference from other stations (T2B10); QSY = changing frequency (T2B11); QRZ = "Who is calling me?"; QTH = "My location is ___" (meanings tested, usage is plain words on FM — §7.6).
- Vocabulary: CQ, monitoring/listening, Q signals (QRM, QSY, QRZ, QTH).

### Beat 5 — Nets and traffic
- In a directed net, the Net Control Station (NCS) calls the net to order and directs communications; unless you are reporting an emergency, transmit only when directed by the NCS (T2C02, T2C07). Check-in technique color from r5 §2 (short check-ins; "<call sign>, no traffic"). Figure: `{{fig:ch06-net-flow}}`.
- "Traffic" = formal written messages exchanged by net stations (T2C05). A radiogram's preamble is the block of information needed to track the message (T2C10); "check" = the number of words or word equivalents in the text portion (T2C11).
- Unusual words are spelled with the standard phonetic alphabet (T2C03). Pin the 26 ITU words with the ITU spellings **Alfa** and **Juliett** (§7.6 — spelling variants are sidebar folklore, never doctrine).
- Winlink relays messages using email addresses based on amateur call signs (T2C08).
- Vocabulary: net, net control station (NCS), traffic, radiogram, preamble, check, phonetic alphabet, Winlink.

### Beat 6 — Public service and emergencies
- FCC rules always apply — RACES, ARES, FEMA operations included (T2C01).
- ARES = licensed amateurs who voluntarily registered qualifications and equipment for public-service duty — an ARRL body anyone can join and drill with (T2C06). RACES = the FCC Part 97 civil-defense service, operating when activated, requiring certification by a civil defense agency (T2C04, T2C12). **Keep the pool's clean split** (§7.6).
- Operating outside your license-class privileges is permitted only in situations involving immediate safety of human life or protection of property (T2C09) — framed as essentially never happening in a Technician's career; emcomm drills never suspend Part 97.
- Vocabulary: ARES, RACES, emergency communications.

## 8. Tone & integrity notes — this chapter's wording laws (canon §7.5/§7.6, all binding)

- **"A common offset," never "the offset"** — odd splits and opposite-direction offsets exist near band edges; never write "the offset is 600 kHz."
- **CTCSS/DCS = access control and selective calling, not privacy** — "PL" folklore explained once; every carrier-squelch receiver hears the traffic. Program tones by frequency (e.g., 100.0 Hz), never by "tone number."
- **Band plans are voluntary community practice** that the rules encourage — never FCC mandates (T2A10's own wording: "a voluntary guideline").
- **CQ is for simplex and HF, not repeaters** — say it plainly.
- **Phonetics:** pin the 26 ITU words with spellings "Alfa"/"Juliett"; "ALPHA"/"Juliet" variants are sidebar folklore at most, never doctrine.
- **Q-signals:** teach meanings (QRM, QSY, QRZ, QTH); on FM voice plain words are the custom.
- **DMR color code:** pool definition first (an access code programmed into the radio to reach a specific repeater); the CTCSS analogy is orientation only.
- **RACES vs ARES:** keep the pool's clean split (ARES = ARRL volunteers, anyone can join; RACES = government-certified civil defense, operates when activated).
- **Emergency operation outside privileges (T2C09):** essentially never in a Tech's career; drills never suspend Part 97.
- **Deliberate exclusions (canon §7.6):** no local-repeater anecdotes, no quantitative "most repeaters" claims, no full CTCSS tone-frequency tables beyond the SO-50 tones (which belong to ch07).
- FM signal-report jargon ("full quieting," "you're scratchy") is jargon color, distinct from the RST system (ch07/r5 §6); the pool tests neither's vocabulary.
- No fabricated quotations; illustrative scenarios (e.g., a first repeater contact script) are plainly framed as illustrations, never attributed to real people. Banned phrases: "little did they know", "in that moment", "a testament to".
