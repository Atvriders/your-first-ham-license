### Beat 1 — Antennas and wavelength: why size follows frequency
- The antenna's job: turn the feed line's RF into a radio wave (and back). Resonant length scales with wavelength — refresher of λ = 300/f.
- A half-wave dipole for 50 MHz is ≈ 3 m; a 19-inch vertical is a resonant quarter-wave on 2 meters (T9A08 — the pool's only antenna-length number; see §5 for the math).
- Length↔frequency runs inverse: shortening a dipole **raises** its resonant frequency (T9A05) — longer antenna, lower frequency.
- Figures: `{{fig:ch04-dipole}}`, `{{fig:ch04-vertical-groundplane}}`.
- Vocabulary: antenna, resonant frequency, half-wave dipole, quarter-wave vertical.

### Beat 2 — Antenna types and what "gain" really means
- A beam antenna concentrates signals in one direction (T9A01); of the antennas the pool lists, a Yagi offers the greatest gain (T9A06). Gain = the increase in signal strength **in a specified direction** compared to a reference antenna (T9A11) — focusing, like a flashlight reflector; no extra power is created.
- A half-wave dipole radiates strongest broadside, weakest off its ends (T9A10) — beginners picture it backwards. Figure: `{{fig:ch04-dipole-pattern}}`.
- Loading = electrically lengthening an antenna by inserting inductors (coils) in the radiating elements — how short mobile whips act longer (T9A02).
- Polarization is described by the orientation of the electric field (T9A03) — vertical whip, vertical polarization (bridge to ch03's cross-pol lesson).
- HT realities: the rubber duck has low efficiency vs a full-size quarter-wave (T9A04); inside a car, the metal shell shields and weakens the signal — use an external antenna (T9A07). A 5/8-wave whip has more gain than a quarter-wave whip for VHF/UHF mobile (T9A09).
- Vocabulary: beam antenna, Yagi, antenna gain, reference antenna, loading / loading coil, polarization, broadside, rubber-duck antenna, 5/8-wave whip, efficiency.

### Beat 3 — Feed lines: coax and loss
- Coaxial cable is the amateur standard: 50 ohms characteristic impedance (T9B02); it dominates because it is easy to use and needs few special installation considerations — not because it is lowest-loss or cheapest (T9B03).
- Coax loss increases as frequency increases (T9B05) — why UHF runs need better or shorter cable. RG-213 has less loss than RG-58 (thicker) (T9B10); air-insulated hardline has the lowest loss of the listed feed lines (T9B11).
- Loss sources: water in connectors, high SWR, multiple connectors — all (T9B08).
- Vocabulary: feed line, coaxial cable, characteristic impedance, 50 ohms, dielectric, hardline.

### Beat 4 — SWR and the antenna tuner
- SWR (standing wave ratio) measures how well a load is matched to a transmission line (T9B12); 1:1 is the perfect match (reading details are ch05's T7C job — here just the meaning).
- Erratic SWR changes point to a loose connection in the antenna or feed line (T9B09).
- An antenna tuner (coupler) matches the antenna system impedance to the transceiver's 50-ohm output (T9B04) — tie back to pool Figure T-3 (the T-network) taught in ch01. Figure: `{{fig:ch04-swr-curve}}`.
- Vocabulary: SWR, impedance match, antenna tuner (coupler/transmatch).

### Beat 5 — Connectors
- PL-259 ("UHF" plug): standard at HF and VHF but not watertight (T9B07); Type N is the right choice above 400 MHz (T9B06) — the pool's deliberate pair: the "UHF connector" is *not* the best UHF choice.
- Any outdoor connector should be carefully taped against weather (T9B01 — "all these choices").
- Figure: `{{fig:ch04-connectors}}`.
- Vocabulary: PL-259, SO-239, BNC, Type N connector, waterproofing/taping.

## 5. Worked examples

- Quarter-wave on 2 meters (the pool's own numbers, T9A08): "λ = 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.05 m. A quarter-wave whip is 2.05 ÷ 4 ≈ 0.51 m ≈ 51 cm ≈ 20 inches — matching the **19-inch** whip (real antennas run about 5% short). So 'resonant quarter-wave' is the answer; a half-wave would need about a meter."
- Dipole scaling (sanity for T9A05): "A dipole cut for 50 MHz is λ/2 = (300 ÷ 50) ÷ 2 = **3 m** long. To raise the resonance to 100 MHz you must **shorten** it to 1.5 m — shorter antenna, higher frequency."
