Condensed from `canon/research/r4-teaching-t6-t0.md` (T0). Per r4's structure note: T0A+T0B are electrical/physical safety, T0C is RF exposure — keep them in separate sections. Serious but not scary: habits, not fear.

### Beat 1 — Electrical safety at the home station (T0A)
- A 12-volt battery will not shock you, but shorting its terminals can cause burns, fire, or an explosion — the danger is the huge current, not the voltage (T0A01).
- Rapidly charging or discharging an unprotected battery risks **overheating or out-gassing** — charge batteries in a ventilated space (T0A10 — quote the *revised* wording, see §8).
- Current through the human body can heat tissue, disrupt cells' electrical function, and cause involuntary muscle contractions — all three (T0A02).
- US three-wire 120 V AC color code: **black = hot**, white = neutral, green = ground (T0A03 tests black; teach all three for life safety).
- Fuses: a fuse removes power in case of an overload (T0A04); never replace a fuse with a larger one — excessive current could start a fire (T0A05); the fuse or breaker goes in series with the hot conductor only (T0A08). (Fuses protect against overload/fire; grounding protects against shock — different jobs.)
- Shock-protection habits: three-wire cords and plugs, all station equipment on a common safety ground, and fully discharging high-voltage capacitors before working inside gear — all (T0A06). A power supply can still kill immediately after switch-off because of the charge stored in its filter capacitors (T0A11).
- Measuring high voltage: the voltmeter and its leads must be rated for the voltage being measured (T0A12).
- Vocabulary: hot/neutral/ground conductors, fuse, circuit breaker, overload, safety ground, filter capacitor, out-gassing.

### Beat 2 — Grounding and lightning protection (T0A second half)
- The lightning arrester goes on a grounded panel near where feed lines enter the building (T0A07).
- All external ground rods must be bonded together with heavy wire or strap (T0A09) — separate, unbonded grounds create dangerous voltage differences.
- Figure: `{{fig:ch09-grounding}}`.
- Vocabulary: bonding, ground rod, lightning arrester.

### Beat 3 — Towers and antenna installation (T0B)
- Tower grounding: connections short and direct (T0B01); separate eight-foot ground rods for each tower leg, bonded to the tower and to each other (T0B08); grounding conductors avoid sharp bends — lightning wants a smooth path (T0B10). Grounding requirements come from **local electrical codes**, not the FCC (T0B11).
- Climbing rules: get training, wear an approved harness, tie off at all times (T0B02); never climb without a helper or observer (T0B03); on a crank-up tower, climb only when it is retracted or mechanical safety locks are installed (T0B07).
- Power lines: before raising anything, look for and stay clear of overhead wires (T0B04); position the antenna so that if it falls, no part can come within **10 feet** of power lines (T0B06 — the rule is about a fallen antenna's worst case, not a fixed horizontal distance); never attach an antenna to a utility pole (T0B09).
- Guy hardware: the safety wire through a turnbuckle keeps vibration from loosening it (T0B05).
- Vocabulary: climbing harness, tie-off, crank-up tower, guy line, turnbuckle, safety wire, utility pole.

### Beat 4 — RF exposure (T0C)
- Radio signals are **non-ionizing radiation**: RF photons do not have enough energy to cause chemical changes in cells or damage DNA, unlike X-rays (T0C01, T0C12). The RF hazard is heating (and shocks or burns), not mutation — but non-ionizing ≠ harmless: "perfectly safe" is wrong.
- Exposure limits (MPE) vary with frequency because the human body absorbs more RF energy at some frequencies than others (T0C05); of the bands the pool lists, **50 MHz (6 meters) has the lowest MPE** (T0C02) — the most-missed fact in T0C; defies "higher frequency = stricter" intuition.
- Duty cycle = the percentage of time the transmitter is transmitting during the averaging time (T0C11); exposure limits are about **average** exposure (T0C10); dropping from 100% to 50% duty cycle **doubles** the allowable power density (T0C03 — the chapter's math; see §5).
- Exposure depends on frequency, power level, distance from the antenna, and the antenna's radiation pattern — all four (T0C04); the cheapest way to reduce exposure is to relocate antennas away from people (T0C08 — note: *increasing* duty cycle increases exposure, that distractor is backwards). Touching an antenna while it is transmitting causes an RF burn to the skin (T0C07).
- Compliance is the station licensee's responsibility (T0C13); acceptable evaluation methods are calculation per FCC OET Bulletin 65, computer modeling, or measurement with calibrated field-strength equipment (T0C06); re-evaluate whenever anything in the transmitter or antenna system changes (T0C09).
- Figures: `{{fig:ch09-rf-exposure}}`, `{{fig:ch09-duty-cycle}}`.
- Vocabulary: non-ionizing vs ionizing radiation, MPE, power density, duty cycle, averaging time, RF burn, RF exposure evaluation, OET Bulletin 65.

## 5. Worked examples

- Duty cycle (the pool's own numbers, T0C03): "Duty cycle drops from 100% to 50% — you're transmitting half as much in each averaging window, so the allowable power density **increases by a factor of 2** (not 50%, not 3× — those are the distractors)."
- Practice the definition (T0C11 in numbers, from r4): "You transmit 30 seconds out of every 5 minutes: duty cycle = 30 ÷ 300 = **10%** — one tenth the average exposure of continuous transmitting."
