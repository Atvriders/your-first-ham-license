## 9. Safety

Picture the day your first station comes together: a radio on the desk, a battery or power supply under it, an antenna waiting to go up outside, and you reaching for the microphone. Before anything gets plugged in, bolted down, or climbed, this chapter walks the ground rules that keep the hobby what it should be — a safe one. Ham radio is a genuinely safe hobby when you do these things, and the exam holds you to them: subelement T0 carries 36 pool questions in three groups — T0A on power, wiring, and batteries, T0B on towers and antennas, T0C on RF exposure — and your 35-question exam draws 3 of them, one per group. In this chapter you'll learn why a 12-volt battery cannot shock you but can still burn a house down, what the three colors in a power cord mean, where fuses and lightning arresters belong, how to ground a station and a tower, the rules of climbing and of power lines, and how the FCC's RF exposure limits work — including the one piece of arithmetic the safety subelement asks you to do.

### Electrical safety at the home station

Start with the fact that surprises everyone. A 12-volt battery will not shock you. Twelve volts cannot push a dangerous current through the resistance of your skin — chapter 1's Ohm's law, I = V / R, is the reason: with your body's resistance in the thousands of ohms, 12 volts drives a current far too small to feel. You can touch both terminals with dry hands and nothing happens. The exam's hazard lives somewhere else entirely: shorting the terminals can cause burns, fire, or an explosion (T0A01). A storage battery has almost no internal resistance, so a metal watchband, a dropped wrench, or a careless screwdriver laid across the terminals becomes a short circuit carrying enormous current — enough to heat metal red-hot in seconds, spatter electrolyte, or ignite gas near the battery. The danger is the huge current, not the voltage. A battery that can start a car can melt a wrench, and the habits that follow are simple: keep jewelry and loose metal tools away from the terminals, cover the terminals when you carry or store a battery, and make connections deliberately, not with a flop of alligator clips.

There is a second battery question, and it exists only in its February 19, 2026 revised form: what hazard exists when rapidly charging or discharging an unprotected battery? The keyed answer is **overheating or out-gassing** (T0A10). Out-gassing means the battery vents gas — hydrogen, in the familiar lead-acid kind — and hydrogen plus a spark is a fire looking for a room. Pushing a battery hard in either direction also heats it, which shortens its life and, in a lithium pack that lacks protection circuitry, can end in flames. So the exam-proof habit is one sentence: charge batteries in a ventilated space, at a sensible rate, with the charger made for that chemistry.

Now the human side of the circuit. Current flowing through the body is dangerous in three ways at once, and the exam wants all three: it can injure by heating body tissue, it can disrupt the electrical functions of cells, and it can cause involuntary muscle contractions (T0A02). That third one is the electrician's nightmare — the muscles in your hand clamp shut on the very thing that is shocking you, and you cannot let go. It is why experienced technicians work around live circuits with one hand kept in a pocket, so a mistake cannot send current across the chest, and why "it's only 120 volts" is not a reassurance. Respect every conductor that comes out of the wall.

So learn the wall's color code, because the exam tests it and life uses it. In a US three-wire 120 V AC cable, **black is the hot conductor**, white is neutral, and green is the equipment ground (T0A03 tests black; the other two are yours for free). The hot wire is the one that bites; neutral carries the working current back; the ground wire is the silent safety path that should never carry working current at all. If you memorize one color, memorize black — and never assume the person who wired an outlet before you got it right. A two-dollar outlet tester is a fine first accessory for a new shack.

**FACT:** In US three-wire 120 V AC cable, black insulation indicates the hot conductor (white is neutral, green is ground).

Protection devices come next. A **fuse** is a deliberate weak link — a thin conductor sized to melt and remove power in case of an **overload** (T0A04), a fault drawing more current than the circuit was built for. A **circuit breaker** is the same idea in a resettable switch. Notice what the fuse does not do, because it is T0A04's own distractor: a fuse does not limit current to prevent shocks. It passes whatever flows, right up to its rating — and its rating, chosen to protect the wiring, is hundreds of times the current that can stop a heart. Fuses fight fire; they do not babysit people.

Two more fuse rules carry two more exam questions. First, never replace a fuse with a larger one. Swap a 5-ampere fuse for a 20-ampere fuse and you have promoted the wiring itself into the fuse's job — excessive current could cause a fire (T0A05). The fuse was sized to the wire, not to your convenience, and no workaround — a bigger fuse, a penny, a strip of foil — is ever acceptable. Second, the fuse or circuit breaker goes in series with the hot conductor only (T0A08). Interrupting the black wire kills power to everything downstream; a fuse in the neutral would leave the equipment's insides sitting at full line voltage even after the fuse blows, waiting for someone to open the case. Keep the division of labor straight, because the distractors blur it: fuses and breakers protect against overload and fire, while grounding protects against shock. Neither does the other's job.

**FACT:** A fuse removes power in case of an overload, you must never replace a fuse with a larger one (excessive current could start a fire), and the fuse or breaker goes in series with the hot conductor only.

Shock protection at the station is three habits, and the keyed answer is all of them (T0A06). Use three-wire cords and plugs for all AC-powered equipment — never saw off the round ground pin to force a three-prong plug into a two-slot outlet, because that pin is the safety ground doing its job. Connect all AC-powered station equipment to a common **safety ground**, so every chassis sits at the same voltage; a fault then trips the breaker instead of energizing a microphone shell and waiting for your lip. And ensure all capacitors used for high-voltage DC are fully discharged before working inside equipment.

That third habit exists because of a trap hiding inside every power supply. Chapter 1 introduced the capacitor as the component that stores energy in an electric field, and the big **filter capacitors** that smooth a supply's DC output do their job so well that they keep doing it after the switch clicks off. A power supply can still be lethal immediately after it is turned off, because of the charge stored in its filter capacitors (T0A11). The discipline is: unplug, wait, discharge the capacitors through a proper resistor — never a screwdriver, which just makes a spark and a welded tip — and then verify with a meter before your fingers go anywhere near the circuit.

Which brings up the meter itself, the last electrical question in the group. When you measure high voltage, the voltmeter and its leads must be rated for use at the voltages being measured (T0A12). Meters carry printed voltage and category ratings, and test leads have ratings of their own — the probe tips, the insulation, the finger guards all matter. Inspect the leads for cracks, set the meter to volts before you connect it, and keep one hand in your pocket. The tool that measures the hazard has to be built for the hazard.

### Grounding and lightning protection

A ham station talks to the earth in two dialects, and keeping them apart in your head makes every grounding question easier. The safety ground inside your wiring exists so that if a hot wire ever touches a chassis, the fault current rushes to earth and trips the breaker — instead of waiting in the metal for your hand. The lightning ground outside exists to give a strike, or the near-miss surge from one, a short and easy path into the soil that does not run through your radio, your house wiring, or you. This chapter is about the second kind, and its questions are all about where things go and how they connect.

The centerpiece is the **lightning arrester**, and the exam wants its address: on a grounded panel near where feed lines enter the building (T0A07). Not at the transceiver's output connector, not up at the antenna's feed point, and not at the AC service panel. Every coax feed line coming in from an antenna passes through an arrester right at the wall, mounted on a panel that is itself grounded, so a surge riding the feed line meets a path to earth before it meets your shack.

Then comes the rule that ties the whole yard together: all external **ground rods** or earth connections must be **bonded** together with heavy wire or conductive strap (T0A09). Bonding just means connecting, deliberately and solidly. The reason is voltage difference. During a lightning strike or a power fault, the soil around one rod can sit at a very different voltage from the soil around another, and two unbonded rods hand that difference a path straight through your equipment — in one ground, out the other. Bond every rod — the one at the entry panel, the one at the tower base, the power company's — into a single system, and the whole station rises and falls together at one potential instead of tearing itself apart between two.

{{fig:ch09-grounding}}

One habit closes the loop: lightning protection is something you install before the forecast, not during it. When thunderstorms are nearby, the wise station is a quiet one — off the air, with nothing inviting the sky inside. The ground system is the station's insurance policy; not operating through the storm is the operator's.

### Towers and antenna work

A tower concentrates every physical hazard in this chapter into one structure — height, weight, electricity, weather — so it earns the most rules. The encouraging part is that the exam's tower questions are all habits, not heroics, and most of them happen with both feet on the ground.

Start with grounding the tower itself, because three questions live there. Ground connections for lightning protection should be short and direct (T0B01) — a long, looping ground lead adds inductance and gives a fast surge a reason to detour through the shack instead. You will see drip loops where cables enter a building, but those fight rainwater, not lightning; they are T0B01's distractor, not its answer. The proper grounding method for a tower is separate eight-foot ground rods for each tower leg, bonded to the tower and to each other (T0B08) — not a single four-foot rod, not a cold-water pipe, not a ferrite choke. And the grounding conductors themselves must avoid sharp bends (T0B10). Lightning is a fast, high-frequency event that wants a smooth path, and a sharp corner is exactly where it can leave the wire for somewhere you would rather it not go. Right-angle bends are the wrong answer twice over in this group; smooth, gentle curves are the right one. And who writes these rules? Not the FCC — grounding requirements for an amateur radio tower or antenna come from local electrical codes (T0B11). Part 97 governs your signal; your city or county's electrical code governs your ground system.

Now the climbing rules, which are short enough to memorize and absolute enough to never bend. What is required when climbing an antenna tower? Sufficient training on safe tower-climbing techniques, appropriate tie-off to the tower at all times, and always wearing an approved climbing **harness** — all three (T0B02). Training first: learn from a club's experienced tower crew or a proper course before your boots leave the ground. The harness is a full-body, approved one, and **tie-off** means you are clipped to the structure at all times — a harness you never clip in is a costume. Add the unglamorous gear around those three: a hard hat, no loose tools in pockets (anything dropped is a hazard to everyone below), and a plan for the job made before the climb. Under what circumstances is it safe to climb without a helper or observer? Never (T0B03) — not for a quick job, not below 20 feet, not when the work is only mechanical. The person on the ground keeps others clear, hoists tools on a rope, and is the one who calls for help if anything goes wrong. One tower type gets its own question: a **crank-up tower**, whose nested sections telescope up and down on a cable, must not be climbed unless it is retracted, or mechanical safety locking devices have been installed (T0B07). Climbing a raised crank-up means trusting your life to the one cable whose failure mode is dropping a section past where you are standing.

Before anything goes up at all, look up. The important safety precaution when putting up an antenna tower is to look for and stay clear of any overhead electrical wires (T0B04). Treat every overhead wire as energized, because you cannot tell a power line from a phone line at 40 feet — and plan the whole job, ladders and masts and long aluminum sections included, around staying clear of them. Then apply the exam's most famous ruler: position the antenna so that if it falls, no part of it can come within 10 feet of the power wires (T0B06). Read that carefully, because every distractor misreads it. The rule is not a fixed horizontal distance, not the height of the line above ground, not a formula with a 1.5 factor, and certainly not a half-wavelength at your operating frequency. It is a worst-case test: tip the entire antenna system over in your imagination, in any direction, and nowhere in its fall may it reach within 10 feet of energized wire. If the geometry fails that test, the antenna goes somewhere else. And one structure is simply off limits: never attach an antenna to a **utility pole**, because the antenna could contact high-voltage power lines (T0B09). The pole belongs to the power company, it carries the very wires you are sworn to avoid, and climbing it is not yours to do.

**FACT:** Before raising an antenna, look for and stay clear of overhead wires, position the antenna so that if it falls no part of it can come within 10 feet of power lines, and never attach an antenna to a utility pole.

The last tower question is small hardware with a long memory. A guyed tower stands because of its **guy lines**, and each guy's tension is set by a **turnbuckle** — a threaded barrel you twist to pull the line taut. Wind makes towers hum, and vibration slowly walks threaded hardware loose, so a **safety wire** is threaded through the turnbuckle's body and eyes after tensioning. Its purpose is to prevent loosening of the turnbuckle from vibration (T0B05) — not to hold the guy if the turnbuckle breaks, and not to carry lightning. Give guys and safety wires a slow binocular inspection from the ground a couple of times a year, and the tower will keep its promises.

### RF exposure: energy, limits, and duty cycle

The last group in the safety subelement is about the transmitter's output itself — the invisible part of the hobby — and it begins by calming the scariest word in the chapter. Radio signals are **non-ionizing radiation** (T0C01). **Ionizing** radiation — X-rays, gamma rays, radioactivity — carries photons energetic enough to knock electrons loose, break chemical bonds, and damage DNA. RF photons do not have sufficient energy to cause chemical changes in cells and damage DNA (T0C12); radio waves sit far, far below that threshold on the spectrum of chapter 2. The RF hazard is heating — the same physics a microwave oven uses, at a tiny fraction of the intensity — plus shocks and burns right at the antenna. Two wrong ideas bracket the truth, and the exam tests both edges: RF is not ionizing, and it is also not "perfectly safe," which is T0C12's own distractor. Real RF at real power at real proximity can hurt you; it just cannot mutate you.

**FACT:** Radio signals are non-ionizing radiation — RF photons do not have enough energy to cause chemical changes in cells or damage DNA, unlike X-rays; the RF hazard is heating (and shocks or burns), not mutation.

The FCC's exposure limits go by **MPE** — maximum permissible exposure — and they are not one flat number. Exposure limits vary with frequency because the human body absorbs more RF energy at some frequencies than at others (T0C05). A body is a lossy conductor of a particular size, and RF energy couples into it most efficiently in the VHF range, so the limits are strictest where absorption is strongest. That produces the most-missed fact in the whole subelement, so pin it now: of the bands the pool lists, the lowest MPE belongs to **50 MHz — the 6-meter band** (T0C02). Every instinct says the highest frequency should carry the strictest limit; the exam's list runs the other way. When the choices are 3.5, 50, 440, and 1296 MHz, the lowest MPE sits on 50. Memorize it cold.

What actually sets the exposure near your station has four factors, and the keyed answer is all of them (T0C04): the frequency and power level of the RF field, the distance from the antenna to a person, and the antenna's radiation pattern — a beam concentrates energy in one direction, while a vertical spreads it around the compass. Distance is the friendliest factor, because **power density** — how much RF power flows through the patch of space where a person stands — falls off steeply as separation grows. That hands you the cheapest compliance fix in radio: relocate antennas away from people (T0C08). Note the shape of that question's trap — "increase the duty cycle" is offered as a fix, and it is backwards, because transmitting more of the time raises exposure rather than lowering it. And one direct-contact rule rounds out the hazards: touching an antenna during a transmission causes an **RF burn** to the skin (T0C07), a genuine burn where RF current enters the body. Never grab a live antenna, and never let anyone else stand within reach of one while you transmit.

{{fig:ch09-rf-exposure}}

The exposure limits are written against **average** exposure, not peak, and that is the door through which the chapter's only arithmetic walks in. **Duty cycle** is the percentage of time that a transmitter is transmitting during the **averaging time** for RF exposure (T0C11). It matters because it affects the average exposure to radiation (T0C10) — keying less does not lower the peak exposure at all; it lowers the average, and the average is what the limit measures. Key down solidly for a whole averaging window and your duty cycle is 100 percent; key five seconds on and five seconds off and it is 50 percent, with the same peak power and half the average exposure. Operating style moves the number around: a slow SSB conversation full of pauses is a lower duty cycle than a long keyed-down carrier, and a mode that transmits 15 seconds out of every 30 runs right at half.

> **Worked example:** Your operating changes so the duty cycle drops from 100 percent to 50 percent. You are now transmitting half as much energy in every averaging window, so the same average exposure permits twice the density: the allowable power density increases by a factor of 2 (T0C03). Not 50 percent, not a factor of 3 — those are the printed distractors — and "no adjustment allowed" is the other trap. The scaling continues in the same proportion: 25 percent duty allows four times, and 10 percent duty allows ten times the continuous limit.

**FACT:** If duty cycle changes from 100 percent to 50 percent, the allowable power density for RF safety increases by a factor of 2.

{{fig:ch09-duty-cycle}}

> **Worked example:** Now practice the definition the way the exam does. You transmit for 30 seconds out of every 5 minutes. Five minutes is 300 seconds, so duty cycle equals transmit time divided by total time: $30 / 300 = 0.1$, or 10 percent. Your average exposure is one tenth of what it would be keyed down continuously — and by the same scaling, the allowable power density is ten times the continuous limit.

Compliance has an owner, and the exam asks who: the station licensee is responsible for ensuring that no person is exposed to RF energy above the FCC exposure limits (T0C13). Not the FCC, not the local zoning board, not the people standing nearby — you, the licensee, the moment your call sign is on the grant. How do you check? All three printed methods are acceptable (T0C06): calculation based on FCC OET Bulletin 65, the FCC's own exposure-evaluation document; calculation based on computer modeling of your antenna, power, and frequencies; or measurement of field strength using calibrated equipment. For a typical Technician station — a handheld at a few watts, or a 50-watt mobile on a roof antenna — a few minutes with an online calculator built on OET Bulletin 65 usually settles the question, with distance doing most of the work in your favor. But you check; you do not assume. And the **RF exposure evaluation** is not one-and-done: you make sure your station stays in compliance by re-evaluating the station whenever an item in the transmitter or antenna system is changed (T0C09). A new antenna, a new amplifier, a new mounting spot, more power — any change reopens the question.

One exam-strategy note applies across the whole subelement, because T0 leans on the pattern hard. "All these choices are correct" is the keyed answer on five safety questions (T0A02, T0A06, T0B02, T0C04, T0C06) — and it is a deliberate trap on eight others (T0A01, T0A04, T0B01, T0B04, T0B07, T0B09, T0C07, T0C08). Never pick it on reflex. Read each option on its own merits; only when every listed option is genuinely true does "all these choices are correct" become your answer.

### Exam Focus

Subelement T0 — groups T0A, T0B, and T0C — holds 36 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 3 of them, one per group. Here are eight, quoted exactly as the pool prints them, spanning all three groups; T0C03 pairs with the duty-cycle figure and T0C13 with the RF-exposure figure above.

> **T0A03** In the United States, what circuit does black wire insulation indicate in a three-wire 120 V AC cable?
> A. Neutral
> B. Hot
> C. Equipment ground
> D. Negative
> **Answer: B** — black insulation marks the hot conductor; white is neutral and green is ground.

> **T0A05** Why should a 5-ampere fuse never be replaced with a 20-ampere fuse?
> A. The larger fuse would be likely to blow because it is rated for higher current
> B. The power supply ripple would greatly increase
> C. Excessive current could cause a fire
> D. Voltage drop in the higher current fuse could result in excessively low voltage to the device
> **Answer: C** — the fuse was sized to protect the wiring, and a larger one lets excessive current flow until something burns.

> **T0A10** What hazard exists when rapidly charging or discharging an unprotected battery?
> A. Overheating or out-gassing
> B. Excess output ripple
> C. Electric shock
> D. Overvoltage
> **Answer: A** — rapid charging or discharging risks overheating or out-gassing, so batteries charge in ventilated space. (Quoted in its revised 2026-02-19 form.)

> **T0B02** What is required when climbing an antenna tower?
> A. Have sufficient training on safe tower climbing techniques
> B. Use appropriate tie-off to the tower at all times
> C. Always wear an approved climbing harness
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: D** — training, tie-off at all times, and an approved harness are each required, so all these choices are correct.

> **T0B06** What is the minimum safe distance from a power line to allow when installing an antenna?
> A. Add the height of the antenna to the height of the power line and multiply by a factor of 1.5
> B. The height of the power line above ground
> C. 1/2 wavelength at the operating frequency
> D. Enough so that if the antenna falls, no part of it can come within 10 feet of the power wires
> **Answer: D** — the rule is the fallen-antenna worst case: if it comes down, no part may reach within 10 feet of the power wires.

> **T0C02** Which of the following bands has the lowest maximum permissible exposure for RF safety?
> A. 3.5 MHz
> B. 50 MHz
> C. 440 MHz
> D. 1296 MHz
> **Answer: B** — the body absorbs more RF at some frequencies than others, and of the bands listed, 50 MHz carries the strictest limit.

> **T0C03** How does the allowable power density for RF safety change if duty cycle changes from 100 percent to 50 percent?
> A. It increases by a factor of 3
> B. It decreases by 50 percent
> C. It increases by a factor of 2
> D. There is no adjustment allowed for lower duty cycle
> **Answer: C** — transmitting half the time halves the average exposure, so the allowable power density doubles: a factor of 2.

> **T0C13** Who is responsible for ensuring that no person is exposed to RF energy above the FCC exposure limits?
> A. The FCC
> B. The station licensee
> C. Anyone who is near an antenna
> D. The local zoning board
> **Answer: B** — the station licensee owns RF exposure compliance, evaluation and re-evaluation included.

### Key Takeaways

- A 12-volt battery will not shock you, but shorting its terminals can cause burns, fire, or an explosion — the danger is the huge current, not the voltage — and rapidly charging or discharging an unprotected battery risks overheating or out-gassing, so charge batteries in ventilated space.

- Current through the body heats tissue, disrupts cells' electrical function, and causes involuntary muscle contractions — all three; in US three-wire 120 V AC cable, black is hot, white is neutral, green is ground.

- A fuse removes power in case of an overload; never replace a fuse with a larger one, because excessive current could cause a fire; the fuse or breaker goes in series with the hot conductor only — fuses fight fire and grounding fights shock, and neither does the other's job.

- Guard against shock with three-wire cords and plugs, a common safety ground for all station equipment, and fully discharged high-voltage capacitors before working inside gear — filter capacitors can be lethal immediately after switch-off, and the voltmeter and its leads must be rated for the voltage you measure.

- The lightning arrester goes on a grounded panel near where feed lines enter the building; all external ground rods are bonded together with heavy wire or strap; tower grounds run short and direct with no sharp bends to separate eight-foot rods per leg, bonded to the tower and each other — and grounding requirements come from local electrical codes, not the FCC.

- Climbing requires training, tie-off at all times, and an approved harness, and it is never safe to climb without a helper or observer; a crank-up tower is climbed only retracted or with mechanical safety locks installed; look for and stay clear of overhead wires, keep the fallen-antenna worst case 10 feet clear of power lines, and never attach an antenna to a utility pole.

- Radio signals are non-ionizing radiation — the hazard is heating, not DNA damage, but "perfectly safe" is wrong; exposure limits vary with frequency because the body absorbs more RF at some frequencies than others, and 50 MHz has the lowest MPE of the bands the pool lists.

- Exposure limits are about average exposure: duty cycle is the percentage of time the transmitter is transmitting, and dropping from 100 percent to 50 percent doubles the allowable power density; the cheapest fix is relocating antennas away from people; and the station licensee is responsible — evaluate by OET Bulletin 65 calculation, computer modeling, or calibrated field-strength measurement, and re-evaluate whenever anything in the transmitter or antenna system changes.
