## 5. Your Station & Equipment

Your new handheld is charged, your call sign is fresh in the FCC database, and now you are staring at a shopping cart full of boxes: a power supply, a microphone, a meter of some kind, a coil of coax, an antenna. What plugs into what, and why? In this chapter you'll learn the anatomy of a complete station from wall outlet to antenna, how to power it safely, how to measure what your transmitter is actually doing, how a computer joins the station for digital modes, what every common control on the radio does, what the parts inside the radio are for, how to track down interference (including the kind your neighbors complain about), and how to use the basic test gear on a workbench. All of it maps to pool subelements T4 and T7 — 67 questions, 6 of them on your exam.

### Anatomy of a station

Every ham station, from a shirt-pocket handheld to a desk full of glowing dials, is the same handful of blocks connected in the same order. Learn the blocks once and every radio you ever meet will feel familiar. Figure 5.1 shows the basic lineup.

Start at the wall. Your house wiring delivers 120-volt AC, but a radio wants smooth, low-voltage DC, so the first box is the **power supply**: it converts wall power into the direct current the radio runs on. Next comes the **transceiver** itself — the box with the knobs and the display. The word tells you what it is: a transceiver combines a receiver and a transmitter in one unit (T7A02). When you listen, the receiver half works; when you push the talk button, the transmitter half works; they share one box, one display, and one antenna connection. You give the transceiver your voice through a **microphone**, or your Morse code through a **key**, and the mic cable also carries the push-to-talk line that flips the radio between listening and sending.

Between the transceiver and the antenna sits the **SWR meter**, a small measuring box wired inline. It watches the radio-frequency energy flowing past and tells you how much is going forward toward the antenna and how much is bouncing back — you will meet it properly in a moment. Finally, the **feed line** — almost always a coaxial cable, "coax" — carries your signal up to the **antenna**, which turns the electrical energy into radio waves. On receive, the same path runs in reverse: the antenna collects waves, the feed line brings them down, and the receiver half of the transceiver turns them back into sound.

{{fig:ch05-station-block}}

One reassuring point before we go further: a handheld transceiver (an **HT**), a mobile rig bolted under a car seat, and a big base-station radio are not three different technologies. They are the same block diagram in three sizes. An HT simply shrinks the power supply into a battery pack, the microphone into a pinhole, and the antenna into a rubber duck on top. Everything you learn about the blocks in this chapter transfers directly, whatever form factor you buy first.

### Powering the station

The pool's favorite power question asks what supply rating suits a typical 50-watt-output mobile FM transceiver, and the answer is a 13.8-volt supply rated around 12 amperes (T4A01). Each number has a reason. Why 13.8 and not 12? Because 13.8 volts is what a vehicle's electrical system actually delivers when the engine is running and the lead-acid battery is charging — "12-volt" equipment is designed around the real-world 13.8. Why 12 amperes for a 50-watt radio? Because no transmitter is close to 100 percent efficient. Multiplying it out, 13.8 volts times 12 amperes is about 166 watts drawn from the supply to deliver 50 watts of radio energy to the antenna; the difference becomes heat in the radio. The supply needs that headroom, so the wrong choices — the right voltage with only 4 amperes, or the right current at 24 volts — both fail in their own way.

**FACT:** A typical 50-watt-output mobile FM transceiver needs a 13.8-volt supply rated around 12 amperes.

Getting those 12 amperes from the supply to the radio without wasting them is the next question. When the radio transmits, current surges, and any resistance in the power leads eats voltage — that loss is called **voltage drop**, and if the voltage sags too far the transmitter misbehaves exactly when you key up. The fix is short, heavy-gauge wires for the DC power connection, because short, fat conductors have very little resistance and therefore minimize voltage drop while transmitting (T4A03). "Heavy gauge" means physically thick copper; in wire sizes a smaller gauge number is a thicker wire. In a vehicle there is one more rule: the transceiver's negative power return should connect at the 12-volt battery chassis ground (T4A11) — the point where the battery's negative terminal bonds to the car's frame — not to any random piece of sheet metal and not through the mounting bracket. A clean, solid return path keeps voltage drop down and keeps vehicle electrical noise out of your audio.

Battery operation brings the chapter's one formula. A battery's capacity is rated in **ampere-hours** (Ah): one ampere-hour means the battery can deliver one ampere for one hour before it is empty. (You will also see **watt-hours**, which folds voltage into the same idea — watt-hours are ampere-hours multiplied by the battery voltage.) To estimate how long equipment can be powered from a battery, divide the battery's ampere-hour rating by the average current draw of the equipment (T4A09). Watch the units do the work: ampere-hours divided by amperes leaves hours, which is exactly what you wanted. The exam's main distractor divides the watt-hour rating by the *peak* power consumption — a wrong pairing of units, and therefore a wrong answer even though it sounds scientific.

> **Worked example:** Your battery is rated 9 Ah and your equipment draws 2 A on average — how long will it run? Runtime equals ampere-hours divided by average current, so $9 \div 2 = 4.5$ hours. Notice what made it easy: you did not need the voltage, the power, or anything about peak draw — just the two numbers the formula asks for. If a question hands you watt-hours and peak watts instead, that is the distractor path; reach for ampere-hours and average current.

One caution that will keep your estimates honest: "average" means average over time. An FM radio that draws 8 amperes while transmitting and under 1 ampere while listening averages far below 8 if you mostly listen — which is why a modest battery runs an HT through a whole day of occasional chatting.

### Measuring as you hook up: SWR and power

Chapter 4 introduced SWR — standing wave ratio — as the measure of how well an antenna system is matched to its feed line. This chapter is about the meter itself, because the pool treats it as a piece of station equipment you must install and read correctly.

First, installation. An SWR meter does not clip on or sense anything from across the room; it goes *inline*, spliced into the feed line between the transceiver and the antenna, usually on a short coax jumper right behind the radio, so that all the transmitted power flows through it (see Figure 5.2). An RF power meter installs the same way — in the feed line, between the transmitter and the antenna (T4A05). And an accessory SWR meter — one you buy separately, rather than the one built into many radios — must be rated for the frequency and power level at which you will measure (T4A02). A meter designed for HF work can read nonsense at UHF, and a meter built for a 5-watt handheld will not survive a 100-watt transmitter.

Second, the readings. An SWR of 1:1 is a perfect impedance match between the antenna and the feed line (T7C04): every watt the transmitter sends forward keeps going forward. A reading of 4:1 indicates an impedance mismatch (T7C06) — a meaningful share of your power is reflecting back down the feed line instead of radiating. The instrument used to determine SWR is a **directional wattmeter** (T7C08), a meter that reads **forward power** (heading to the antenna) and **reflected power** (bouncing back) separately; the ratio between the two is what SWR describes. One of the pool's answer choices for that question is "iambic pentameter" — the exam writers' one poetry joke, and never the right answer.

**FACT:** An SWR reading of 1:1 indicates a perfect impedance match between antenna and feed line, while 4:1 indicates an impedance mismatch.

Third, what the radio does about it. Modern solid-state transmitters watch their own reflected power, and when SWR climbs too high they automatically reduce their output power. They do this to protect the RF output transistors — the final amplifying devices, which reflected power can overheat and destroy (T7C05). This leads to a diagnostic question the pool loves: what might cause *low* RF power output from a solid-state transceiver? *High* SWR (T7B04). Low SWR, the distractor, is the healthy condition and never cuts anyone's power. Remember the division of labor: the radio protects itself, but it cannot fix the antenna. Fold-down power is a symptom pointing you at the antenna system, not a repair.

Two more measuring tools belong in this picture. A **dummy load** is a fake antenna: a 50-ohm **non-inductive resistor** mounted on a heat sink (T7C03) — "non-inductive" meaning a plain resistor with no coil-like behavior that would add unwanted reactance at radio frequencies. Screwed onto the end of the feed line in place of the antenna, it absorbs everything the transmitter produces and turns it into harmless warmth, which lets you test a transmitter without putting a signal on the air (T7C01) — no interference to anyone, no rule issues, just quiet heat. And an **antenna analyzer** is a handheld instrument that generates its own tiny test signal and reports whether an antenna is resonant at your operating frequency (T7C02) — the quick way to find out where an antenna wants to work before you ever key the big radio.

### Computers, digital interfaces, and small accessories

Digital modes marry the radio to the computer, and the pool wants you to know exactly what crosses between them. A digital-mode interface moves three signals: receive audio (radio to computer), transmit audio (computer to radio), and transmitter keying (computer telling the radio when to transmit) (T4A06). Concretely, receive audio often comes from the transceiver's speaker connector, which feeds the computer's line-in or microphone input (T4A07). For a mode like FT8 — the wildly popular weak-signal mode where computers exchange short, precisely timed messages — the audio itself flows between the radio and the computer running the FT8 software (T4A04): on receive, tones come out of the radio and the software decodes them; on transmit, the software generates tones and the radio sends them. Sending information as audio tones like this is called AFSK, audio frequency-shift keying — the radio only ever sees sound.

Two other gadgets complete the small-accessories tour. A **digital hotspot** is a small RF gateway — usually a box the size of a deck of cards — that connects nearby transceivers to an internet digital voice or data network (T4A10). Your handheld talks to the hotspot over the air on low power; the hotspot carries your voice the rest of the way over the internet to the network and back. And for Morse code, an **electronic keyer** assists manual sending by forming the dits and dahs for you (T4A12): you work a paddle with your fingers, timing the elements, and the keyer generates clean, uniform dots and dashes. The skill is still yours; the keyer just makes every dit the same perfect length.

Last, a piece of hardware with no electronics in it at all: **bonding** — connecting equipment chassis together and to ground so stray RF has a short, easy path that does not run through your audio cables. The preferred conductor for RF bonding is flat copper strap, because of its low inductance at radio frequencies (T4A08). At RF, a wire's shape matters as much as its metal: a flat strap presents far less opposition to fast-changing RF current than a round wire of the same copper, so the braid-and-strap look in serious stations is physics, not fashion.

### The operating controls

Open any transceiver manual and the front panel can look like an airplane cockpit. The pool's control questions boil down to a friendly handful of ideas.

Setting frequency comes first. You enter the operating frequency with the keypad or the VFO knob (T4B02) — the keypad for typing 146.520 directly, the big tuning knob for dialing around. The **VFO**, variable frequency oscillator, is the circuit that sets the frequency; we will look inside it in the next section. When you would rather let the radio hunt for you, the **scanning** function tunes through a range of frequencies checking for activity (T4B05), stopping when it hears a signal.

**Squelch** is the control beginners set backwards, so take it slowly. Between signals, an FM receiver produces loud, raw static — hiss with nothing in it. Squelch is a gate that mutes the receiver audio when no signal is present, saving your ears. The knob sets a threshold: a signal stronger than the threshold opens the gate and you hear it; anything weaker stays muted. Now the backwards part. If a weak station is below your threshold, the gate never opens and you hear silence — and turning the knob *up* (a higher threshold) makes it worse. To hear a weak FM signal you go the other way: set the squelch threshold so the receiver audio is on all the time — squelch fully open, threshold at zero (T4B03). You will hear constant hiss with the weak signal buried in it, but you will hear it. The mnemonic: squelch silences; less squelch, more sound. Two related FM facts: a signal received slightly off frequency does not change pitch — the audio becomes distorted (T4B04) — and on the transmit side, excessive microphone gain on SSB produces distorted transmitted audio (T4B01), the SSB cousin of the FM overdeviation you will meet in the interference section. If a contact says your audio is harsh or crushing, back the mic gain down.

Single-sideband operators get one more receive control, because on SSB the receiver itself reconstructs the voice pitch. If an SSB station's voice sounds too high or too low — chipmunk or Darth Vader — the fix is the **RIT**, also labeled **Clarifier** (T4B06). RIT stands for receiver incremental tuning: it nudges the *receive* frequency a little without moving your *transmit* frequency, so you can tune the other station's voice to natural pitch while your own signal stays put. Nudging the main VFO would retune both sides and drag the contact off frequency.

Filters shape what you hear. **Filter bandwidth** is the width of the slice of spectrum the receiver lets through, and a multimode transceiver offers several widths so you can select a bandwidth matching the mode, reducing noise and interference (T4B08). Noise arrives across the whole dial; your desired signal occupies only its own width. A filter that matches the signal keeps all of it and trims away the noise on either side — too wide lets extra noise in, too narrow clips the signal itself. SSB voice occupies roughly 2400 Hz, so among the pool's listed choices, a 2400 Hz filter bandwidth provides the best signal-to-noise ratio for SSB reception (T4B10). For context: 500 Hz suits narrow CW Morse, while 5000 Hz is wider than an SSB signal needs.

**FACT:** Among the pool's listed choices, a 2400 Hz receiver filter bandwidth provides the best signal-to-noise ratio for SSB reception.

Digital voice radios move some of these settings off the front panel and into configuration data. A DMR radio's **code plug** is the configuration data — repeaters, talkgroups, and settings — loaded into the radio (T4B07), usually built on a computer and written to the radio with a cable. To reach a group of stations on DMR, you enter the group's identification code (T4B09) — the **talkgroup** number, which works like a channel name shared by everyone in the conversation. And a D-STAR radio has one personal requirement: your call sign must be programmed into it before it can transmit (T4B11), because the network carries your call sign with every transmission.

### Inside the radio

You never have to solder a transceiver to pass the exam, but the pool expects you to know the names and jobs of the main circuits inside — and two of them form the most-missed pair in this whole subelement.

The pair is **sensitivity** versus **selectivity**. Sensitivity is a receiver's ability to detect the presence of signals — especially weak ones (T7A01). Selectivity is its ability to discriminate between multiple nearby signals (T7A04). Detect versus separate. A sensitive receiver hears the faint station; a selective receiver picks that station out of a crowd on adjacent frequencies. The exam swaps the definitions on purpose, so anchor each word: sensitivity shares its root with "sensing" something faint; selectivity shares its root with "selecting" one signal from many.

Three more circuits do the frequency work. An **oscillator** generates a signal at a specific frequency (T7A05) — a steady electronic tone, the radio's heartbeat. The VFO you met on the front panel is the oscillator that sets a transceiver's receive and transmit frequency (T7A11). A **mixer** converts a signal from one frequency to another (T7A03) by combining two inputs and producing sum and difference frequencies; receivers use mixers to shift every incoming station to one fixed internal frequency where the filtering is easiest. And **modulation** is the act of combining speech (or data) with an RF carrier signal (T7A08) — imprinting your voice onto the radio wave so it can travel.

A few questions cover what bolts *onto* the radio. A **transverter** converts a transceiver's RF input and output to another band (T7A06) — it lets a radio that only covers, say, HF operate on a VHF band by translating both directions. Do not confuse it with a transceiver or a transformer. The **push-to-talk input** — the PTT line on a mic jack or accessory port — switches the transceiver from receive to transmit when it is grounded (T7A07); that is literally all "keying" a radio means electrically, which is why foot switches, headset buttons, and computer interfaces can all key the same rig by pulling that one line to ground. To raise your transmitted output power, add an **RF power amplifier** after the transceiver (T7A10) — the radio drives, the amplifier multiplies. (That question's "all these choices are correct" option is a trap; the other listed gadgets do not raise power.)

Finally, the question with a history: some VHF power amplifiers carry a switch labeled SSB on one side and CW-FM on the other, and the pool asks what it does. The revised 2026 wording keys it plainly: the switch sets the amplifier for proper operation in the selected mode (T7A09). The intuition is about who chooses what. *You* choose the mode, at the transceiver, when you decide to operate SSB or FM. The switch cannot change the mode of your transmitted signal — the tempting wrong answer — because the signal's mode was fixed when your radio created it. All the switch does is adapt the amplifier: SSB needs an amplifier that responds instantly and linearly to a voice signal that is constantly changing strength, while CW and FM run at steady full power and let the amplifier run in a more efficient style. Operator picks the mode; switch prepares the amplifier.

**FACT:** The SSB / CW-FM switch on some VHF power amplifiers sets the amplifier for proper operation in the selected mode — it adapts the amplifier; it does not change your signal's mode.

### When things interfere (RFI)

Sooner or later, radio meets the rest of the electronics in the world, and somebody hears something they should not. **RFI** — radio-frequency interference — is the pool's name for the whole topic, and its questions follow one master principle: find where the unwanted energy enters, and fix it there.

Start with interference you cause to your *own* signal. **Overdeviation** on FM means your audio is swinging the carrier farther than the channel allows: the louder or closer you talk, the wider the swing, until the transmitted audio distorts or drops out on voice peaks. If a contact reports overdeviation, the fix is to talk farther away from the microphone (T7B01). If your audio through an FM repeater is distorted or unintelligible, the possible causes include being slightly off frequency, speaking too loudly or too close to the microphone, and being in a bad location — and the keyed answer is that all these choices are correct (T7B10).

Now the neighbor's television that plays your voice over the evening news. What is happening is almost never that your transmitter is broken. The neighbor's device is being overloaded by a strong signal it cannot reject — your fundamental frequency arriving at a receiver that was never designed to keep strong out-of-band signals out. The name is **fundamental overload**, and the pool's point is that the problem is in the affected receiver, not necessarily your station (T7B02). That said, RFI genuinely can originate at your end too: it can come from fundamental overload, from **harmonics** (unwanted copies of your signal at exact multiples of your frequency), or from **spurious emissions** (any other unintended output your transmitter makes) — all three (T7B03). This is why the first move is always to check your own house before pointing anywhere.

The repair questions have a beautiful logic: the filter goes where the problem lives. Learn the direction and three questions answer themselves.

- Your clean, legal signal bothers the neighbor's receiver. The overload is happening *in their receiver*, so the fix is a filter at the affected receiver's antenna input that blocks your frequency while passing what they want to watch (T7B05).

- A nearby commercial FM broadcast station overloads *your* 2-meter rig. Now the overload is in *your* receiver, so the fix is a **band-reject filter** at your receiver that notches out the broadcaster's frequency (T7B07).

- A cable TV system (not fiber) picks up interference. Cable signals travel inside shielded coax, so the usual entry point is broken shielding — and the first step is to check that all coax connectors are properly installed (T7B09). Filters come later, if at all.

The etiquette questions have the same shape as the technical ones. When a neighbor complains, your first step is to verify that your own station is operating properly (T7B06) — check it on a dummy load, confirm you are clean, before any discussion about their equipment. And when *their* device interferes with *you* — a growling power supply, a noisy gadget — the pool's answer combines all of the good moves: work with your neighbor to identify the offending device, remember that FCC rules prohibit devices that cause interference, and make sure your own station is above reproach first (T7B08). Politeness plus a clean station solves nearly everything. One caution for exam day: "all these choices are correct" is genuinely keyed on several T7 questions, but the same option sits as bait on others (T7B06, T7A10, T7D07 among them). Never auto-pick it — read each listed choice and confirm every one is true before you believe it.

One last gremlin lives inside your own station: **RF feedback**. Transmit, and your own radiated RF gets picked up by the microphone cable, rides it back into the rig, and distorts your audio — the classic symptom is a transmit signal that sounds fine on a dummy load but raspy on the antenna. The cure is a clip-on **ferrite choke** snapped onto the microphone cable (T7B11): a bead of ferrite material that acts like a one-way sponge for RF on the cable's outside, soaking up the unwanted current while leaving your voice — which travels inside the wire — untouched.

### The workbench: feed-line health, meters, soldering

The last group of T7 questions is about the workbench: keeping feed line healthy, using meters without hurting them, and making solder joints that last.

Feed line is a consumable. Any power lost in a feed line is converted into heat (T7C07) — it does not vanish, and it does not radiate usefully; it just warms the cable. Coax has three classic enemies. Moisture contamination is the classic coax-killer (T7C09): water wicks into the cable, corrodes the braid, and ruins its electrical properties, which is why outdoor connectors get carefully weatherproofed. Sunlight is enemy number two: ultraviolet exposure cracks the outer jacket and lets the water in, so coax intended for outdoor use must have a UV-resistant jacket (T7C10). And when you are choosing cable, know that foam-dielectric coax has less loss per foot than solid-dielectric coax (T7C11) — the **dielectric** is the insulating layer between the center conductor and the shield, and foaming it with gas makes it a better insulator at radio frequencies.

Meters measure the invisible, and each of the big three has one rule you must not break. A **voltmeter** measures voltage — electric potential — and connects in parallel with the component, touching both sides of it at once (T7D01, T7D02). An **ammeter** measures current and connects in series, so the current flows through the meter (T7D03, T7D04) — you break the circuit and let the traffic pass through the instrument. A **multimeter** combines both jobs (plus an ohmmeter) in one box with a selector switch; among the pool's choices, it measures voltage and resistance (T7D07) — and current too — but not impedance or signal strength, which is why "all these choices are correct" is the trap on that one.

**FACT:** A voltmeter measures electric potential (voltage) and connects in parallel with the component; an ammeter measures current and connects in series so the current flows through the meter.

The **ohmmeter** setting deserves its own paragraph because it works differently and dies differently. An ohmmeter measures resistance by applying a small current from its own internal battery and measuring what comes back — so the circuit under test must not be powered (T7D05, T7D11). Measuring a live circuit injects outside voltage into a setting that expects only its own battery, and attempting to measure voltage while the meter is on the resistance setting can damage the meter (T7D06). There is one famous side effect of that internal battery: connect an ohmmeter across a large discharged capacitor and the reading starts low and climbs — increasing resistance with time — because the meter's battery is slowly charging the capacitor, and a charging capacitor draws less and less current as it fills (T7D10). If a meter seems to be "misbehaving" across a big capacitor, it is actually telling you the capacitor works.

Finally, the solder. For electronics work the only correct choice is **rosin-core solder** — solder with a core of rosin flux that cleans the joint as it melts. **Acid-core solder** is for plumbing, and its acid keeps corroding long after the joint cools, destroying electronic connections; never use it on electronics (T7D08). And learn the look of a good joint: a proper solder joint is shiny and smooth, while a **cold solder joint** — one where the solder melted without properly wetting the surfaces — looks rough or lumpy (T7D09). Dull and grainy means reheat it; shiny and smooth means it will outlive the radio.

### Exam Focus

Subelements T4 and T7 (groups T4A–T4B and T7A–T7D) hold 67 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 6 of them — one per group. Here are eight, quoted exactly as the pool prints them, spanning all six groups; T7A09 appears in its current revised form.

> **T4A01** Which of the following is an appropriate power supply rating for a typical 50-watt output mobile FM transceiver?
> A. 24.0 volts at 4 amperes
> B. 13.8 volts at 4 amperes
> C. 24.0 volts at 12 amperes
> D. 13.8 volts at 12 amperes
> **Answer: D** — 13.8 volts matches a real vehicle electrical system, and roughly 12 amperes of headroom is needed because a transmitter draws well over 50 watts to make 50 watts.

> **T4A09** How can you determine the length of time that equipment can be powered from a battery?
> A. Divide the watt-hour rating of the battery by the peak power consumption of the equipment
> B. Divide the battery ampere-hour rating by the average current draw of the equipment
> C. Multiply the watts per hour consumed by the equipment by the battery power rating
> D. Multiply the square of the current rating of the battery by the input resistance of the equipment
> **Answer: B** — ampere-hours divided by average amperes leaves hours; choice A pairs watt-hours with peak power, the wrong units.

> **T4B10** Which of the following receiver filter bandwidths provides the best signal-to-noise ratio for SSB reception?
> A. 500 Hz
> B. 1000 Hz
> C. 2400 Hz
> D. 5000 Hz
> **Answer: C** — 2400 Hz matches the width of an SSB voice signal, passing all of it while trimming away noise on both sides.

> **T7A09** What is the function of the switch which selects either SSB or CW-FM on some VHF power amplifiers?
> A. Change the mode of the transmitted signal
> B. Set the amplifier for proper operation in the selected mode
> C. Change the frequency range of the amplifier to operate in the proper segment of the band
> D. Reduce the received signal noise
> **Answer: B** — you pick the mode at the transceiver; the switch only adapts the amplifier to run properly in it.

> **T7B04** Which of the following might be the cause of low RF power output from a solid-state transceiver?
> A. Poor amplifier noise figure
> B. Poor amplifier linearity
> C. Low SWR
> D. High SWR
> **Answer: D** — high SWR reflects power back at the transmitter, which folds back its output to protect the final transistors; low SWR is the healthy case.

> **T7C04** What reading on an SWR meter indicates a perfect impedance match between the antenna and the feed line?
> A. 50:50
> B. Zero
> C. 1:1
> D. Full Scale
> **Answer: C** — 1:1 means all forward power and no reflected power: a perfect match.

> **T7C08** Which instrument can be used to determine SWR?
> A. Voltmeter
> B. Ohmmeter
> C. Iambic pentameter
> D. Directional wattmeter
> **Answer: D** — a directional wattmeter reads forward and reflected power, and SWR comes from comparing the two; choice C is the pool's poetry joke.

> **T7D06** Which of the following can damage a multimeter?
> A. Attempting to measure resistance using the voltage setting
> B. Failing to connect one of the probes to ground
> C. Attempting to measure voltage when using the resistance setting
> D. Not allowing it to warm up properly
> **Answer: C** — the resistance setting drives the meter's own battery out of the probes, so external voltage on that setting can cook the meter.

### Key Takeaways

- A station is a short block diagram — power supply, transceiver, mic or key, SWR meter inline, feed line, antenna — and a transceiver is simply a receiver and a transmitter sharing one box.

- A 50-watt mobile FM transceiver wants a 13.8-volt supply rated around 12 amperes; feed it with short, heavy-gauge leads, ground the negative return at the battery chassis ground, and remember transmitters draw far more power than they radiate.

- Battery runtime equals the ampere-hour rating divided by the average current draw: 9 Ah at 2 A runs about 4.5 hours.

- SWR of 1:1 is a perfect match and 4:1 is a mismatch; a directional wattmeter determines SWR, high SWR makes a solid-state radio cut power to protect its output transistors, and a dummy load lets you test without putting a signal on the air.

- Squelch runs backwards to intuition: to hear a weak FM signal, open it fully so the audio is on all the time; 2400 Hz is the best filter bandwidth for SSB among the pool's choices.

- Sensitivity detects weak signals, selectivity separates nearby ones; the SSB / CW-FM switch on a VHF amplifier adapts the amplifier to the mode you already chose — it never changes your signal's mode.

- Interference fixes live where the problem enters: filter at the affected receiver, band-reject at your own overloaded receiver, check coax connectors for cable TV, clip a ferrite choke on a mic cable for RF feedback — and always verify your own station first.

- A voltmeter connects in parallel, an ammeter in series, and an ohmmeter is used only on unpowered circuits — measuring voltage on the resistance setting can damage the meter; solder electronics with rosin-core only and trust shiny, smooth joints.
