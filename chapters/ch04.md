## 4. Antennas & Feedlines

Your new handheld reaches the repeater fine from the driveway, but from the kitchen table nobody hears you. A club member tells you the radio is fine — the antenna is the problem — and suggests a 19-inch whip on the car roof and better coax. Someone else mentions checking the SWR. To a brand-new ham this all sounds like folklore, but every piece of it follows one simple rule: antennas are sized by wavelength, and everything between the radio and the antenna either carries your signal faithfully or eats a slice of it. In this chapter you'll learn why antenna length follows frequency, what antenna gain really means (and what it does not), how the common antenna shapes behave, what coaxial cable and connectors do to your signal, what SWR measures, and where an antenna tuner fits in. All of it maps to pool subelement T9 — 23 questions, 2 of them on your exam.

### Antennas and wavelength: why size follows frequency

Everything in your station up to this point has been electronics — circuits pushing electrical energy around. The **antenna** is where that energy becomes a radio wave. An antenna is the component that takes the radio-frequency (RF) energy your transmitter delivers and launches it into space as a wave; on receive, the same antenna works in reverse, catching a passing wave and turning it back into electrical energy for your receiver. It is the doorway between the wires and the sky, in both directions.

An antenna works best when its physical size matches the wave it handles, and the matching length is tied directly to the wavelength you met in chapter 3. The frequency at which a given antenna's size matches the wave is its **resonant frequency**, and an antenna operating there is called resonant — the radio equivalent of a guitar string tuned to its note. A resonant antenna takes the transmitter's energy readily and radiates it well; far from resonance it radiates poorly and gives the transmitter a hard time, a problem we will return to when SWR comes up later in the chapter.

The link between size and frequency is the pool's own shortcut from chapter 3: $\lambda = 300 / f$ — wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz, presented as the handy approximation it is. Because higher frequency means shorter wavelength, it also means a shorter antenna. The 2-meter band and the 70-centimeter band are named for exactly this: the wavelength tells you roughly how big a properly sized antenna will be.

The simplest practical antenna is the **half-wave dipole**: a straight conductor one half-wavelength long overall, split in the middle into two quarter-wave legs, with the feed line connected at the center. For the 6-meter band at 50 MHz, the wavelength is 300 ÷ 50 = 6 meters, so a dipole for 50 MHz works out to about 3 meters tip to tip — the size of a small room, strung horizontally between two supports. That is the scale of HF and 6-meter antennas, and it is why hams with yards use wire and hams with balconies use shorter, cleverer things.

{{fig:ch04-dipole}}

The other classic shape is the **quarter-wave vertical**: a single upright whip one quarter-wavelength long, worked against **ground radials** — a few rods or wires spreading out from the base — that stand in for the other half of the antenna. The whip plus its ground system behaves like one half of a dipole standing on end. On 2 meters this shrinks to something you can hold in one hand: the wavelength at 146 MHz is 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.05 meters, so a quarter-wave is about half a meter — and the pool itself prints the number every ham recognizes. A 19-inch vertical antenna is often used on 2 meters because it is a resonant quarter-wave at that band (T9A08).

{{fig:ch04-vertical-groundplane}}

> **Worked example:** Why 19 inches? Take the middle of the 2-meter band, around 146 MHz. The wavelength shortcut gives λ = 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.05 meters. A quarter-wave whip is one quarter of that: 2.05 ÷ 4 ≈ 0.51 meters, which is about 51 centimeters, or roughly 20 inches. Real antennas run about 5 percent short, which lands you right on the 19-inch whip sold everywhere. So the exam answer is "resonant quarter-wave" — and you can see why the "resonant half-wave" distractor fails: a half-wave vertical at 146 MHz would need about a meter of whip, twice the antenna on the roof of every second car in the club parking lot.

**FACT:** A 19-inch vertical antenna is often used on 2 meters because it is a resonant quarter-wave at that band.

Because size and wavelength move together, the relationship between length and resonant frequency runs inverse: a shorter antenna resonates at a higher frequency, a longer antenna at a lower one. The pool asks which of several changes increases the resonant frequency of a dipole, and the answer is shortening it (T9A05). The distractors all push the other way: lengthening the wire obviously lowers the frequency, and inserting coils or adding capacitive loading are ways of making the antenna electrically longer — which also lowers it. If you remember one direction — cut it shorter to move it up — the question answers itself.

> **Worked example:** Suppose you build a dipole for 50 MHz, down in the 6-meter band. Half a wavelength is (300 ÷ 50) ÷ 2 = 3 meters, tip to tip. Now you want the same wire to resonate at 100 MHz instead — twice the frequency. Twice the frequency means half the wavelength, so the dipole must come down to 1.5 meters. You raise the resonance by shortening the antenna. No new math is on the exam for this; the direction is the whole lesson.

None of this means your first antenna must be cut to the exact centimeter. Real antennas are tuned by measuring and trimming: hams cut a dipole a little long, check how it behaves, and snip it shorter a bit at a time until it resonates where they want. An antenna also works over a small range around its resonant frequency — a dipole cut for the middle of 2 meters happily covers the whole band. The exam never asks you to calculate a length; it asks which direction the length-frequency relationship runs, and why the 19-inch whip belongs on 2 meters. You have both.

One more orientation detail hides in these two shapes, and it leads straight into the next section. A dipole is usually strung horizontally, parallel to the ground, while a quarter-wave vertical stands straight up. That physical orientation is not just about convenience — it decides the polarization of the wave the antenna launches, and polarization decides whom you can hear.

### Antenna types and what gain really means

Some antennas spread their energy in every direction; others concentrate it. A **beam antenna** is an antenna that concentrates signals in one direction (T9A01) — that is the entire definition, and the pool's answer choices about square aluminum beams or an inventor named Clarence Beam are pure fiction. The classic beam is the **Yagi**: a driven element — the one rod actually connected to the feed line — plus a row of parallel elements mounted on a boom that shape the pattern, the same silhouette as an old rooftop TV antenna. Of the antenna types the pool lists — a 5/8 wave vertical, an isotropic antenna, a J pole, and a Yagi — the Yagi offers the greatest gain (T9A06). The isotropic choice deserves a word, because it explains the vocabulary: an **isotropic antenna** is a theoretical point that radiates equally in all directions. You cannot build one, but you can measure real antennas against it — an antenna that spreads its energy everywhere wins in no particular direction.

Beams are everyday tools, not exotic gear. A small Yagi on a rotator is how serious VHF operators chase weak stations: point the boom at the other station and both your transmitted signal and your hearing improve in that one direction. Hams hunting a hidden transmitter in a direction-finding game called a fox hunt carry small handheld Yagis for the same reason — the direction with the strongest signal is the direction to walk.

That comparison is the pool's definition of **antenna gain**: the increase in signal strength in a specified direction compared to a reference antenna (T9A11). Read it twice, because the exam's wrong answers are built for people who skim. Gain is not power added to your transmitter — an antenna has no battery and no amplifier, so it cannot create energy. Think of a flashlight reflector: the bulb puts out the same total light with or without it, but the reflector gathers that light into a beam. Where the beam points, things get brighter; everywhere else, dimmer. Antenna gain is exactly that focusing, and no more. The reciprocity is a free bonus: a beam that transmits better in one direction also receives better from that same direction.

**FACT:** A beam antenna concentrates signals in one direction, and of the antennas the pool lists, a Yagi offers the greatest gain.

Gain also explains why there is no single best antenna, only the best antenna for a job. Focusing is a trade: energy aimed at one horizon is energy not sent somewhere else. A vertical whip covers every direction around the car at once, which is exactly what a mobile operator on unpredictable routes wants. A beam covers one direction superbly and ignores the rest, which is what a fixed station pointing at a distant repeater or a satellite wants. Same total power, different arrangements — you pick the pattern that matches the job.

Even a plain dipole focuses, and beginners reliably picture its pattern backwards. A half-wave dipole radiates its strongest signal **broadside** — out from its sides, at right angles to the wire — and its weakest off the ends (T9A10). The first-time mental image is a garden hose, with signal squirting out of the wire tips; the truth is the reverse, a pattern shaped like a donut with the wire through the hole. Off the ends of the wire there is almost nothing. So if your dipole runs north to south, its best coverage is east and west — you aim a dipole with its sides, not its ends.

{{fig:ch04-dipole-pattern}}

**FACT:** A half-wave dipole radiates strongest broadside (out from its sides) and weakest off its ends.

Sometimes physics will not give you the length you want — a 40-meter half-wave is twenty meters of wire, and no sedan can wear that. The workaround is **loading**: electrically lengthening an antenna by inserting inductors (coils) in the radiating elements (T9A02). The coil makes a physically short whip behave, electrically, like a longer one, so a mobile whip far shorter than the band's free-space quarter-wave can still resonate on it. The pool's distractors are the mechanical look-alikes: a spring in the whip's base just adds flexibility, a resistor in the element would waste power as heat, and beefing up a beam against wind is structural work, not loading. Loading trades a little efficiency for a lot of convenience — which is the theme of every antenna you will actually carry or drive around with.

**Polarization**, met in chapter 3, has a one-line definition here: an antenna's polarization is described by the orientation of the electric field (T9A03) — never the magnetic field, never the shape of the element. A vertical whip radiates a vertically polarized wave; a horizontal dipole radiates a horizontally polarized one. That is why the FM and repeater world, built on car and handheld whips, standardized on vertical — and why the chapter 3 lesson still holds: on a line-of-sight path, cross-polarized antennas cost you received signal, so match orientations when you can.

Now the practical trio every handheld owner learns, all three straight from the pool. First, the short flexible **rubber-duck antenna** that ships on every handheld is a convenience, not a performer: compared to a full-sized quarter-wave antenna, it has low efficiency (T9A04) — it is physically short, electrically loaded, and working against no real ground system, so a slice of your power never becomes a wave. Second, using that handheld from inside a car makes things worse: the vehicle's metal shell shields and weakens the signal (T9A07). The fix for both is an external antenna — a magnetic-mount whip on the roof puts a properly sized, properly grounded radiator outside the metal box. Third, when you shop for that mobile whip you will meet the **5/8-wave whip**: a longer mobile vertical that has more gain than a quarter-wave whip for VHF or UHF mobile service (T9A09), because its shape concentrates more of the signal low, toward the horizon, where mobile contacts actually live.

### Feed lines: coax and loss

Between the radio and the antenna sits the **feed line** — whatever carries the RF energy between the two. For nearly every amateur installation the answer is **coaxial cable**, universally shortened to coax. Cut a piece open and you find four layers: a center conductor, an insulating layer called the **dielectric**, a braided metal shield wrapped around that, and a protective outer jacket. (The connectors figure later in the chapter shows the cross-section.) The signal travels in the space between the center conductor and the shield, so the RF stays inside the cable and the outside world stays out — you can route coax through walls, past gutters, and along baseboards without special precautions, which is most of why the hobby runs on it.

Every feed line has a **characteristic impedance** — the natural impedance it presents to the signal, set by the geometry of its conductors and spacing, not by how long the piece is. The most common impedance of coaxial cables used in amateur radio is 50 ohms (T9B02), and nearly every transceiver, tuner, and antenna you meet is built to match that number. When the exam asks why coax is the most common feed line, resist the answers about performance: the keyed reason is that it is easy to use and requires few special installation considerations (T9B03). Choices claiming coax has less loss than any other feed line, handles more power than any other, or costs less than any other are all bait — coax wins on convenience, and the pool says so plainly.

One practical warning follows from that number. The television coax at the hardware store — the cable that feeds home TV antennas and satellite dishes — is built to 75 ohms, and 75-ohm flexible coax even shows up among the pool's feed-line answer choices as a distractor. Amateur radio is a 50-ohm ecosystem: radios, tuners, and antennas are all designed around that one number. Stay with 50-ohm coax for your station and every box in the chain matches without a second thought.

**FACT:** The most common impedance of coaxial cables used in amateur radio is 50 ohms.

No feed line is a perfect courier. Part of your signal warms the cable instead of reaching the antenna, and the lost fraction is not fixed: coax loss increases as frequency increases (T9B05). A cable run that is perfectly fine on an HF band can waste a noticeable share of your power at 440 MHz, which is why UHF installations need better cable, shorter cable, or both. Notice the trade the physics hands you as you move up in frequency: the antennas get shorter and easier, while the feed line gets fussier and more expensive.

**FACT:** Coax loss increases as frequency increases — which is why UHF runs need better or shorter cable.

Cable grades follow a rough rule of thumb: thicker is better. RG-58 is the thin, flexible coax about the size of a pencil; RG-213 is roughly twice that diameter. The electrical difference the pool wants is that RG-213 has less loss at a given frequency (T9B10) — more copper, more dielectric, less signal turned to heat per foot. At the top of the ladder sits **hardline**: stiff coax built with an air-filled dielectric and a solid outer conductor, and air-insulated hardline has the lowest loss of the feed lines the pool lists (T9B11). It is what repeater owners run up towers — excellent, costly, and about as bendable as plumbing, which circles back to why ordinary stations live with flexible coax and its losses.

Loss has more than one source, and the pool bundles them into one all-of-the-above question. Water intrusion into coaxial connectors, high SWR, and multiple connectors in the line all cause feed-line loss (T9B08). Every connector is a small discontinuity where a little signal is spent; water creeping into a fitting corrodes it and soaks the cable; and high SWR — the mismatch you will read about next — forces power to make extra reflected trips through the lossy line, paying the cable's toll each way. The habits that follow are simple: keep runs short, keep connectors few, and keep every joint dry.

### SWR and the antenna tuner

The word hams use for feed-line-and-antenna health is **SWR**, standing wave ratio, and the pool's definition is one precise sentence: SWR is a measure of how well a load is matched to a transmission line (T9B12). Here is the mechanics behind the sentence. When the antenna's impedance matches the feed line's characteristic impedance, power flows forward and is radiated. When they do not match, part of the power reaches the antenna, fails to transfer, and reflects back down the line toward the radio. The forward and reflected waves combine into a stationary ripple along the cable — a **standing wave** — and the ratio of the ripple's strongest point to its weakest is the SWR, written as a pair like 1:1 or 3:1.

A reading of 1:1 is the perfect match — everything forward, nothing reflected — and climbing numbers warn of mismatch, with 4:1 the pool's usual example of trouble. Because the mismatch is worst away from the antenna's resonant frequency, a plot of SWR against frequency dips to its lowest point right at resonance and rises on either side, which is exactly the curve in the figure below. Actually reading an SWR meter — where it installs in the line, what forward and reflected power mean — belongs to chapter 5's station-equipment tour; this chapter's exam questions only ask what SWR is and what disturbs it. One reason to care crosses chapter boundaries, though: high SWR can make a modern solid-state transceiver reduce its output power on purpose, protecting its RF output transistors from the reflected energy — so a mismatched antenna costs you range twice, once in the feed line and again inside the radio.

{{fig:ch04-swr-curve}}

SWR also talks to you over time, and the pool wants you to listen. If your SWR reading changes erratically — jumping when you wiggle the cable, when the car hits a bump, when the weather shifts — the classic cause is a loose connection in the antenna or feed line (T9B09). Hold the distinction in two plain sentences: a steady high SWR is a tuning problem, but a jumping SWR is a hardware problem. A connector backing off its threads, a cracked solder joint, a whip working loose in its mount — anything that makes and breaks contact as things move will make the meter dance, and a thunderstorm or a strong local station, the distractors offered, will not.

Keep the definition narrow, because the exam's wrong answers stretch it. SWR is not about your station's ground connection, and it is not an efficiency rating or an amplifier power ratio — those are exactly the distractors the pool offers. A perfectly grounded station can show a miserable SWR if the antenna does not match the line, and a beautifully built antenna still needs the match. One number, one job: how well the load at the end of the transmission line matches the line.

Last comes the box that fixes the steady kind of mismatch. An **antenna tuner** — also called an antenna coupler or transmatch — sits at the radio, and its major function is to match the antenna system impedance to the transceiver's output impedance (T9B04). The transmitter wants to look into 50 ohms; the tuner adjusts itself until the radio sees that, whatever the antenna system actually presents. You have already met its innards: pool figure T-3 from chapter 1 is a tuner circuit, a T-network of variable capacitors and a variable inductor, and those adjustable components are exactly what the knobs turn. Be honest with yourself about what the box does, though: it makes the radio happy by presenting a match at the radio end of the line — it does not shorten the coax, dry out a connector, or make a badly sized antenna radiate better. The exam only asks the function, and the function is matching.

### Connectors

Feed-line hardware ends at the **connector**, and a few plugs cover nearly everything a Technician touches. The PL-259 is the big silver plug with the screw-on collar found on most HF and VHF coax, and its matching chassis socket — the one on the back of your radio — is the SO-239. The pool's fact about it: PL-259 connectors are commonly used at HF and VHF frequencies (T9B07). Everyone calls it the "UHF connector," a nickname left over from long ago — but the plug is not watertight, and despite the name it is not the best choice at the frequencies we call UHF today.

The genuinely good high-frequency choice is the **Type N connector**: a threaded, weather-resistant design whose performance holds up as frequency climbs, making it the most suitable of the listed connectors for frequencies above 400 MHz (T9B06). The pool asks T9B07 and T9B06 as a deliberate pair, so learn them together: the connector named "UHF" is the everyday standard at HF and VHF, while the connector that actually belongs above 400 MHz is the Type N. Two smaller plugs round out the picture — the BNC, the quarter-turn bayonet plug on handhelds and test gear, and the SMA, the tiny screw-on fitting on many handheld antennas. The figure shows all three common plugs alongside the four layers of coax they attach to.

{{fig:ch04-connectors}}

Whatever the connector type, the outdoor rule has no exceptions. The pool asks which connectors should be carefully taped for weather protection when used outdoors — PL259, BNC, Type N — and the answer is all these choices (T9B01). Every outdoor joint gets wrapped against the weather, because water in a fitting means corrosion, mismatch, and the ruined coax from the T9B08 loss list. Hams do the wrapping with self-fusing silicone tape or a coax sealant putty, molded over the joint so water sheds off instead of sitting in the threads. A few minutes with weatherproofing when you install the antenna beats a frustrating afternoon on a ladder a season later.

### Exam Focus

Subelement T9 (groups T9A–T9B) holds 23 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 2 of them — one per group. Here are six, quoted exactly as the pool prints them, spanning both groups: the four figure-backed questions (the 19-inch whip, the dipole pattern, the connector choice, and the SWR definition), the rubber-duck disadvantage every handheld owner meets first, and the 50-ohm number that anchors the feed-line section.

> **T9A04** What is a disadvantage of a handheld radio transceiver’s short flexible antenna compared to a full-sized quarter-wave antenna?
> A. It has low efficiency
> B. It transmits only circularly polarized signals
> C. It is more susceptible to receiver desensitization
> D. It only works on analog signals, not digital ones
> **Answer: A** — the rubber duck's short, loaded build trades away efficiency; a full-sized quarter-wave is the fair comparison the pool makes.

> **T9A08** Why is a 19-inch-long vertical antenna often used on 2 meters?
> A. It has high gain
> B. It is a resonant half-wave
> C. It is a resonant quarter-wave
> D. It has low RF radiation exposure
> **Answer: C** — 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2 meters, and a quarter of that is about 19 inches, just as the chapter's first worked example shows.

> **T9A10** In which direction does a half-wave dipole antenna radiate the strongest signal?
> A. Equally in all directions
> B. Off the ends of the antenna
> C. In the direction of the feed line
> D. Broadside to the antenna
> **Answer: D** — the energy leaves out from the sides of the wire; off the ends is where the dipole is weakest, the reverse of the common first guess.

> **T9B02** What is the most common impedance of coaxial cables used in amateur radio?
> A. 8 ohms
> B. 50 ohms
> C. 600 ohms
> D. 12 ohms
> **Answer: B** — amateur coax is standardized at 50 ohms of characteristic impedance, and nearly every rig and antenna is built to match it.

> **T9B06** Which of the following connector types is most suitable as an RF connector for frequencies above 400 MHz?
> A. PL-259
> B. Type N
> C. RS-213
> D. DB-25
> **Answer: B** — above 400 MHz the threaded, weather-resistant Type N is the right connector; despite its nickname, the PL-259 "UHF" plug is not.

> **T9B12** What is standing wave ratio (SWR)?
> A. A measure of how well a load is matched to a transmission line
> B. The ratio of amplifier power output to input
> C. The transmitter efficiency ratio
> D. An indication of the quality of your station’s ground connection
> **Answer: A** — SWR is purely about the match between the transmission line and its load; the other choices describe amplifiers, efficiency, and grounding.

### Key Takeaways

- Antenna size follows wavelength: λ ≈ 300 ÷ frequency in MHz, so a half-wave dipole for 50 MHz is about 3 meters tip to tip, and the common 19-inch vertical is a resonant quarter-wave on 2 meters.

- Length and frequency run inverse: shortening a dipole raises its resonant frequency, and loading — inserting inductor coils in the elements — electrically lengthens an antenna to lower it.

- A beam antenna concentrates signals in one direction, and of the antennas the pool lists, a Yagi offers the greatest gain. Gain is focusing — the increase in signal strength in a specified direction compared to a reference antenna — never power created out of nothing.

- A half-wave dipole radiates strongest broadside, out from its sides, and weakest off its ends; polarization is described by the orientation of the electric field.

- The rubber duck on a handheld trades efficiency for convenience, a car's metal shell shields and weakens your signal (use an external antenna), and a 5/8-wave whip gives more mobile gain than a quarter-wave.

- Amateur coax is 50 ohms, and it dominates because it is easy to use and needs few special installation considerations. Coax loss increases as frequency increases; RG-213 beats RG-58, and air-insulated hardline has the lowest loss of the feed lines the pool lists.

- SWR measures how well a load is matched to a transmission line — 1:1 is a perfect match — erratic SWR changes point to a loose connection in the antenna or feed line, and an antenna tuner matches the antenna system impedance to the transceiver's output.

- The PL-259 "UHF" plug is the HF/VHF standard but is not watertight; the Type N connector is the right choice above 400 MHz; and every outdoor connector should be carefully taped against the weather.
