## 6. Operating: Repeaters & VHF/UHF Life

Your call sign appeared in the FCC database this morning, your first handheld is charged, and tonight the local club's repeater is humming with voices. You know the frequency — it is printed in the club newsletter — but when you key the microphone and say hello, nothing comes back. Welcome to the gap between owning a radio and operating one. Operating has its own machinery (a repeater is not one frequency but two), its own customs (you never call CQ on a repeater), and its own vocabulary (a tone is not privacy, a net has a boss, and "check" counts words). In this chapter you'll learn how a repeater's input, output, and offset fit together; how to bring up a machine with the right tone; how to call, answer, and share a frequency politely; how directed nets and formal message traffic work; and how ARES and RACES fit into public service. All of it maps to pool subelement T2 — 37 questions, 3 of them on your exam.

### Inside a repeater: input, output, and offset

A repeater is an automatic relay station, usually perched on a hilltop, a water tower, or a tall building — anywhere high. It does one job: it hears your signal and simultaneously retransmits it on a different channel, so a conversation that would die at the edge of your neighborhood instead reaches across the county. Your five-watt handheld sounds big because the machine on the ridge does the heavy lifting. And "the machine" is genuinely what hams call it: "Are you on the 940 machine tonight?" is a perfectly normal sentence on 2 meters.

The repeater lives on two frequencies at once. It listens on its **input** — that is where you transmit — and it transmits on its **output** — that is where you listen. When a club says "our repeater is 146.940," the number they mean is the output, because that is the frequency you tune to hear it. Your radio transmits 600 kHz away, on the input, without you ever dialing that second frequency.

The distance between those two frequencies has a name: the **offset** is the difference between the repeater's transmit and receive frequencies (T2A07). Read that definition twice, because the exam tests it nearly word for word. The sizes worth memorizing are two: a common repeater frequency offset on the 2-meter band is plus or minus 600 kHz (T2A01), and on the 70-centimeter band it is plus or minus 5 MHz (T2A03). Notice the wording — a **common** offset, never "the" offset. These numbers are community custom, not an FCC rule, and near the band edges repeaters run **odd splits** that match neither convention. The exam's favorite trap is swapping the two bands' numbers: 600 kHz belongs to 2 meters, 5 MHz to 70 centimeters, and each shows up as a wrong answer on the other's question.

In practice your radio handles this for you. Modern radios **auto-shift**: pick the output frequency and the radio applies the common offset for that part of the band on its own, transmitting on the input whenever you key the mic. An odd split near a band edge is the rare case where you program the shift by hand — one more reason to know what the radio is doing behind your back.

{{fig:ch06-repeater-offset}}

> **Worked example:** A 2-meter repeater's output is 146.760 MHz and it uses a common −600 kHz offset. What frequency do you transmit on? Subtract: 146.760 − 0.600 = **146.160 MHz**. That is the repeater's input — the machine listens there and repeats you on 146.760, where everyone else is tuned. Your radio's auto-offset usually handles this arithmetic without being asked, but now you know exactly what it is doing.

Where do beginners find machines? Club websites and online repeater directories — RepeaterBook is the de facto one — list each repeater's output frequency, offset direction, and access tone. Look up the repeaters around your home and your commute, write down the output and the tone for each, and you have your first programming list.

**FACT:** A common repeater frequency offset in the 2-meter band is plus or minus 600 kHz.

**FACT:** A common repeater frequency offset in the 70-centimeter band is plus or minus 5 MHz.

### Simplex and the national calling frequency

Take the repeater away and radio gets even simpler. **Simplex** means transmitting and receiving on the same frequency (T2A11): two handhelds talking directly, one channel, no machine in the middle. Repeater operation is the contrast case — your radio receives on one frequency and transmits on another, a split sometimes called **duplex** because two frequencies carry one conversation. Both patterns matter, and the exam wants the simplex definition straight: same frequency, both directions.

{{fig:ch06-simplex-duplex}}

Watch a repeater contact make its round trip and the two patterns lock in. Radio A transmits on the repeater's input; the machine retransmits on its output, where radio B is listening. When it is B's turn to talk, B transmits on that same input and A hears the reply on the output. Each radio does its own offset in its own direction, turn by turn — one conversation riding two frequencies, with the machine in the middle passing every word along.

Why reserve channels for simplex at all? Because a repeater is a shared resource, and two friends a mile apart do not need a mountaintop relay to chat. Band plans designate simplex channels so that stations within range of each other can talk without tying up a repeater (T2B09). The polite habit follows directly: if you are ragchewing with someone in direct range, move the conversation to simplex and free the machine for the driver on the highway who actually needs it.

One simplex channel is pinned to memory: **146.520 MHz** is the national calling frequency for FM simplex operations on the 2-meter band (T2A02). A calling frequency is exactly what it sounds like — the channel everyone monitors when they have nobody particular to talk to. Raise someone there, and the courteous move is to slide the conversation to a nearby simplex channel so the calling frequency stays open for the next station. The exam's distractors live in other bands and other uses; 146.520 lives in your 2-meter band and nowhere else.

**FACT:** 146.520 MHz is the national calling frequency for FM simplex operations in the 2-meter band.

Those designations — the simplex channels, the calling frequency, the repeater sub-bands — come from a **band plan**, and the exam pins the definition precisely: a band plan is a voluntary guideline for using different modes or activities within an amateur band, beyond the privileges established by the FCC (T2A10). Voluntary is the load-bearing word. Part 97 does not contain a band plan; it only asks for good engineering and good amateur practice, and frequency coordinators merely recommend. Yet band plans are socially load-bearing — decades of custom and repeater coordination stand behind them — so treat a band plan as expected community behavior, never as an FCC mandate, and never as a suggestion you can ignore without consequence. Ignoring it will not bring an enforcement letter, but it will bring a crowded, unhappy repeater.

### Tones, codes, and access

Start with the receiver control you already use every day: the **squelch** mutes the receiver's audio when no signal is present (T2B13). Without it, your speaker would hiss with static around the clock. Ordinary **carrier squelch** opens for any signal strong enough to quiet the noise — which is exactly why the next invention was needed.

On a busy hilltop, dozens of conversations share the air, and a repeater that repeated everything it heard would be chaos. The fix is an access gate called **CTCSS**: a sub-audible tone transmitted along with your normal voice audio to open the squelch of a receiver (T2B02). Sub-audible means below what you notice hearing — a steady low hum, say 100.0 Hz, that your radio adds to your voice automatically once you program it. The repeater's squelch stays shut against everything except signals carrying the right tone; the right tone opens the gate and your voice is retransmitted. **DCS** is the digital equivalent: instead of a steady tone, your radio sends a slow stream of digital bits that plays the same gatekeeper role.

{{fig:ch06-ctcss-gate}}

Here is the wording law this book will not bend: CTCSS and DCS are **access control and selective calling, not privacy**. Every receiver in carrier squelch — every scanner, every other ham's open radio — hears the whole conversation on the repeater's output. The tone only decides whose signal gets *repeated*; it hides nothing from anyone listening. Old-timers say "PL," and now you know the folklore: PL was Motorola's 1951 trade name "Private Line," a marketing label that never described reality. The tone is a doorman, not a curtain.

**FACT:** CTCSS and DCS are access control and selective calling, not privacy — any receiver in carrier squelch hears the whole conversation, and "PL" is just Motorola's 1951 trade name "Private Line."

One practical commandment goes with that law: program tones by **frequency**, never by your radio's "tone number." A directory will tell you the repeater wants 100.0 Hz, and 100.0 Hz is what you enter — tone number 12 on one brand can be number 13 on another, while 100.0 Hz is 100.0 Hz everywhere.

Tones explain the classic beginner mystery, and the exam turns it into a question: you can hear a repeater's output — the machine is right there, loud and clear — but it never answers you. The usual suspects are a wrong offset, a wrong CTCSS tone, or a wrong DCS code, and the pool's keyed answer is all three (T2B04). Hearing the output only proves your receiver works; bringing the repeater up proves your transmitter is on the input, shifted the right direction, carrying the right key. When a machine ignores you, check those three settings in that order before you blame the radio.

Three more receiver-and-transmitter tricks round out daily repeater life. The **reverse function** swaps your transmit and receive frequencies so you are listening on the repeater's input — which means you hear the other station directly, machine and all removed (T2B01). If they come through strong on reverse, the two of you can move to simplex and give the repeater back to everyone else. **DTMF** signaling is the touch-tone language of your keypad: two simultaneous audio tones per keypress (T2B06), and it is how you drive repeater features such as an autopatch or an IRLP link. And if other operators report that your audio drops out on voice peaks, you are **overdeviating** — talking too loudly into an FM microphone — and the fix is beautifully mechanical: talk farther away from the microphone (T2B05). Back off an inch and speak across the mic, not into it.

Some machines do not work alone. In a **linked repeater network**, a signal received by one repeater is retransmitted by all the repeaters in the network (T2B03) — your handheld in one town can come out of mountaintops across the whole region. The etiquette follows from the reach: on a linked system you may be talking statewide, so keep transmissions concise and say where you are.

Digital machines have their own keys, and the exam wants the pool's definitions first. On DMR, a **color code** is an access code programmed into the radio to reach a specific repeater (T2B12). Learn it exactly that way; only then is it harmless to notice, as orientation, that a color code plays roughly the role a CTCSS tone plays on an analog machine — it is a number, not a color. A **talkgroup** is an identifier that organizes DMR traffic so listeners hear only their group (T2B14), and you join one by programming the group's ID into your radio (T2B07). Hams call the whole DMR configuration file a "codeplug," which tells you how much programming a digital machine expects.

Finally, the manners that keep a machine friendly. While it is in use, a repeater identifies itself in Morse or synthesized voice roughly every ten minutes — that call sign you hear announced out of nowhere is the machine doing its legal duty, not a person. After each user unkeys, you'll hear a short beep: the **courtesy tone**, the repeater's way of saying the floor is open. Leave a beat before you transmit so anyone — especially someone with emergency traffic — can break in. The rude opposite is **kerchunking**: keying the repeater without identifying, just to hear it respond. It is bad manners and an unidentified transmission in one. If you want to know whether you can hit the machine, say so out loud: your call sign followed by "testing" is legal, identifiable, and universally understood.

### Calling and conversation manners

Every contact begins the same way, and the exam tests the order: to call a specific station — on a repeater, or when answering a CQ anywhere — say the other station's call sign first, then identify with your own (T2A04, T2A05). Called station first, always. Picture an illustration: your friend is K3XYZ and you are W1ABC. You key the mic and say "K3XYZ, this is W1ABC." Their call gets their attention; your call tells them who is asking. Answering a CQ uses the identical pattern, because a CQ is simply someone asking to be called by anyone.

And what is a CQ? On simplex or HF phone, to raise any station at all, you repeat "CQ" a few times, say "this is" followed by your call sign, pause to listen, and repeat as needed (T2A06). The word itself means "calling any station" (T2A08) — an open invitation to whoever is listening. CQ belongs to the places where nobody owns the channel: the simplex calling frequency, the HF bands.

On a repeater, the custom changes, and the pool draws the line in ink. To indicate you are monitoring and open to a contact, you say your call sign followed by the word "listening" (T2A09) — "W1ABC listening." That is all. Do not call CQ on a repeater: CQ is for simplex and HF, not repeaters. The machine is already a gathering place with one shared channel; a long CQ call ties it up asking a question the room already answers. Say your call, say "listening," and anyone who wants you knows you are there.

**FACT:** On a repeater you indicate you are monitoring by saying your call sign followed by "listening" — calling CQ is for simplex and HF, not repeaters.

Sharing runs deeper than manners on one machine: nobody owns any frequency. No frequency is assigned for the exclusive use of any station, so when two stations keep interfering with each other, the expected behavior is to negotiate continued shared use (T2B08). "We were here first" is not a rule — it is not even an argument. If your favorite simplex channel is busy, pick another; if you keep doubling with someone, work out who transmits when. The spectrum is a commons, and the commons runs on negotiation.

You will also hear a compact slang built from three-letter **Q signals**, and the exam tests their meanings. QRM indicates that you are receiving interference from other stations (T2B10) — keep it distinct from its exam distractors: QRN is natural static, QSB is fading, and QTH is a location, not interference at all. QSY indicates that you are changing frequency (T2B11), as in "let's QSY to simplex." Two more are worth recognizing: QRZ asks "Who is calling me?", and QTH means "My location is ___" — "what is your QTH?" is "where are you?" Two more you will meet early, especially on HF: QSO is a contact, and QSL means "I acknowledge" — hence the QSL cards hams exchange to confirm their contacts. Here is the usage law: on FM voice, plain words are the custom. Q-signals earn their keep on CW and weak-signal work where every second costs; on the local repeater, "I'm getting interference" beats "I have QRM" every time. Learn the meanings for the exam and for recognition; speak plainly on FM.

While we are on plain speech: everyday FM signal reports are jargon, not a formal system. "You're full quieting" means your signal arrives strong and noise-free; "you're scratchy" means noise is riding along. The numbered RST report system belongs to SSB and CW — readability, strength, and tone numbers instead of plain words — and the pool tests neither system's vocabulary, so treat both as color for now.

### Nets and traffic

A **net** is an organized on-air meeting — same repeater, same time each week, a purpose. The form the exam cares about is the **directed net**, which has a boss: the Net Control Station, always abbreviated **NCS**. The NCS calls the net to order and directs communications between stations checking in (T2C02), and the discipline is one sentence: unless you are reporting an emergency, transmit only when directed by the NCS (T2C07). A directed net is not a conversation; it is a queue, and the NCS is the traffic light that keeps thirty stations from doubling into noise. You will meet the whole taxonomy in your first months: social and club nets, swap nets for buying and selling gear, training nets, ARES and RACES drills, and formal traffic nets — different purposes, the same directed-net manners underneath.

{{fig:ch06-net-flow}}

Checking in is deliberately small. Listen first — the NCS will announce the net and invite check-ins, usually by district, call-sign suffix, or simply "check-ins please." Then give your call sign, clearly and once. Short check-ins keep a big net moving; the NCS acknowledges each station in turn while everyone else listens. If you have nothing formal to pass, the whole transaction shrinks to your call sign plus "no traffic." Wait for the courtesy tone, leave room for doubles, and let the NCS run the show — those habits, learned on a friendly club net, are the same ones that keep an emergency net usable. You'll hear prowords doing traffic-light duty along the way — "over," "roger," "stand by," "clear" — inherited from formal procedure; on a friendly net they are seasoning, not law.

The word **traffic** in net operation has a specific meaning the exam pins: formal written messages exchanged by net stations (T2C05) — not chatter, not "traffic" as in cars. The standard envelope is the **radiogram**, and two pieces of its header are pool questions. The **preamble** is the block of information needed to track the message — number, precedence, origin, and so on (T2C10). The **check** is the number of words or word equivalents in the text portion of the message (T2C11), so the receiving operator can verify that nothing was lost in relay. When the exam asks what "check" means, it is not a checkbox and not a relay list — it is a word count.

Radiograms are also why the phonetic alphabet exists. Unusual words in a voice message are spelled with the standard phonetic alphabet to ensure correct receipt (T2C03) — names, street addresses, and any word where a misheard letter changes the meaning. The standard set is the ITU alphabet, and it is worth pinning all 26 words exactly: Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu. Yes, the ITU really spells them "Alfa" and "Juliett" — chosen to guide non-English pronunciation — and those are the spellings this book uses; you will see variants in old handouts, but the words themselves are what matter. Two pronunciation traps from the standard handout: Quebec is "keh-BECK" and Papa is "pah-PAH" — the stresses land where English ears do not expect them. Our illustrative W1ABC becomes "Whiskey One Alfa Bravo Charlie." On a quiet FM repeater, plain speech is fine; when conditions are rough or the word matters, phonetics turn a guess into a copy.

One more tool moves formal messages in and out of the internet: **Winlink** relays messages using email addresses based on amateur call signs (T2C08). Your Winlink address is built on your call sign, messages can travel over radio or internet links, and it is the emcomm tool new Technicians meet first at ARES exercises — the bridge between a served agency's inbox and a ham station with no internet at all.

### Public service and emergencies

Everything in this chapter operates under one umbrella rule, and it is the first question of the group: FCC rules always apply, including RACES, ARES, and FEMA operations (T2C01). No drill, no disaster, no served agency suspends Part 97. Emergency communications change what you talk about, never the rules you operate under.

Two organizations dominate ham public service, and the exam's trap is blending them. **ARES** — the Amateur Radio Emergency Service — is a body of licensed amateurs who have voluntarily registered their qualifications and equipment for public-service communications duty (T2C06). It is an ARRL program, and the door is open: any licensed amateur can join, train, and drill. **RACES** — the Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service — is the FCC Part 97 service for civil defense communications during national emergencies (T2C04), it operates only when activated by the government it serves, and operating a RACES station requires certification by a civil defense agency (T2C12). Keep the split clean: ARES is the volunteer body anyone can join; RACES is the government-certified service that stands up when activated.

Last, the emergency exception with the scary wording. Operating outside your license-class privileges is permitted only in situations involving the immediate safety of human life or the protection of property (T2C09). Read the bar for what it is: immediate, human life, protection of property — a stranger trapped and no other communication available, not a dead car battery and an inconvenient evening. In practice this essentially never happens in a Technician's career, you must still identify your station, and it certainly never applies to drills, which are ordinary operations under ordinary rules. Know where the door is; expect never to walk through it.

### Exam Focus

Subelement T2 (groups T2A–T2C) holds 37 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 3 of them — one per group. Here are seven, quoted exactly as the pool prints them, spanning all three groups; T2A01 pairs with the offset figure, T2B02 with the tone-gate figure, and T2C02 with the net-flow figure above.

> **T2A01** What is a common repeater frequency offset in the 2-meter band?
> A. Plus or minus 5 MHz
> B. Plus or minus 600 kHz
> C. Plus or minus 500 kHz
> D. Plus or minus 1 MHz
> **Answer: B** — 600 kHz is the common 2-meter split; choice A is the 70-centimeter value, planted as the deliberate band swap.

> **T2A02** What is the national calling frequency for FM simplex operations in the 2-meter band?
> A. 146.520 MHz
> B. 145.000 MHz
> C. 432.100 MHz
> D. 446.000 MHz
> **Answer: A** — 146.520 MHz, the one simplex frequency worth memorizing cold; the distractors live in other bands and uses.

> **T2A09** Which of the following is a customary way to indicate a station is listening on a repeater and looking for a contact?
> A. “CQ CQ” followed by the repeater’s call sign
> B. The station’s call sign followed by the word “listening”
> C. The repeater’s call sign followed by the station’s call sign
> D. “QSY” followed by your call sign
> **Answer: B** — on a repeater you say your call sign plus "listening"; CQ is for simplex and HF, which is exactly why choice A is the trap.

> **T2B02** What term describes the use of a sub-audible tone transmitted along with normal voice audio to open the squelch of a receiver?
> A. Carrier squelch
> B. Tone burst
> C. DTMF
> D. CTCSS
> **Answer: D** — CTCSS is the sub-audible tone riding with your voice; carrier squelch opens for any signal, and DTMF is keypad signaling.

> **T2B10** Which Q signal indicates that you are receiving interference from other stations?
> A. QRM
> B. QRN
> C. QTH
> D. QSB
> **Answer: A** — QRM is interference from other stations; QRN is natural static, QTH is a location, and QSB is fading.

> **T2C02** Which of the following are typical duties of a Net Control Station?
> A. Choose the regular net meeting time and frequency
> B. Ensure that all stations checking into the net are properly licensed for operation on the net frequency
> C. Call the net to order and direct communications between stations checking in
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: C** — the NCS runs the net itself: calls it to order and directs communications between the stations checking in.

> **T2C11** What is meant by “check” in a radiogram header?
> A. The number of words or word equivalents in the text portion of the message
> B. The call sign of the originating station
> C. A list of stations that have relayed the message
> D. A box on the message form that indicates that the message was received and/or relayed
> **Answer: A** — "check" is a word count of the text, so the receiving operator can verify nothing was lost in relay.

### Key Takeaways

- A repeater listens on its input and transmits on its output; the offset is the difference between the repeater's transmit and receive frequencies, and a common offset is plus or minus 600 kHz on 2 meters, plus or minus 5 MHz on 70 centimeters — community custom, never "the" offset.

- Simplex means transmitting and receiving on the same frequency; 146.520 MHz is the 2-meter national calling frequency for FM simplex, and band plans are voluntary community guidelines — expected behavior, never FCC mandates.

- CTCSS and DCS open a receiver's squelch: access control and selective calling, not privacy. Program tones by frequency (100.0 Hz), and if you hear a repeater but cannot bring it up, suspect a wrong offset, a wrong CTCSS tone, or a wrong DCS code — all three.

- Squelch mutes the receiver when no signal is present; the reverse function listens on the repeater's input so you can hear the other station directly; DTMF's two-tones-per-keypress drives autopatch and IRLP; on DMR, a color code reaches a specific repeater and a talkgroup ID joins a conversation.

- Call a specific station with the other station's call sign first, then yours; CQ means "calling any station" and belongs to simplex and HF — on a repeater, say your call sign plus "listening," and never call CQ.

- QRM is interference from other stations and QSY is changing frequency; QRZ asks "Who is calling me?" and QTH is "My location is ___" — but on FM voice, plain words are the custom.

- In a directed net, the Net Control Station calls the net to order and directs communications; transmit only when directed unless you are reporting an emergency, and "check" in a radiogram header counts the words in the text.

- FCC rules always apply, drills included; ARES is the ARRL volunteer body anyone can join, RACES is the FCC civil-defense service requiring agency certification, and operating outside your privileges is for immediate threats to human life or property only — essentially never.
