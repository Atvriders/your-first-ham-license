## 8. The Rules (Part 97, in Plain English)

Your grant has appeared in the FCC database, your radio is programmed, and your finger is hovering over the transmit button — when a perfectly reasonable worry arrives: am I actually allowed to do this? This chapter is the answer. It is a plain-English tour of Part 97, the FCC rule part that governs amateur radio, organized the way you will actually use it: what you may do, what you must do, and what you must never do. In this chapter you'll learn why the Amateur Radio Service exists at all; who writes and enforces its rules; what your license class, your grant, and your call sign really mean; exactly which frequencies, modes, and power levels a Technician may use; who the control operator is and why one is always required; how and when to identify your station; and which transmissions are prohibited. This chapter carries pool subelement T1 — 68 questions across groups T1A through T1F, 6 of them on your 35-question exam. It is the most memory-heavy chapter in the book, so we will lean on one organizing idea the whole way through.

### Why the service exists

Here is the organizing idea, worth memorizing before any single rule: the FCC wants amateur radio to be self-regulating, interference-free, non-commercial, and experimental. Keep those four ideas in mind and most of subelement T1 stops being memorization and starts being common sense, because nearly every "what is prohibited" and "what is required" answer follows from them. A service that experiments does not broadcast entertainment. A service that is non-commercial does not take payment or advertise. A service that self-regulates identifies itself, shares frequencies politely, and stays inside its allocations. A service that is interference-free never jams anyone, on purpose or by carelessness.

The official version of that idea lives in §97.1, the basis and purpose of the Amateur Radio Service, and it has exactly five prongs. In plain sentences: first, amateur radio is a voluntary noncommercial communication service whose value to the public shows up especially in providing emergency communications. Second, hams have a proven ability to contribute to the advancement of the radio art, and the service exists to continue and extend that. Third, the rules encourage and improve the service by advancing skills in both the communication and technical phases of the art. Fourth, the service expands the existing reservoir of trained operators, technicians, and electronics experts. Fifth, hams have a unique ability to enhance international goodwill, and the service continues and extends that too.

The exam tests the third prong almost word for word: "advancing skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art" is the keyed answer to T1A01. Study the distractors, because they teach the method. Providing personal radio communications for as many citizens as possible sounds noble, and providing communications for international contesting sounds like something hams genuinely do — but neither is in §97.1, which is exactly why "all these choices are correct" is bait on this question. Contesting is real and enormous fun (chapter 7 said so); it is simply not the legal purpose of the service. When a "which is part of the basis and purpose" question appears, match the wording against the five prongs and let nothing else in.

Who writes and enforces these rules? The FCC — the Federal Communications Commission — and nobody else (T1A02). The ARRL is a private membership society: a fine club, a publisher of books like the ones in your library, the organizer of contests and emergency volunteers, but not a regulator. Homeland Security is a different agency entirely. Once again "all these choices are correct" is the trap rather than the answer, and the pattern is worth flagging now because it runs through the whole subelement: "all of the above" is right only when every listed item is genuinely true.

One rule sits above all the operating rules: "No amateur operator shall willfully or maliciously interfere with or cause interference to any radio communication or signal" (§97.101(d), pool T1A11). There is no circumstance that makes deliberate interference acceptable — not a crowded repeater, not an on-air argument, nothing. The matching positive duty is that at all times and on all frequencies, each control operator must give priority to stations providing emergency communications (§97.101(c)). If a station is passing emergency traffic, you yield, every time, on every frequency. The rules call genuinely damaging interference **harmful interference**, and avoiding it is the deepest duty in the service.

### License classes, grants, and call signs

Five classes of amateur operator license exist in the rules, but the FCC currently issues new licenses for only three of them — and the exam asks exactly that.

**FACT:** Five classes of amateur operator license exist in the rules — Novice, Technician, General, Advanced, and Amateur Extra — but the FCC currently issues new licenses only for Technician, General, and Amateur Extra.

Novice, Advanced, and Technician Plus are legacy classes: people who hold them keep their privileges, but no new grants are issued. This question was revised in the February 2026 errata to the pool, so the exam now asks it directly — for which classes does the FCC currently issue new licenses? Technician, General, Amateur Extra (T1C01). Your own Technician ticket requires passing Element 2 only, the 35-question exam this book prepares you for; General adds Element 3 and Amateur Extra adds Element 4, both waiting for you later if you want them.

When do you actually have a license? Not when the volunteer examiners hand you the CSCE — the Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination — because a CSCE only proves you passed the exam. Your operating authority begins when your license grant appears in the FCC's ULS consolidated license database (T1A05, T1C10, §97.7(a)). Licensing is all-electronic now: the FCC emails you a link to download the official license grant, and no paper license arrives in the mail (T1A04). That download-link mechanism is current FCC practice as described by ARRL; the rule underneath it is §97.23, which requires every grant to show a correct name, mailing address, and email address — and warns that revocation of the station license or suspension of the operator license may result when FCC correspondence comes back undeliverable (T1C04). So the first chore of license maintenance is wonderfully boring: keep your email address current with the FCC, forever.

Then come three clocks, and the exam loves to mix them up, so keep them straight. The renewal window opens 90 days before your expiration date (T1C07) — earlier than that, the FCC will not accept the renewal. The license term itself is 10 years (T1C08, §97.25). And if your license does expire, a 2-year grace period lets you renew anyway (T1C09) — but with a sharp edge that the exam tests twice: during the grace period you may renew, and you may not transmit until the renewal is granted (T1C11, §97.21(b)). Ninety days, ten years, two years. Say them out loud a few times and you have banked three exam answers.

Your call sign is not random; it is a tiny piece of geography and license history. A US amateur call sign has three parts: a prefix of one letter (K, N, or W) or two letters, a numeral from 0 to 9 indicating the call district — 1 for New England, 6 for California, 0 for the tenth region — and a suffix of one to three letters. The sequential call sign system hands these out from four groups, pinned by the FCC's Amateur Call Sign Systems page: Group A for Amateur Extra (formats like 1×2 and 2×1), Group B for Advanced (2×2 starting with K, N, or W), Group C for General and Technician (1×3, like K1XXX), and Group D for Novice, club, and military recreation stations (2×3 with a K- or W-first prefix, like KF1XXX). The exam asks which is a valid Group D call sign format for a Technician and keys KF1XXX (T1C05). The distractors are not gibberish — KA1X is a genuine 2×2 Group B format and W1XX is a genuine 1×2 Group A format — they simply belong to other groups. A Technician may hold Group C or Group D formats, which is why your first call sign will most likely look like KF1XXX's family: two letters, a numeral, three letters.

If you would rather choose your own call sign — your initials, a combination with personal meaning, anything available — any licensed amateur may apply for a vanity call sign (T1C02, §97.19(a)). The same group eligibility applies, so a Technician can request Group C or D formats. And one piece of vocabulary the rules use constantly: your license is formally an operator/primary station license grant — one grant that makes you both a licensed operator and the licensee of your own station.

Two travel notes round out this group. Operating from a ship or an aircraft requires the approval of the master of the ship or the pilot in command of the aircraft (T1C06, §97.11(a)) — the captain's word governs, not the FCC's. And international communications are limited to communications incidental to the purposes of the amateur service plus remarks of a personal character (T1C03, §97.117): hobby talk and personal chat cross borders freely; business content does not.

### Your privileges: bands, modes, and power

Now the part every new Technician actually wants: where may you transmit, with what modes, and how loud? Start with the good news. On VHF and up, Technicians share the same allocations as every higher license class. In ITU Region 2, which covers the Americas, the everyday bands are 6 meters at 50–54 MHz, 2 meters at 144–148 MHz, 1.25 meters at 219–220 and 222–225 MHz, and 70 centimeters at 420–450 MHz, with more bands above those (§97.301(a)). The band names come from wavelength — chapter 3's shortcut, wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz. Divide 300 by 146 and you get about 2, which is why 144–148 MHz answers to "2 meters."

The exam checks band membership with two specific numbers, and they are worth memorizing as numbers, not just ideas: 52.525 MHz is inside the 6-meter band (T1B03), and 146.52 MHz is inside the 2-meter band (T1B04). Notice that both questions are really the same skill — compare a frequency against two band edges and see whether it falls between them. The worked example below practices that skill once more, at the edge where it gets interesting.

HF is where Technician privileges narrow to careful slices. On 80 meters you get 3.525–3.600 MHz, on 40 meters 7.025–7.125 MHz, and on 15 meters 21.025–21.200 MHz — and on those three segments a station with a Novice or Technician control operator may only transmit a CW emission using the international Morse code (T1B06, §97.307(f)(9)). No phone on 80, 40, or 15 meters for a Technician, ever. Then comes 10 meters, the Technician's HF playground: 28.0–28.5 MHz, with CW and data on 28.0–28.3 MHz and SSB phone on 28.300–28.500 MHz (T1B01). That 200 kHz of 10-meter phone is the only HF voice a Technician may transmit, and the exam guards its edges fiercely.

**FACT:** Technician phone operation on HF exists only on 10 meters, 28.300–28.500 MHz, where Novice and Technician control operators may transmit CW or SSB phone (emissions J3E and R3E).

Modes have their own map, and three exam answers live on it. Data emissions — the digital modes like FT8 that chapter 7 taught — are authorized for Technicians on 10 meters, on 6 meters, and on 2 meters: all three (T1B05, §97.305(c)). SSB phone may be used in at least some segment of every amateur band above 50 MHz (T1B10), so your voice is welcome everywhere from 6 meters upward. And CW is authorized on any frequency where the control operator has privileges (§97.305(a)) — while the phone and data tables on 6 meters and 2 meters begin at 50.1 MHz and 144.1 MHz. Do the arithmetic and the bottom 100 kHz of each band, 50.0–50.1 MHz and 144.0–144.1 MHz, comes out CW-only (T1B07). Those CW-only bottoms are emission-rule arithmetic, not a band plan. Band plans, calling frequencies, and repeater offsets are voluntary community practice — load-bearing customs that the rules encourage but never mandate. The rule books you must obey are the allocation tables; the band plans are how the community organizes itself inside them.

Sharing has a rule of its own. On some bands amateur radio is the secondary service, and there you may encounter non-amateur stations — government radiolocation on 70 centimeters is the classic case — and you must avoid interfering with them (T1B08, §97.303). A secondary service must not cause harmful interference to, and must accept interference from, stations in the primary service. If the primary user shows up, the frequency is theirs.

Wherever you operate, stay off the exact band or sub-band edge. The exam lists three separate reasons and keys "all these choices are correct" for once, honestly (T1B09): your display calibration may be off, your modulation sidebands extend past your displayed frequency, and your transmitter can drift. The rule behind all three is §97.307(b): emissions resulting from modulation must be confined to the band or segment available to the control operator. Your sidebands count, not just the number on the display — which is exactly the arithmetic the worked example does.

> **Worked example:** Is 52.525 MHz inside the 6-meter band? The band runs 50–54 MHz, and 52.525 sits comfortably between the edges, so yes (T1B03). Now the edge case. Your 10-meter SSB signal is centered at 28.499 MHz, and your sidebands run about 3 kHz above the carrier. Convert 3 kHz to megahertz — 0.003 MHz — and add: 28.499 + 0.003 = 28.502 MHz. That is past the 28.500 MHz band edge, and because sidebands count, you would be transmitting outside the band. Back down a few kHz: center the signal at 28.495 MHz and the highest sideband lands at 28.498 MHz, safely inside. One line of arithmetic is the whole lesson of T1B09.

Power has two numbers and one philosophy. The philosophy comes first and it is a direct quote: "An amateur station must use the minimum transmitter power necessary to carry out the desired communications" (§97.313(a)). The legal maximum is a ceiling you stay under, not a target you aim for. The two numbers are measured in PEP — peak envelope power, the transmitter's average power during one RF cycle at the very crest of the modulation envelope (§97.3(b)(9)); the plain-English version is that PEP measures your signal at its loudest peaks, which is why it is the honest way to rate an SSB signal that swells and dips with your voice.

**FACT:** The maximum peak envelope power output for Technician class operators in their HF band segments is 200 watts.

Above 30 MHz the ceiling rises dramatically: 1500 watts PEP — one and a half kilowatts — but the exam's own wording carries a caveat you must always carry with it: except for some specific restrictions (T1B12, §97.313(b)). The 1500 W figure is the general amateur ceiling, never a band-by-band grant. Specific bands and places impose lower limits — 50 W PEP on 219–220 MHz per §97.313(h) is the example to remember — and those restrictions win wherever they apply. Two exam answers, then: 200 W PEP in your HF segments, 1500 W PEP above 30 MHz with specific restrictions, and minimum necessary power everywhere, always.

Two privileges deserve their own mention because beginners consistently underrate them. Automatically controlled HF propagation beacons live on 10 meters, between 28.200 MHz and 28.300 MHz (T1A06, §97.203(d)) — listen for them and you can hear with your own ears whether the band is open to distant places before you ever transmit. And any US amateur with a Technician class or higher license may contact the International Space Station (T1B02): the rules at §97.207(a) and §97.209(a) make any license class eligible to be the control operator of a space or Earth station, subject to the privileges of their class. Satellites are not an Extra-class playground; your Technician ticket is already a satellite license, exactly as chapter 7 promised.

### The control operator and the three control types

Every transmission an amateur station makes has a named human responsible for it. That human is the control operator, and the first rule about control operators is absolute.

**FACT:** When transmitting, each amateur station must have a control operator — there is no exception, not even for automatically controlled stations.

The exam asks when a station may transmit without a control operator and keys a single word: never (T1E01, §97.7). Not when the repeater is running itself, not when the beacon is on automatic, not ever. So who is this person? A control operator is an amateur operator designated by the licensee of a station to be responsible for the transmissions from that station (T1E11, §97.3(a)(13)). The station licensee does the designating (T1E03, §97.103(b)), and the FCC presumes the licensee is the control operator unless station records document otherwise. In your own shack, that is all invisible: you are the licensee, you are the control operator, and the responsibility is yours by default.

The pivotal rule is the one beginners trip over: a station's transmitting privileges are those of the control operator's license class — not the station licensee's, not the highest class of anyone standing nearby (T1E04). The on-point rule text is §97.105(b): a station may only be operated in the manner and to the extent permitted by the privileges authorized for the class of operator license held by the control operator, with §97.103(b) covering the licensee's duty to designate that operator. The consequences follow immediately. An Extra-class licensee cannot lend you Extra privileges by designating you: a Technician may never be the control operator of a station transmitting in an Amateur Extra–only segment (T1E06, emergencies aside). And responsibility is shared — when the control operator is a different amateur than the station licensee, both persons are equally responsible for proper operation of the station (T1E07, §97.103(a)). Your friend operating your radio does not take the responsibility off you; it doubles the people holding it.

Where does the control operator function happen? At the control point — the location at which the control operator function is performed (T1E05, §97.3(a)(14)). The control point need not be where the transmitter sits, which is exactly what makes the three control types possible.

Local control is the ordinary kind: you are physically at the station, hands on the radio, and the control point is your operating position. Remote control means you operate the station indirectly, through a control link — for example over the internet (T1E10, §97.3(a)(39)) — and any amateur station may be remotely controlled (T1E09, §97.109(c)). Your control point is wherever you sit with the link, and you remain the control operator the whole time. Automatic control means the station operates itself through devices and procedures, with no control operator present at a control point — and only the station types specifically designated in Part 97 may use it, with repeater operation as the canonical example the exam keys (T1E08, §97.109(d), §97.205(d)). Notice how T1E01 survives intact: even an automatically controlled repeater has a designated control operator who is responsible for it; automatic control means the operator need not be present, not that no operator exists.

One eligibility rule ties this group back to satellites: any amateur authorized to transmit on a satellite's uplink frequency may be the control operator of a station communicating through that satellite (T1E02, §97.207(a) and §97.209(a)). Since Technicians may transmit on the VHF and UHF uplinks the satellites use, the satellite control operator can be you, on your first day with a license.

### Identification: the ten-minute rule

Amateur radio self-regulates, and self-regulation starts with every station saying who it is. The rule is short enough to memorize word for word.

**FACT:** Every amateur station (except a space or telecommand station) must transmit its assigned call sign "at the end of each communication, and at least every 10 minutes during a communication."

That is §97.119(a) and pool T1F03: every 10 minutes during a contact, and at the end of it. The exam's distractors move the requirement to the beginning of each contact or stretch the interval to 15 minutes; the real rule is 10 minutes and the end. Tactical call signs fit inside this rule rather than replacing it. At a public-service event you may call yourself "Race Headquarters" or "Mile Marker 5" for convenience (T1F02) — it makes the net run smoothly — but a tactical call never substitutes for the FCC-assigned call sign, which still goes out every 10 minutes and at the end of each communication.

The how has four tested details. Phone identification must be in the English language (T1F04, §97.119(b)(2)). Using a phonetic alphabet — Kilo Foxtrot One X-ray X-ray X-ray — is encouraged as an aid to correct identification, but encouraged, not required (T1A03). The required ID may be sent by CW or by phone emission; either satisfies the rule for phone operation (T1F05, §97.119(b)(1)–(2)). And when you add a self-assigned indicator such as "mobile" or "portable 3," the separator may be spoken as "stroke," "slant," or "slash" — all are acceptable (T1F06, §97.119(c)).

Two ID questions make a contrast pair the exam loves. Transmissions directed only at controlling a model craft are exempt from station identification, provided a label with the call sign and the licensee's name and address is affixed to the transmitter and the power stays at 1 W or less (T1D11, §97.215). But on-the-air test transmissions — even a two-second "is this thing working?" into a dummy antenna — must identify with your call sign, because no station may transmit unidentified communications or signals (T1D12, §97.119(a)). The model-craft exemption is the exception; the test-transmission rule is the habit.

### What you must never transmit

Section §97.113 is the prohibited-transmissions section, and the organizing idea predicts almost all of it: a non-commercial, two-way, experimental service does not broadcast, does not do business, and does not hide its meaning. Work through the list once and the exam's dozen questions stop looking like a list at all.

Start with the borders. Amateur stations may not exchange communications with a country whose administration has notified the ITU that it objects to such communications (T1D01, §97.111(a)(1)) — that ITU notification is the one and only country-based prohibition in the rules. Neither the ARRL nor any club has the power to put a country off limits; only the objecting administration's own notification does.

Broadcasting is the big one, and it has a precise definition: transmissions intended for reception by the general public, either direct or relayed (T1D10, §97.3(a)(10)). An amateur station shall not engage in any form of broadcasting, nor transmit one-way communications except as specifically provided in the rules (T1D02, §97.113(b)). Those specifically provided one-way transmissions form an enumerated list — emergency communications, Morse code practice, telecommand and telemetry, brief adjustment transmissions, transmissions to establish two-way contact, information bulletins — and the exam's favorite trap, announcements of upcoming ham radio operating events, is not on it. You may talk about the hamfest in a two-way contact; you may not broadcast a one-way announcement to whoever is listening.

Hidden meanings are next. Amateur stations may not transmit messages encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning, and the tested exceptions are exactly two: telecommand to space stations and control signals to model craft (T1D03; §97.113(a)(4), §97.211(b), §97.215(b)). Published digital codes are fine — anyone can decode FT8 or packet with free software, so nothing is obscured. Obscurity is the test, not the presence of a code. Music is narrower still: prohibited except when incidental to an authorized retransmission of manned spacecraft communications, which itself requires prior NASA approval (T1D04; §97.113(a)(4), §97.113(c)). The exception is absurdly narrow on purpose — pin it as the exception and answer "no music" everywhere else. Obscene or indecent words or language are prohibited categorically, and there is no FCC list of prohibited words to memorize (T1D06, §97.113(a)(4)); the prohibition itself is the whole rule.

Money follows the non-commercial principle with two tested carve-outs. You may not transmit communications in which you have a pecuniary interest — but an amateur operator may notify other amateurs of the availability for sale or trade of apparatus normally used in an amateur station, provided the activity is not conducted on a regular basis (T1D05, §97.113(a)(3)(ii)). Occasional swap-and-shop for your own gear, yes; running a store on the repeater, no. And a control operator may accept compensation for operating only as an incident of a teaching position, when the station is used as part of classroom instruction at an educational institution (T1D08, §97.113(a)(3)(iii)) — the teacher using ham radio in class is the one case where pay and operating mix. Communications supporting broadcasting or news gathering are allowed only when directly related to the immediate safety of human life or the protection of property, and only where no other means of communication is reasonably available (T1D09, §97.113(b)) — the pool's printed citation tag on that question contains a typo, but the operative rule is §97.113(b), and the idea is simple: in a genuine emergency you may help; as a routine news-gathering service, never.

Retransmission has its own short list. Only auxiliary, repeater, or space stations may automatically retransmit the radio signals of other amateur stations (T1D07, §97.113(d)) — an auxiliary station being the point-to-point link inside a cooperating system, such as the link that carries a remote receiver's audio back to the main repeater transmitter. A repeater, by definition, simultaneously retransmits another amateur station's transmission on a different channel (T1F09, §97.3(a)(40)). And if a repeater inadvertently retransmits communications that violate the rules — someone swears on the input, say — accountability falls on the originating station's control operator, not the repeater's (T1F10, §97.205(g)).

Third-party communications complete the picture: a message from the control operator (first party) of one amateur station to another amateur station's control operator (second party) on behalf of another person — the third party, typically a non-licensed friend or family member (T1F08, §97.3(a)(47)). Inside the United States that is routine; with a foreign station it is allowed only where that country's administration has made a third-party agreement with the United States (T1F07, §97.115(a)(2)).

Three accountability rules close the subelement. The licensee must make the station and its records available for inspection at any time upon request by an FCC representative (T1F01, §97.103(c)) — no warrant, no ten-day notice, at any time. A club station license grant requires at least four persons, plus a name, a document of organization, management, and a primary purpose devoted to the amateur service (T1F11, §97.5(b)(2)). And the people who keep repeaters from interfering with each other — frequency coordinators — are volunteers recognized in a local or regional area by the amateurs whose stations are eligible to be repeater or auxiliary stations; they recommend transmit and receive channels and operating parameters, and they are not appointed by the FCC or anyone else in government (T1A08, T1A09, §97.3(a)(22)). Two definitions round out the vocabulary: a space station is an amateur station located more than 50 km above the Earth's surface (T1A07, §97.3(a)(41)) — pin the 50 km — and a RACES control operator needs, beyond the amateur license itself, certification by a civil defense organization as enrolled in that organization (T1A10, §97.407(a)), which is the same civil-defense certification chapter 6 met in passing.

### Exam Focus

Subelement T1 — groups T1A through T1F — holds 68 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 6 of them, one per group. Here are nine, quoted exactly as the pool prints them and spanning all six groups. T1C01 appears in its revised (February 2026) form; T1B01 pairs with the band chart and T1C05 with the call-sign anatomy figure.

> **T1A01** Which of the following is part of the Basis and Purpose of the Amateur Radio Service?
> A. Providing personal radio communications for as many citizens as possible
> B. Providing communications for international contesting
> C. Advancing skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: C** — advancing skills in both phases of the art is §97.1's third prong nearly word for word; contesting and personal communications sound noble but are not among the five prongs.

> **T1A02** Which agency regulates and enforces the rules for the Amateur Radio Service in the United States?
> A. ARRL
> B. Homeland Security
> C. The FCC
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: C** — the FCC regulates and enforces; the ARRL is a private membership society with no regulatory power.

> **T1B01** Which of the following frequency ranges are available for phone operation by Technician licensees?
> A. 28.050 MHz to 28.150 MHz
> B. 28.100 MHz to 28.300 MHz
> C. 28.300 MHz to 28.500 MHz
> D. 28.500 MHz to 28.600 MHz
> **Answer: C** — Technician HF phone exists only on 10 meters, 28.300–28.500 MHz; the ranges below 28.3 MHz are CW and data territory, and 28.500 MHz is the band edge itself.

> **T1B11** What is the maximum peak envelope power output for Technician class operators in their HF band segments?
> A. 200 watts
> B. 100 watts
> C. 50 watts
> D. 10 watts
> **Answer: A** — 200 watts PEP is the Technician ceiling in the HF segments, per §97.313(c)(2).

> **T1C01** For which classes of amateur radio licenses does the FCC currently issue new licenses?
> A. Novice, Technician, General, Amateur Extra
> B. Technician, Technician Plus, General, Amateur Extra
> C. Novice, Technician Plus, General, Advanced
> D. Technician, General, Amateur Extra
> **Answer: D** — five classes exist in the rules, but new licenses issue only for Technician, General, and Amateur Extra; Novice, Advanced, and Technician Plus are legacy classes.

> **T1C05** Which of the following is a valid Group D call sign format for Technician class?
> A. KF1XXX
> B. KA1X
> C. W1XX
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: A** — KF1XXX is the 2×3 Group D format; KA1X and W1XX are genuine formats of other groups, so "all these choices" is bait.

> **T1D02** Under which of the following circumstances are one-way transmissions by an amateur station prohibited?
> A. Announcements of upcoming ham radio operating events
> B. Broadcasting
> C. International Morse Code Practice
> D. Telecommand or transmissions of telemetry
> **Answer: B** — broadcasting is always prohibited; Morse practice and telecommand or telemetry sit on the enumerated list of permitted one-way transmissions, while ham-event announcements notably do not.

> **T1E01** When may an amateur station transmit without a control operator?
> A. When using automatic control, such as in the case of a repeater
> B. When the station licensee is away and another licensed amateur is using the station
> C. When the transmitting station is an auxiliary station
> D. Never
> **Answer: D** — never; a control operator is required for every transmission, even under automatic control.

> **T1F03** When are you required to transmit your assigned call sign?
> A. At the beginning of each contact, and every 10 minutes thereafter
> B. At least once during each transmission
> C. At least every 15 minutes during and at the end of a communication
> D. At least every 10 minutes during and at the end of a communication
> **Answer: D** — the rule is every 10 minutes during a communication and at its end; the distractors move it to the beginning or stretch the interval to 15 minutes.

### Key Takeaways

- The basis and purpose of the Amateur Radio Service is five prongs in §97.1 — emergency communications, advancing the radio art, advancing skills in both the communication and technical phases of the art, a trained-operator reservoir, and international goodwill — and the exam's keyed prong is advancing skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art.

- The FCC — not the ARRL, not Homeland Security — regulates and enforces the rules; willful or malicious interference is always prohibited, and emergency communications get priority at all times and on all frequencies.

- Five license classes exist in the rules, but the FCC issues new licenses only for Technician, General, and Amateur Extra; your authority begins when the grant appears in the ULS, the term is 10 years, renewal opens 90 days before expiration, and the 2-year grace period lets you renew but not transmit.

- Identify with your FCC-assigned call sign at least every 10 minutes during a communication and at its end; phone ID is in English, phonetics are encouraged not required, and tactical call signs never replace the assigned call sign.

- Technician HF phone exists only on 10 meters, 28.300–28.500 MHz; the 80-, 40-, and 15-meter Technician segments are CW-only; on VHF and up you share the same allocations as every license class, with the bottom 100 kHz of 6 meters and 2 meters CW-only by emission-rule arithmetic.

- Power: 200 W PEP in the Technician HF segments, 1500 W PEP above 30 MHz except for some specific restrictions — a general ceiling, never a band-by-band grant — and always the minimum power necessary for the communication.

- A control operator is required for every transmission, with no exception; the station's transmitting privileges are those of the control operator's license class, and when operator and licensee differ, both are equally responsible.

- Never broadcast, never transmit for pay or business, never send messages encoded to obscure their meaning (the tested exceptions are telecommand to space stations and model-craft control), and never set your transmit frequency exactly at a band edge.
