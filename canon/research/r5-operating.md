## 1. Repeater life (Chapter 06)

*The daily-driver world most new Techs enter first: machines, tones, offsets, and the manners around them.*

- FACT — Common 2 m repeater offset: **±600 kHz**. Common 70 cm offset: **±5 MHz**. "Offset" = the difference between the repeater's transmit and receive frequencies. Modern radios auto-shift once you pick the output frequency, but "odd splits" exist near band edges — which is why the pool says "a common" offset, not "the" offset. (pool T2A01, T2A03, T2A07) `[pool-supported: T2A01, T2A03, T2A07]`
- Jargon: the repeater is "the machine"; the frequency it listens on is the "input" (where you transmit), the frequency it transmits on is the "output" (where you listen). (hamuniverse.com/repeaterterminology.html) `[practice]`
- Where beginners find machines and tones: club websites and online repeater directories (RepeaterBook is the de facto one) list output frequency, offset direction, and CTCSS tone per repeater. `[practice: repeaterbook.com]`
- FACT — **146.520 MHz** is the national calling frequency for FM simplex on 2 m. (pool T2A02; pool T1B04 places 146.52 in the 2 m band) `[pool-supported: T2A02]`
- Say this: to call a specific station, THEIR call sign, then yours — "W1ABC, this is K3XYZ" (the same pattern answers a CQ anywhere). To just be noticed on a repeater: **"<call sign> listening"** (or "monitoring"). Do NOT call CQ on a repeater — CQ is for simplex and HF; "listening" is the repeater custom. `[pool-supported: T2A04, T2A05, T2A09, T2A06, T2A08]`
- You'll hear: the repeater ID'ing itself in Morse or synthesized voice roughly every 10 minutes while in use (FCC 47 CFR 97.119(a) sets the every-10-minutes ID rule), and a "courtesy tone" (beep) after each user unkeys — wait for the beep / leave a beat between overs so anyone, especially someone with emergency traffic, can break in (hvnet.org's preamble instructs check-ins to wait for the courtesy tone). The rude opposite is **"kerchunking"**: keying the repeater without identifying — an unidentified transmission that "violates amateur radio regulations" (hamuniverse.com/repeaterterminology.html). To test whether you can hit the machine, say "<call sign> testing"; the pool itself requires ID even for on-air test transmissions. `[pool-supported: T1D12, T1D11]` `[practice]`
- Simplex = transmit and receive on the same frequency. Band plans designate simplex channels so stations in range of each other can talk without tying up a repeater; the practical habit is to move a ragchew to simplex when you're in direct range. The "reverse" button listens on the repeater's input — that's how you check whether you can hear the other station directly. `[pool-supported: T2A11, T2B09, T2B01]`
- VHF/UHF voice repeaters run FM (or PM); squelch mutes the receiver when no signal is present. Everyday FM signal reports are "full quieting" (strong, noise-free) or "you're scratchy" — jargon, not the RST system (see §6). If your audio drops out on voice peaks, you're talking too loudly (overdeviation): back off the mic an inch and speak across it, not into it. `[pool-supported: T8A04, T2B13, T2B05]` `[practice: FM report jargon]`
- FACT — CTCSS = a sub-audible tone sent with your voice to open the repeater's squelch; DCS is the digital-bitstream equivalent. If you can hear a repeater but can't bring it up, the three usual suspects are wrong offset, wrong CTCSS tone, wrong DCS code. Program tones by FREQUENCY (e.g., 100.0 Hz), never by radio "tone number" — tone #12 on one brand can be #13 on another. (pool T2B02, T2B04; repeater-builder.com "Notes on Tones and Tone Selection") `[pool-supported: T2B02, T2B04]` `[practice]`
- FACT — CTCSS/DCS is access control and selective calling, **NOT privacy**. "PL" comes from Motorola's 1951 trade name "Private Line," but a receiver in carrier squelch (and every scanner) hears the whole conversation. (repeater-builder.com tone-squelch overview: "PL does NOT make a repeater closed or private!"; "the requirement to use a subaudible tone or a DCS code to access a repeater does NOT mean it is a closed or private repeater") `[practice]` — consistent with the pool's functional framing (T2B02).
- DTMF — two simultaneous audio tones per keypress — is how you drive repeater features: autopatch where offered, and IRLP linking (see §4). `[pool-supported: T2B06, T8C06]`
- Linked repeater networks: a signal received by one repeater is transmitted by all of them — on a linked system you may be talking statewide, so keep it concise and say where you are. `[pool-supported: T2B03]` `[practice]`
- On DMR repeaters you join a "talkgroup" by programming its ID into your radio; the "color code" is simply the access code for that specific repeater — functionally DMR's analog of a CTCSS tone, not a color. DMR time-multiplexes two voice signals on one 12.5 kHz channel. Hams call the whole DMR config file a "codeplug." `[pool-supported: T2B07, T2B12, T2B14, T8D07]` `[practice: codeplug]`
- Two stations keep doubling on one frequency? The expected behavior is to negotiate continued use — "we were here first" is not a rule. `[pool-supported: T2B08]`
- Band plans (where repeater, simplex, weak-signal, and satellite sub-bands live) are voluntary guidelines layered on FCC privileges — but repeater coordination and decades of custom make them socially load-bearing. See Watch item 1. `[pool-supported: T2A10]`

## 2. Nets: how they actually run (Chapter 06)

*Directed nets are where new hams learn discipline; the NCS runs the show and short check-ins keep it moving.*

- A "net" is an organized on-air meeting. In a DIRECTED net, the Net Control Station (NCS) calls the net to order and directs communications between stations checking in; unless you are reporting an emergency, transmit only when directed by NCS. `[pool-supported: T2C02, T2C07]`
- FACT — Real NCS preamble, verbatim from the Hudson Valley Net (hvnet.org, an ARRL NTS local net): "This is a directed net and all communication will be directed through Net Control for the duration of the net." Expect a scripted opening like this on any formal net. `[pool-supported: T2C02]` `[practice]`
- Standard check-in technique on a busy repeater net: wait for NCS to invite check-ins, then give your call sign — one formal variant is "Here is...", un-key, wait ~3 seconds to catch doubles, then call sign + name + whether you have traffic. (ema.arrl.org net protocol) `[practice]` — supports the discipline tested in T2C07.
- Another real check-in instruction, from the same HVN preamble: "call this station..., drop your carrier to check for doubling, then after the courtesy tone, please indicate your call sign and your call sign only; give specific information when instructed." Short check-ins keep a big net moving. (hvnet.org) `[practice]`
- "Traffic" in net-speak = formal written messages exchanged by net stations, not chit-chat. Checking in with nothing to pass, you say "<call sign>, no traffic." `[pool-supported: T2C05]` `[practice: check-in phrase]`
- Traffic nets log their work in Q-signal abbreviations: real NTS net statistics are reported as QNI (check-ins), QTC (traffic listed), QSP (traffic passed), QND (net duration in minutes) — see the Hudson Valley Net's monthly tables. (hvnet.org) `[practice]`
- You'll hear prowords: "over," "roger," "clear," "stand by." ARRL communications procedures map them to CW prosigns (GO AHEAD = K, OVER = AR, ROGER = R, CLEAR = SK) — useful when the same operator works voice and CW nets. (ema.arrl.org "ARRL Communications Procedures") `[practice]`
- Radiogram nuts and bolts the pool tests: the PREAMBLE is the block of information needed to track the message (number, precedence, origin...); the "check" is the number of words or word-equivalents in the text portion. `[pool-supported: T2C10, T2C11]`
- Unusual words in a voice message get spelled with the standard phonetic alphabet (§3) — the pool's stated reason phonetics exist, and the reason traffic handlers drill them. `[pool-supported: T2C03]`
- ARES = licensed amateurs who have voluntarily registered their qualifications and equipment for public-service communications duty (an ARRL program — any ham can join and drill). RACES = an FCC Part 97 service for civil defense communications during national emergencies, and it requires certification by a civil defense agency. `[pool-supported: T2C06, T2C04, T2C12]`
- Winlink relays messages using email addresses based on amateur call signs — over RF or internet — and is the emcomm tool new Techs meet first at ARES exercises. `[pool-supported: T2C08]` `[practice]`
- Net taxonomy a beginner will meet: social/club nets, "swap nets" (buy/sell), training nets, ARES/RACES drills, NTS traffic nets. Same directed-net manners on all of them. Reference for NTS net operations: ARRL NTS Methods and Practices Guidelines (arrl.org/files/file/NTS_MPG2014.pdf — the working reference for traffic-net procedure). `[practice]`
- FCC rules always apply — nets, drills, and emergencies included (T2C01). Operating outside your license privileges is permitted only in situations involving immediate safety of human life or protection of property (T2C09); in practice this is vanishingly rare, and you must still identify. `[pool-supported: T2C01, T2C09]` `[practice]`

## 3. Phonetics & Q-signals (Chapters 06–07)

*Vocabulary to recognize and use precisely — pinned word lists here are exam-safe.*

- FACT — Standard ITU phonetic alphabet, the "standard phonetic alphabet" of T2C03. Pin the exact 26 words:
  **Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu.**
  (ITU spellings "Alfa"/"Juliett" per ITU phonetic tables, e.g. rmrl.org/dl/ITU_PHONETICS.pdf; full word list also in the ema.arrl.org "Standard ITU Phonetic Alphabet" handout and ARRL NTS procedures page. Spelling variants in print: see Watch item 4.) `[pool-supported: T2C03]`
- Pronunciation gotchas worth a sidebar (ema.arrl.org Standard ITU Phonetics handout): Quebec = "keh-BECK," Papa = "pah-PAH," Juliett = "JEW-lee-ett," Hotel = "hoh-TELL." The stresses fall where English ears don't expect them. `[practice]`
- Usage: phonetics for call signs and for spelling unusual words when conditions are rough or the word matters (names, addresses, radiogram text). On a quiet FM repeater, plain speech is fine. `[pool-supported: T2C03]` `[practice]`
- FACT — Q-signals the pool tests: **QRM = receiving interference from other stations** (pool T2B10); **QSY = changing frequency** (pool T2B11). `[pool-supported: T2B10, T2B11]`
- FACT — **QRZ = "Who is calling me?"**; **QTH = "My location is ___"** (question form: "What is your location?"). (ARRL "Communicating with Other Hams — Q Signals" PDF, arrl.org/files/file/Get%20on%20the%20Air/Comm%20w%20Other%20Hams-Q%20Signals.pdf) `[practice]`
- You'll hear Q-signals as nouns and verbs on the air, exactly as the ARRL Q-signal page notes: "'QRZed?' as someone asks 'Who is calling me?' or 'I'm getting a little QRM' from an operator receiving some interference or 'Let's QSY to 146.55' as two operators change from a repeater frequency to a nearby simplex" frequency. (same ARRL PDF) `[practice]`
- Other Q-signals a new ham will meet on HF/CW: QRN (static), QSL ("I acknowledge / confirm" — hence QSL cards, the written confirmations hams exchange; even ISS contacts can be QSL'd per ariss.org), QSO (a contact), QRT (going off the air), QRP — in ham usage, low-power operating, drifting from its treaty meaning "reduce power" (ARRL Sweepstakes even has QRP/Low/High entry categories — local: ~/arrl-calendar/src/data/rules.ts ss entry). `[practice]`
- Usage color: on CW and weak-signal SSB these abbreviations earn their keep; on FM phone, plain English is the custom (ARRL NTS guidance for voice nets is "say it with words" — NTS MPG, arrl.org/files/file/NTS_MPG2014.pdf). Frame Q-signals in ch 06/07 as vocabulary to recognize, not seasoning for repeater chatter. Consistent with T2B10/B11, which test meanings, not usage. `[practice]`
- Deeper reading pointer for the author: the NTS Methods and Practices Guidelines devote a section to "Net and International Q-Signals" — the canonical working list for traffic handlers, far longer than the exam's two. (NTS MPG, arrl.org) `[practice]`

## 4. Digital modes & internet linking (Chapter 07)

*The chapter-07 tour: what each mode is FOR, what the beginner will actually see and hear.*

- "Digital" spans keyboard-to-keyboard modes, image modes, and data systems — the pool's examples: packet radio, IEEE 802.11 (yes, Wi-Fi counts), and FT8. `[pool-supported: T8D01]`
- FACT — FT8 = "Franke and Taylor, 8-FSK modulation": it "uses 15-second T/R sequences and provides 50% or better decoding probability down to −20 dB on an AWGN channel," with 75-bit messages, LDPC forward error correction, and ~50 Hz occupied bandwidth (WSJT-X v1.8.0 release notes + K1JT's characteristic list, wsjt.sourceforge.io). That is the pool's "low signal-to-noise operation." An FT8 QSO is minimal and scripted — call signs, signal reports, RR73/73, 60–90 seconds total — and the software's auto-sequencing can even answer the first reply to your CQ automatically. `[pool-supported: T8D02]` `[practice: auto-sequencing]`
- WSJT-X is the free suite for FT8 and friends; it also supports Earth-Moon-Earth, weak-signal propagation beacons (WSPR), and meteor scatter. `[pool-supported: T8D10]`
- FT8 setup reality: your PC clock must be accurate to about a second ("accuracy to within a second is plenty good enough in practice" — G4IFB FT8 operating guide; sync to internet time) or nothing decodes. Techs may run FT8 on 10 m (28.074 MHz is the standard watering hole in the WSJT-X default frequency table) as well as 6 m and 2 m. `[pool-supported: T1B05]` `[practice: WSJT-X User Guide]`
- FACT — APRS carries **GPS position, short text messages, and weather data**; its signature use is real-time tactical digital comms with stations plotted on a map. On-air in North America it lives on **144.390 MHz**, 1200-baud AFSK AX.25 packet (Europe: 144.800 MHz, per the IARU Region 1 band plan). (pool T8D03, T8D05; frequencies: alauda.ro APRS tutorial, hamdeck.com IARU-R1 band-plan listing) `[pool-supported: T8D03, T8D05]`
- APRS mechanics in one breath: your tracker beacons a packet, "digipeaters" relay it, internet gateways ("iGates") push it to the APRS Internet System — which is why friends can watch you on aprs.fi. The channel is shared, so set beacon rates sensibly. `[pool-supported: T8D03]` `[practice: aprs.fi]`
- APRS color beyond position dots: club weather stations beacon temperature/rain/wind, and chase crews track high-altitude balloons by APRS — both easy "look what's on the map right now" demos for ch 07. `[practice]`
- Packet radio proper: transmissions include a header with the destination station's call sign, a checksum for error detection, and ARQ — the receiving station detects errors and sends a request for retransmission. VHF packet rides on FM. The modem box is called a TNC. `[pool-supported: T8D08, T8D11, T8A02]` `[practice: TNC]`
- PSK = phase shift keying (think PSK31 keyboard chats). CW is simply another name for Morse code transmission — and at ~150 Hz it's the narrowest common emission, which is why it still punches through when nothing else does. `[pool-supported: T8D06, T8D09, T8A05, T8A11]`
- Amateur mesh networks: an amateur-radio data network built from commercial Wi-Fi equipment with modified firmware — the AREDN project is the one emcomm groups deploy for broadband links on amateur allocations. `[pool-supported: T8D12]` `[practice: arednmesh.org]`
- IRLP = a technique to connect amateur radio systems such as repeaters via the internet; over-the-air access to a node is by DTMF digits punched on your radio. `[pool-supported: T8C08, T8C06]`
- EchoLink = the protocol that lets an amateur station transmit through a repeater without using a radio to initiate the transmission — you run an app on a computer or phone, and an internet-connected gateway station puts you on RF. `[pool-supported: T8C09, T8C11]`
- FACT — Before using EchoLink you must **register your call sign and provide proof of license** (pool T8C10; echolink.org/validation: "Each new user of EchoLink must provide proof of license before access is granted."). `[pool-supported: T8C10]`
- Both IRLP and EchoLink are VoIP — voice delivered over the internet using digital techniques — bolted onto RF. Link etiquette: announce you're on the link, leave extra pause time (internet + double-squelch delays), and send the disconnect code when done. `[pool-supported: T8C07]` `[practice]`
- Old-tech image note for color: NTSC = the analog fast-scan color TV standard; amateur fast-scan TV needs about 6 MHz of bandwidth — why ATV lives on 70 cm and up. `[pool-supported: T8D04, T8A10]`

## 5. Satellites (Chapter 07)

*An FM satellite is a cross-band repeater in the sky — with a 10-minute appointment and moving-target frequencies.*

- FACT — LEO = **Low Earth Orbit, period around 100 minutes** (pool T8B10). Practical consequence: an FM "bird" is workable only while it's above your horizon — the ARRL Field Day satellite guide's SO-50 memory table spans AOS → zenith → LOS in 3-minute steps, i.e. a roughly 10-minute pass, repeating a few times a day. (arrl.org Field Day "Above 30 MHz" PDF) `[pool-supported: T8B10]` `[practice]`
- FACT — SO-50, the classic first FM satellite: uplink **145.850 MHz** FM, downlink **436.795 MHz**; it sleeps until you arm a 10-minute timer with a 2-second carrier using a **74.4 Hz** tone, then operate with a **67.0 Hz** tone. (amsat.org SO-50 satellite information page, verbatim) `[practice]`
- The easy mental model: an FM satellite is a cross-band repeater in the sky. "U/V mode" = uplink on 70 cm, downlink on 2 m (pool T8B08); satellites commonly use FM, SSB, and CW/data, and linear transponders relay a slice of spectrum rather than one FM channel. `[pool-supported: T8B08, T8B04]`
- Perspective that lands with beginners: SO-50's downlink runs about **250 mW** — you're copying a quarter-watt transmitter hundreds of km overhead. That's why antenna and squelch discipline matter. (AMSAT "Getting Started with the FM Satellites," amsat.org Work_FM_Sats-20131010.pdf) `[practice]`
- FACT — Doppler shift = the observed change in signal frequency caused by relative motion between the satellite and the Earth station (pool T8B07). Magnitude color: start listening ~**10 kHz above** the published downlink and follow it down in 5 kHz steps, ending ~10 kHz below at LOS (AMSAT FM-satellite guide). Doppler scales with frequency, so on the 2 m uplink it's only a few kHz — which is why the ARRL table holds transmit fixed. `[pool-supported: T8B07]` `[practice]`
- Beginner Doppler hack: program a ladder of memory channels and step through them during the pass — ARRL's Field Day satellite guide publishes exactly such a table for SO-50 (AOS 436.805 → zenith 436.795 → LOS 436.785 receive, transmit fixed at 145.850 with 67 Hz tone). AOS/LOS = acquisition/loss of signal, the start and end of your pass. (arrl.org "Above 30 MHz" Field Day PDF) `[practice]`
- Tracking programs take Keplerian elements ("keps," updated regularly over the internet) and return ground-track maps, the time/azimuth/elevation of each pass's start–peak–end, and even the apparent frequency including Doppler. `[pool-supported: T8B06, T8B03]` `[practice: AMSAT FM-satellite guide]`
- Beacons are transmissions containing status information — telemetry on the health and status of the satellite — and anyone, licensed or not, may receive telemetry. Decoding a beacon is a zero-pressure first satellite project. `[pool-supported: T8B05, T8B01, T8B11]`
- Power discipline: excessive uplink ERP can block access by other users — on a one-channel FM bird, the loudest station captures it. On linear transponders, set uplink power so your downlink is about the same strength as the beacon; louder means you're hogging the transponder's power budget. `[pool-supported: T8B02, T8B12]`
- Expect spin fading — periodic deep fades from the rotation of the satellite and its antennas. It's normal; don't "fix" it with more power. `[pool-supported: T8B09]` `[practice]`
- Operating style: exchanges on FM birds are fast and minimal — call signs and grid locators (T8C05), contest-style brevity (T8C04) — because many stations share a 10-minute pass. AMSAT's three keys to FM-satellite success, verbatim: "Open up your squelch • Antenna, antenna, antenna • Tune for the Doppler effect," plus a full-duplex dual-band radio so you can "listen to yourself while transmitting (highly recommended)." And the iron rule: "Make sure you can hear the satellite BEFORE [transmitting]... If you can't, DO NOT TRANSMIT!" (AMSAT FM-satellite guide) `[practice]`
- Say it like this on a pass: your call sign and grid locator, phonetically (AMSAT's worked example: "WHISKEY-DELTA-NINE-ECHO-WHISKEY-KILO") — no CQ needed on an FM bird; on a quiet pass you can add your name and state. (AMSAT FM-satellite guide) `[practice]`
- FACT — The ISS: any US amateur with a **Technician class or higher** license may contact it on VHF (pool T1B02). Per ariss.org: voice downlink **145.800 MHz** worldwide; VHF packet **145.825 MHz** (up and down); a typical ground station is a 2 m FM transceiver at 25–100 W — "successful contacts have even been made with vertical and ground plane antennas." `[pool-supported: T1B02]` `[practice: ariss.org "Contact the ISS"]` — see Watch item 8 on SSTV and packet-status changes.

## 6. An HF taste for Technicians (Chapter 06–07 bridge)

*Techs do have HF: 10 m SSB/CW/data plus CW slivers on 80/40/15 m — a different operating culture from repeater life.*

- FACT — Tech HF privileges: phone (SSB) on **10 m only, 28.300–28.500 MHz** (pool T1B01, T1B06); CW/RTTY/data 28.000–28.300; CW-only segments on 80 m (**3.525–3.600**), 40 m (**7.025–7.125**), 15 m (**21.025–21.200**). **200 W PEP** max in the HF segments (pool T1B11; ARRL Tech Band Chart PDF: Technicians "are limited to 200 Watts on the HF bands"). `[pool-supported: T1B01, T1B06, T1B11]`
- On 10 m SSB — and on VHF/UHF SSB generally — the convention is UPPER sideband. `[pool-supported: T8A06]`
- Style differences a repeater-raised Tech will notice on HF: (1) you call CQ — repeat "CQ" a few times, then "this is," your call sign, pause and listen, repeat as needed (T2A06); (2) answers come as your-call-then-their-call (T2A05); (3) signal reports are RST numbers — readability 1–5, strength 1–9 on phone, tone 1–9 added on CW (ARRL RST system, arrl.org/quick-reference-operating-aids) — instead of "full quieting"; (4) phonetics are routine. `[pool-supported: T2A06, T2A05, T2C03]` `[practice: RST page]`
- Courtesy step repeaters don't teach: before calling CQ on HF, listen, then ask if the frequency is in use — occupied frequencies are negotiated, never seized (same principle the pool tests for VHF in T2B08; see ARRL/IARU "Ethics and Operating Procedures for the Radio Amateur," arrl.org/files/file/DXCC/Eth-operating-EN-ARRL-CORR-JAN-2011.pdf). `[practice]`
- SSB vs FM in one line: SSB squeezes voice into ~3 kHz versus 10–15 kHz for FM, which is why SSB is the long-distance weak-signal voice mode on HF and VHF/UHF; FM's capture effect means only one signal — the strongest — is received at a time. `[pool-supported: T8A08, T8A09, T8A03, T8A12, T8A01, T8A07]`
- When 10 m "opens," a Tech can work surprising distances with a modest station; the ARRL 10 Meter Contest (December, CW+phone) and the ARRL International Digital Contest (June — Technicians explicitly welcome on 10 and 6 m) are beginner-sized targets. (local: ~/arrl-calendar/src/data/rules.ts, m10 and digital entries) `[practice]`
- The Rookie Roundup was built for this book's reader: "Rookies" (licensed within the last three years) work anyone, "Old Timers" may only work Rookies; April's running is SSB on 80–10 m with a 100 W limit; exchange = call sign, first name, two-digit year first licensed, and state — a Tech can play on 10 m. (local: rules.ts rookie entry; arrl.org/rookie-roundup) `[practice]`
- Contesting in general: contact as many stations as possible in a specified period, and send only the minimum information needed for ID plus the contest exchange. VHF contests exchange 4-character Maidenhead grid locators — a letter-number designator for a geographic location. `[pool-supported: T8C03, T8C04, T8C05]` (local: rules.ts janVhf/junVhf/sepVhf entries)
- Sweepstakes color: the ARRL November Sweepstakes exchange is a serial number, a precedence code, your call sign, a "check" (last two digits of the year you were first licensed), and your ARRL/RAC section — the traffic-handler's "check" (T2C11) living on in contest jargon. (local: rules.ts ss entry) `[pool-supported: T8C04]` `[practice]`
- Field Day (fourth full weekend of June) is where a new Tech sees all of it at once — off-grid stations, every mode, and a satellite contact worth 100 bonus points; exchange is class + section, e.g. "3A Ohio." (local: rules.ts fieldDay entry; satellite bonus: AMSAT FM-satellite guide) `[practice]`
- CW color: Straight Key Night (every January 1) is a no-pressure CW party — send "SKN" in your report — and W1AW, the ARRL headquarters station, sends daily code practice from 5 to 35 WPM plus CW/digital/voice bulletins. (local: rules.ts skn and w1aw entries) `[practice]` — CW is pool-relevant via T8D09 and the bandwidth questions T8A05/A11.
- For the reader's household: ARRL Kids Day (January and June) puts kids on HF phone alongside a licensed adult — call "CQ Kids Day," exchange name, age, location, and favorite color. (local: rules.ts kidsDay entry) `[practice]`
- Fox-hunt flavor for ch 07 activities: radio direction finding with a directional antenna is how clubs run hidden-transmitter hunts ("fox hunts") and track down noise sources or jammers — a beginner-friendly Saturday with an HT and a tape-measure Yagi. `[pool-supported: T8C01, T8C02]` `[practice]`

## Watch items (pool framing vs. common practice; unverified claims)

1. **Band plans are voluntary but socially enforced.** Pool T2A10 is exact: a band plan is a "voluntary guideline." The ARRL Tech Band Chart itself adds: "It is good amateur practice to follow the band plan established by the Amateur Radio community." Real-world: coordination bodies assign repeater pairs inside those plans, and operating against them earns corrective on-air coaching. Teach band plans as *expected behavior*, never as FCC law. (pool T2A10; Tech Band Chart PDF)
2. **CTCSS/DCS "privacy" myth.** The pool tests function only (T2B02/B04). The Motorola trade name "Private Line" makes beginners think tones hide their traffic — they don't; everyone in carrier squelch hears it. Teach tones as access/selective calling. (repeater-builder.com)
3. **CQ on repeaters.** The pool teaches CQ procedure for non-repeater use (T2A06/A08) and "listening" for repeaters (T2A09). Common newbie error: calling CQ on a repeater. Chapters should say plainly: CQ is for simplex/HF, not repeaters — the pool's split already implies this.
4. **Phonetics: one alphabet, two spellings.** The 26 words are fixed, but print varies: ITU's official respellings are "Alfa" and "Juliett" (to guide non-English pronunciation), while ARRL NTS material prints "ALPHA" (still "JULIETT"), and some handouts write "Juliet." The pool tests only the technique (T2C03), never the list. Pin the words; note the spelling folklore in a sidebar, not as doctrine. (ema.arrl.org NTS page vs. Standard-ITU-phonetics.pdf)
5. **Q-signals: meaning vs. usage.** The pool tests meanings (T2B10 QRM, T2B11 QSY). On FM voice the custom is plain words; on CW/SSB the abbreviations are idiomatic — and they leak into speech as nouns/verbs ("let's QSY to 146.55"). Ham-usage drift is real (QRP = low power, not "reduce power") — harmless on the exam, worth one sidebar line. (ARRL Q-signals PDF; NTS MPG)
6. **Offsets are "common," not universal.** The pool says "a common repeater frequency offset" (T2A01/A03). Odd splits and opposite-direction offsets exist near band edges; radios with auto-offset handle typical cases. Don't write "the offset is 600 kHz."
7. **LEO period.** Pool T8B10 says "around 100 minutes." Beginner material prints various round numbers for LEO periods; altitude decides the actual value, and it differs per satellite. Keep the pool's number in anything exam-facing, use "roughly a 10-minute pass" (per the ARRL SO-50 table) for operating color, and don't print a specific satellite's period without checking current orbital data.
8. **ISS frequencies and modes change.** Per ariss.org (fetched 2026-07-23): voice downlink 145.800 worldwide, VHF packet 145.825 — but SSTV now downlinks on **437.550 MHz (Robot36)**, not the 145.800 many older guides print, and the packet digipeater has gone inactive (and returned) over the years. Any ISS how-to box must be re-checked against ariss.org / AMSAT news close to print.
9. **Emergency operation outside privileges (T2C09).** Pool-correct framing: only for immediate safety of life/property. Practice: essentially never happens in a Tech's career; don't let a chapter imply emcomm drills suspend Part 97 — T2C01 explicitly says FCC rules always apply.
10. **RACES vs ARES.** Keep the pool's clean split (T2C04/T2C06/T2C12): ARES = ARRL volunteers, anyone can join and drill; RACES = government-certified civil-defense service that operates when activated. Beginner guides routinely blur these; ours shouldn't.
11. **"Full quieting" vs RST.** Everyday FM talk uses "full quieting," "you're scratchy," "you're making the trip" — the pool never tests FM report vocabulary, so mark it as jargon color, distinct from the ARRL RST system used on SSB/CW. (arrl.org/quick-reference-operating-aids)
12. **Tone folklore.** "PL," "tone," and "tone squelch" are used loosely; radio manuals typically say "tone" for encode and "tone squelch" for decode, and open/closed/private repeater listings are the owner's policy choice, not a property of the tone. Chapters should show a concrete radio-menu example rather than abstract tone talk. (repeater-builder.com)
13. **"Color code ≈ CTCSS" is teaching shorthand.** The pool defines the DMR color code strictly as an access code programmed in to reach a specific repeater (T2B12); the CTCSS analogy (§1) orients beginners but is not the pool's language. State the pool definition first.
14. **UNVERIFIED items: none.** Every FACT line carries a pool ID or a URL fetched and content-checked 2026-07-23. Deliberately excluded for lack of a citable source: local-repeater anecdotes, quantitative "most repeaters" claims, and full CTCSS tone-frequency tables beyond the SO-50 tones cited above.
