## Appendix B: Glossary & Formulas

This appendix is for looking things up, not for reading straight through. The glossary defines every term the book uses, in the same plain language as the chapters — a chapter may say more about a term, but never something different. The formulas section gathers the book's complete formula set, and every formula carries a worked example built from the exam's own numbers.

### Formulas

This is the book's complete formula set — nothing more is ever asked of you. Each relation comes with a plain statement and one worked example using the question pool's own numbers.

**Ohm's law: $V = I \times R$.** Voltage equals current times resistance — the push equals the flow times the opposition. Rearranged, the same law reads I = V / R and R = V / I, so any one of the three quantities falls out of the other two. The pool prints this law with E for voltage and a plain "x" for multiplication — "E = I x R," "I = E / R," "R = E / I" (T5D01–T5D03). E and V both mean volts.

> **Worked example:** A resistor has 12 volts across it and 1.5 amperes flowing through it. You want R, so R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 1.5 = 8 Ω (pool T5D05).

**The power law: $P = V \times I$.** Power equals voltage times current. Rearranged: I = P / V and V = P / I. The pool prints it as "P = I x E" (T5C08) — the same sentence in pool notation.

> **Worked example:** A 13.8-volt supply delivering 10 amperes provides P = 13.8 × 10 = 138 watts (pool T5C09).

**The wavelength shortcut: λ(m) = 300 / f(MHz).** Wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz — the pool's own formula (T3B06). It is a working approximation of wavelength = speed of light ÷ frequency with c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s, never an exact identity — but this shortcut is the only form the exam asks for.

> **Worked example:** 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.05 meters — which is why the band near 146 MHz is called the 2-meter band — and 300 ÷ 50 = 6 meters for the 6-meter band.

**The prefix ladder.** Metric units climb by rungs: pico (10⁻¹²) → nano (10⁻⁹) → micro (10⁻⁶) → milli (10⁻³) → base unit → kilo (10³) → mega (10⁶) → giga (10⁹), each rung a factor of 1000 from its neighbor. Moving toward a smaller unit, multiply; moving toward a larger unit, divide.

> **Worked example:** 3.525 MHz is one rung toward the smaller unit: 3.525 × 1000 = 3525 kHz (pool T5B07). Likewise 1.5 amperes = 1.5 × 1000 = 1500 milliamperes (pool T5B01).

**Decibels by anchors.** No logarithms needed — three anchors cover the exam: double power is approximately 3 dB; four times power is approximately 6 dB, so one quarter power is approximately −6 dB; ten times power is exactly 10 dB.

> **Worked example:** A drop from 12 watts to 3 watts is quarter power, approximately −6 dB (pool T5B10). A rise from 20 watts to 200 watts is ten times power, exactly 10 dB (pool T5B11).

> **The math, if you want it:** Decibels are defined by $\mathrm{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(P_2 / P_1)$ — ten times the base-10 logarithm of the power ratio. Optional only; the anchors above answer every exam question.

**Battery runtime.** To estimate how long a battery can power your equipment, divide the battery's ampere-hour rating by the average current draw: runtime in hours = ampere-hours ÷ amperes.

> **Worked example:** A 9 ampere-hour battery running equipment that draws 2 amperes on average lasts 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 hours — pool T4A09's formula.

**Duty cycle.** Duty cycle is the share of time a transmitter is actually transmitting: transmit time ÷ total time, usually written as a percentage. For RF safety, allowable power density scales inversely with duty cycle.

> **Worked example:** Transmitting 30 seconds out of every 5 minutes is 30 ÷ 300 = 10 percent duty cycle. If duty cycle changes from 100 percent to 50 percent, the allowable power density increases by a factor of 2 (pool T0C03).

**Notation and units — how the pool and this book write things.**

**E and x versus V and ×.** The pool prints E for voltage and a plain "x" for multiplication, so its formulas read "P = I x E" and "E = I x R." This book's prose uses V and ×. E and V both mean volts.

**Unit case is load-bearing.** Write kHz with a lowercase k, MHz and GHz with a capital M or G, and always a capital H; mA, µV, and pF follow the same prefix case rules. Never "KHZ," "mhz," or "Mhz."

**The speed of light.** The working value is c = 3×10⁸ m/s = 300,000 km/s. The exact value, 299,792,458 m/s, is never needed.

**Frequency and period.** The two are reciprocals: f = 1 / T. A 1 MHz signal completes one million cycles per second, so each cycle lasts one microsecond.

**Displayed math.** Where a formula is displayed inline, this appendix uses the same math style as the chapters.
